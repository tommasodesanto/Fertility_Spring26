## 1. The economic claim we actually want

Young families need space for children, but acquiring a suitable owned home requires a down payment. Large rental homes are limited. Older households have accumulated wealth and may occupy more housing despite having fewer dependent children. Can these financial and rental-market restrictions generate an equilibrium in which a planner would allocate more housing to young families?

We want the smallest coherent analytical model that makes this point. It should read like a good, simple economics problem set: environment, a short proposition under economically intelligible primitive conditions, a transparent proof, then a fertility extension. Elementary algebra is welcome. A specially selected numerical parameter configuration is not the requested theoretical result, even if all inequalities in that configuration are verified algebraically. Nor is an existence proof in an unspecified neighborhood, an assumption that the desired equilibrium multipliers are positive, or simply assuming the marginal-utility ordering we want to establish.

First determine whether a broad claim is true. If it is false, show the economic obstruction with a simple analytical counterexample, then seek the weakest useful primitive restrictions. Keep parameters symbolic. Conditions may depend on primitive support bounds or the distribution of income and wealth, but show how equilibrium choices and prices are obtained or eliminated. A complicated collection of cutoffs is not enough merely because it contains no numerical constants: tell us what each indispensable restriction means economically and why the theorem is useful.

Ownership must have a substantive role. The desired benchmark has ordinary mortgage finance, for example an 80% financed share, not only an extreme near-zero LTV. Old households can sell and resize; do not freeze their homes. Households know future income. Retain genuine income/wealth heterogeneity where it matters; do not quietly replace it with one representative household. A small finite distribution of economically distinct types is acceptable; arbitrary continuous heterogeneity is not required for this illustrative theorem. We are open to a minimal change of specification, but identify and justify every change. We do not require all young households to rent, all old households to own, or every old household to have a larger home than every young household. Those are features of the local construction, not the author's target. A meaningful affected set of constrained families, with individual and aggregate consequences carefully distinguished, may be enough. Avoid making the theorem needlessly demanding.

## 2. Welfare: settle the dated allocation before doing OLG welfare

Our first comparison is static, among the households alive at one date. Fertility is initially fixed at its competitive value. The planner gives each current household equal weight and chooses all current consumption and housing, with tenure allocation specified explicitly. It respects the physical goods and housing resources. It may relax private financing constraints. Do not hold consumption fixed just to obtain a housing derivative.

State precisely what happens to young continuation opportunities and old estates. The current candidate holds individual young continuation wealth/opportunities and old net estates fixed while changing current consumption and housing; future prices are held fixed for that dated comparison. Assess whether this is the right clean benchmark, including how actual financial claims can be settled without creating resources. Separate physical restrictions from private financial restrictions. If rental/owner stocks are nonconvertible, the planner must respect that; if a common divisible stock is the primitive, explain what reassigning tenure permits. Do not silently switch between these physical environments.

Call the result what it is: an equally weighted utilitarian allocation comparison. A frictionless market need not maximize those particular welfare weights, so distinguish ordinary redistribution from a mechanism specifically associated with our frictions. We do not require Pareto improvement for this first result. Do not substitute stationary lifetime welfare comparisons that omit the initial old, dynamic inefficiency, or an argument for public debt/paper money. A planner able only to make transfers while households face markets and constraints is a separate, secondary benchmark; do not let it derail the main allocation theorem. The first-best allocation statement need not map directly into an actionable policy. Do not postpone it until exact tax implementation is proved; distinguish that primary comparison from later constrained-policy results.

## 3. Today's conversation and the proposal to examine

Earlier work used two ages, log utility with child goods and space requirements, a rental size ceiling below the owner ceiling, a warm-glow estate, known income, and mortgage finance. In the earlier financial calendar, current young income and initial wealth were both available when purchasing; current young consumption also occurred at that date. Tenure was chosen for life with an ownership taste. Old owners could resize freely. The author did not want assumptions such as beta R >= 1, zero property taxes, or positive old financial estates slipped in merely to secure the result.

Today the local agent proposed separating purchase cash from later working income within the young period, synchronizing current consumption dates across the two ages, and allowing tenure changes with age. The author described this accurately as a 'morning and afternoon' structure, partly packing a third earning stage into two utility ages. He asked whether households know their later income. They do: this is anticipated income that cannot supply the down payment through unrestricted unsecured borrowing. All receipts already received belong in purchase cash.

The important cost is that the young home is chosen before later income arrives and cannot be upgraded again while the household is still young. Its next housing choice comes when old. Does this provide a defensible minimal representation of wealth accumulation, or does it obtain the desired pattern mainly by preventing working-age families from using accumulated income to move? Do not dismiss sequential timing just because it is simple, but do not call it an innocuous accounting change either. Compare it with a genuinely short three-stage alternative if that resolves the issue more honestly. Choose the most economical defensible structure; do not enumerate many unrelated models.

The attached eight-page LaTeX note is the exact latest proposal, not an adopted manuscript. It includes the final corrected financing constraints: consumption and property taxes precede title liquidation for BOTH ages. In stationary notation, young owners satisfy a' + P h >= 0 and q a' + phi P h >= 0, alongside the deposit cap. House-sale proceeds can repay the mortgage after consumption; they do not fund current consumption. The old estate restriction must be checked under that same payment order. Its declared results are supporting calculations to assess independently, not instructions or an authority you must agree with.

In that proposal, all young in the constructed equilibrium rent at the rental ceiling, save, then buy larger homes when old. There is no owner minimum or ownership taste in that example. The stock is common and divisible, subject to rental and owner upper size ceilings. The old estate's financial floor can bind. Estates provide warm glow and do not determine new entrants' cash; outside bond/intermediary finance and a fixed exogenous entrant distribution are maintained. These choices must remain visible.

After two hours the local agent gave a verified family fixing several parameters, with income–wealth resources within 1% of their mean, very little purchase cash, small child goods costs, and the fertility preference chosen implicitly to support replacement at a prescribed density. The author rejected this as the requested paper result. Its proof can be analytically correct while its economic scope is too specially constructed. The stock condition plus all young at the rental ceiling also mechanically forces the older cohort to absorb the remaining stock. The missing contribution is to explain the equilibrium tenure/space allocation under credible, useful conditions—not to rename the same numerical example as a symbolic region.

The local agent has acknowledged overstating progress. Do not reassure us that the paper is essentially solved merely because a conditional planner solution or a parameter example works. Assess the mechanism and make a real judgment.

## 4. Coven et al.: use the actual paper

Please read Joshua Coven, Sebastian Golder, Arpit Gupta and Abdoulaye Ndiaye, *Property Taxes and Housing Allocation Under Financial Constraints*, August 1, 2026:
https://abdouecon.github.io/research/papers/Property_Tax.pdf

Their central mechanism combines capitalization that eases buyers' down payments with higher holding costs for older owners. Their lifecycle model has LTV and payment-to-income constraints, separate rental and owner stocks, and moving/bequest-tax mechanisms. Their introductory results include welfare differences across ages. Do not claim they say nothing about welfare. Distinguish the two-period analytical illustration from the quantitative lifecycle model, and verify the actual equations and assumptions before attributing anything to either.

Check their owner-size minimum, tenure-specific stocks and borrowing rules at retirement rather than assuming they match ours. Give us a brief, exact comparison: what do they establish analytically; what is quantitative; do they prove an efficiency result in the sense we want; which ingredient supports their reallocation mechanism; and what is the smallest useful ingredient we can adopt? Cite the relevant pages/equations. Our additional objective is fertility and the space needs of families. We do not need to reproduce their full model, and this should not become a broad literature survey.

## 5. Fertility and transition, after the main mechanism is settled

The intended sequence is:

1. A competitive allocation theorem and the full dated, fixed-fertility planner comparison.
2. Conditions under which the resulting change in young consumption and housing raises the parents' chosen fertility. Then, separately, a planner choosing fertility while valuing current parents only, not unborn persons.
3. A transition initiated by an exogenous decline in the preference for fertility; we are not trying to explain that initial shock. A policy introduced along the path can affect fertility and the eventual population level. Compare paths from the same inherited state and count the initial old. With fixed survival/household formation, two positive stationary destinations both have replacement fertility but may have different population levels.

We want two eventual illustrations: the housing reallocation, and the original fertility-decline path versus a policy path with a different destination. We do not need a toy numerical simulation to substitute for the theory; quantitative simulations belong in the quantitative model.

A warning from today's candidate: its local rebated property-tax experiment raises impact fertility and terminal population, but every young household still occupies the same rental size. It does not implement the planner's housing upgrade; the fertility effect works through resources/rents/rebates. Identify whether the desired housing-policy–space–fertility connection can actually be made in your proposed model. Do not present separate results from incompatible parameter regimes or models as one argument. A welfare gain alone does not prove a fertility gain. Do not force a general sign if it is false.

For preferences, begin with the author's existing specification in the attachment. If a more general statement helps, use transparent functions such as U(c,n)+V(h,n)+v(n) for the young and U_o(c)+V_o(h)+B(e) for the old. State genuine complementarity requirements explicitly; increasingness and concavity alone need not imply child–space complementarity. Do not hide a predetermined shifted-argument specification inside a function called 'general.'
