\section{Testing the model}
\label{sec:quant-fit}

In this section I ask how well the model accounts for features of the data
that the calibration does not target. The policy experiments of
Section~\ref{sec:policy} change the cost of housing for families, so the
model must reproduce when women have their first child, how households
adjust housing and tenure around a birth, and how ownership and housing
evolve over the life cycle. I first compare the 2007 economy with the data:
Table~\ref{tab:quant-untargeted} reports untargeted moments, and
Figure~\ref{fig:quant-lifecycle-2007} compares life-cycle profiles. I then
compare the 2023 economy, the starting point of every policy experiment,
with the 2023 data. The transition of Section~\ref{sec:quant-transition}
targets only period fertility, so every other 2023 moment is out of sample.

\subsection{Fertility}
The model matches the targets closely except children ever born by age 25,
0.56 against 0.81 (Table~\ref{tab:quant-moments}). The share
of women who are mothers by 25 is matched, 45.5\% against 45.7\%, so the miss
is in the number of children of young mothers: 1.2 at 25 in the model,
against 1.8 in the data. A household can have at most one birth in a
four-year period, so in the model no woman has three children by 25 and few
have two; no parameter can change this. Later first births are close to the
data: 24.2\% of first births occur at 30 or later, against 24.9\%. The right
panel of Figure~\ref{fig:quant-lifecycle-2007} shows the whole distribution.
The model places fewer first births between 26 and 33, 28.5\% against
33.6\%, and more at 18 to 21 and after 38.

\subsection{Housing around the first birth}
The model reproduces the size of the room response after the first birth,
which it targets, but not its timing. In the data, parents occupy the same
number of rooms in the years before the reference period, add 0.62 rooms in
the window of the birth and 1.44 three to four years after it, and keep
adding rooms, to 1.8 more eleven or more years after the birth. In the
model, households add 0.78 rooms in the window of the birth and 1.48 three to
four years after it. But they already add rooms in the years before the
birth: eight or more years before it they occupy 0.65 fewer rooms than in
the reference period, and four to five years before it 0.20 fewer. Part of
the model's response therefore reflects the growth of housing with age and
income among future parents, rather than the birth itself. After the birth,
model households stop adding rooms, and eleven or more years after it the
response falls to 0.65 rooms.

Tenure responds less than in the data. Ownership rises around the first
birth by 14 percentage points in the model, against 26 in the data,
consistent with the higher ownership of young households in the model
(below). Larger families also add more space than in the data: households
with three or more children occupy 0.57 more rooms than those with one or
two, against 0.39.

\subsection{Life-cycle profiles, 2007}
The left and middle panels of Figure~\ref{fig:quant-lifecycle-2007} compare
ownership and rooms by age of the head with the 2005--2006 ACS. The model
reproduces the rise of ownership with age, and between 34 and 49 the two
profiles are within four percentage points. Young households own too often:
34\% at 18--21 and 44\% at 22--25, against 12\% and 24\% in the data. At old
ages, ownership keeps rising in the model, to nearly all households after
80, while in the data it falls from 82\% at 66--69 to 75\% at 82--85. The
model has no health or long-term-care risk that would lead older owners to
sell. The model also reproduces the hump in housing size, but its profile is
steeper: rooms rise from 4.6 at 18--21 to a peak of 6.5 at 42--45, against
4.2 and 6.0 in the data, and then fall faster. The ACS records at most nine
rooms, and its profile lies below the AHS mean that the model targets, so
the comparison is informative about shape rather than level.

\subsection{Wealth}
The model accounts for the dispersion of wealth among the old: the ratio of
the 90th percentile to the median of wealth relative to income at ages 76 to
84 is 3.27, against 3.52 in the PSID.

\subsection{The 2023 economy}
\label{sec:quant-2023}

The transition matches only period fertility, so the 2023 economy is an
out-of-sample test of what the decline in $\psi_t$ alone does to the rest of
the economy. Table~\ref{tab:quant-2023} compares the change from 2007 to 2023
in the data and in the model. The model reproduces the shift of first births
to later ages in direction but not in size: the share of women who are
mothers by 25 falls by 13 percentage points in the model and by 18 in the
data, the mean age at first birth rises by 0.9 years against 2.1, and the
share of first births at 30 or later rises by 5 points against 13. Three
changes have the wrong sign. Childlessness among women aged 40 to 44 rises by
8.6 points in the model, while it fell by one point in the data. Ownership
among heads aged 30 to 55 rises by 4.1 points, while it fell by 8.9. The
house price falls by 3.6\%, while the real price rose by 15.6\%. Wealth
relative to earnings, which rose from 4.46 to 7.05 in the PSID, barely moves
in the model. Mean rooms are close to the data, 5.51 against 5.58. The extra
space of families with three or more children falls by 0.03 rooms in both,
but stays too large in the model, 0.54 rooms against 0.36.

The fertility moments reveal the limitations of explaining the decline with
changes in the preference for children alone. In the data, the decline
in period fertility since 2007 came with almost unchanged childlessness among
women aged 40 to 44 (19.8\% in 2004--2006, 18.8\% in 2024) and
with later first births: the mean age at first birth rose from 26.0 to 28.1
and the share of first births at 30 or later from 25 to 38\%. In the
model, a lower $\psi_t$ works on every cohort alive, so 29\% of women
aged 40 to 44 in 2023 are childless and their completed fertility is 1.69,
against 1.92 in the data. The data
show a prominent role for later first births among younger cohorts; the
model instead reduces fertility across the cohorts exposed to the common
preference change. This discrepancy leaves the distribution of preference changes across
cohorts unresolved.

With only preference shocks,
falling fertility lowers the demand for family space, so the model has to
deliver a falling price: 3.6\% below 2007 in 2023. In the data, the real
house price rose by 15.6\% between 2007 and 2023, and real rents by 15
to 19\%. Part of the decline in fertility since 2007 therefore came with
a rising cost of space that the estimation assigns to preferences. The
transition says what housing would have done in response to the fertility
decline, not that housing caused it.

Figure~\ref{fig:quant-lifecycle-2023} compares life-cycle profiles in 2023.
The model reproduces the rise of ownership with age and the hump in housing
size, but its households buy too early: 43\% of heads aged 18
to 21 and 52\% of those aged 22 to 25 own their home in the
model, against 11\% and 15\% in the ACS. This miss matters
for the credit experiments below, because it places too many young households
near the margin of buying. First births are also too early:
29\% of first births in the model are to women aged 18 to 21,
against 17\% in the 2021--2023 natality data, and 14\% are at
30 to 33, against 22\%.
