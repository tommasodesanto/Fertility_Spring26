\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper builds a life-cycle model of fertility with realistic housing, in
which households choose when to have children, how much space to occupy, and
whether to rent or own it. Children need space, the largest homes must be
bought with a down payment, and earnings are uninsurable. I calibrate the
model to the United States in 2007 and treat the fall in fertility since then
as the start of a transition to a smaller population, along which a durable
housing stock makes space cheaper for the smaller cohorts that follow.

Three results emerge. First, earnings risk matters for fertility: a
mean-preserving reduction in the standard deviation of earnings shocks from
0.48 to 0.35 raises completed fertility by 16 percent. Second, housing costs
matter: a 10 percent expansion of the housing supply raises the completed
fertility of today's young by about 5 percent and of cohorts twenty years
later by about 4 percent. Third, easier mortgage credit mainly changes the
timing of births. It raises births on impact and the ownership of the young,
who buy earlier, smaller and more leveraged homes, but leaves completed
fertility unchanged. Credit tied to children is the exception: restricted to
parents, it raises completed fertility by about 6 percent. The common thread
is that a child commits a household to paying for family space every period.
Policies raise fertility when they lower that cost or make it easier to bear,
and credit that only lowers the one-time cost of buying does neither.

The analysis has limitations. The numbers in this draft are at the current
calibration and will change when the decline in the preference for children
from 2007 to 2023 is re-estimated as a gradual process, which changes the 2023
economy from which every policy starts. The preference shocks that account for
the decline fall on every cohort alive, so the model overstates childlessness
and misses the postponement of first births, and with only preference shocks
it predicts a falling house price where the data show a rising one. The
reduction in earnings risk also compresses the distribution of earnings, so
part of its effect may reflect lower inequality rather than lower uncertainty.
Young households own too often in the model, which places too many of them at
the margin where credit acts, and credit for parents is unpriced. Finally,
landlords and builders are outside the household sector, so the income from
renting and building housing does not return to households. A housing shock
that matches the rise in prices and rents since 2007, a fertility shock that
falls on young cohorts, and a decomposition of the risk effect into its
housing and non-housing parts are the natural next steps.
