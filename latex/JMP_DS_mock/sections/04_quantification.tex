\section{Quantification}
\label{sec:quantification}
\label{sec:quant_calibration}

I calibrate a stationary reference economy representing 2007, before the
subsequent decline in fertility. I use observations from the surrounding years
to discipline fertility, housing, and household wealth. Housing moments use
the national household sample in the 2005--2006 ACS; fertility moments come from the
2004 and 2006 CPS and 2003--2006 natality records, while wealth moments use the
2003 and 2005 PSID. This initial steady state provides the starting point for
the historical transition, in which changes in fertility preferences account
for the subsequent path of births while the other structural parameters remain
fixed.\footnote{This working section describes the 2007 calibration under
review. The persistent-earnings process and parenthood housing floor provide
the comparison specification; earnings, entry-wealth mapping, and the
alternative utility with child-dependent housing shares remain provisional.
No new set of parameter estimates or model fit is adopted here.}

I organize the model parameters into two groups. The first consists of
parameters assigned from external evidence, measured directly in the data, or
fixed as maintained restrictions. The second consists of parameters estimated
internally by matching model moments to their empirical counterparts. I describe
the two groups in turn, explain which moments are particularly informative
about the internally estimated parameters, and then assess the model's fit.

\subsection{Parameters Set Outside the Estimation}

I first describe the parameters assigned outside the estimation.
Table~\ref{tab:quant_assigned} summarizes their values and restrictions.

\paragraph{Demographics and preferences.}
A model period is four years. Households enter adult life at age 18, retire at
age 66, and live at most through age 85. Survival is certain before retirement
and thereafter follows age-specific survival probabilities. Dependent children
remain in the household for an expected 18 years, implying a maturity
probability of $2/9$ per period. I set risk aversion to $\sigma=2$ and retain
the power equivalence scale with child weight $\omega=0.7$ and exponent
$\nu=0.7$. The consumption weight is $\alpha_0=0.733$, measured using
childless renters in the 2019--2023 CEX. These preference restrictions are
held fixed in the estimation; they need not be measured in the base year.

\paragraph{Housing finance and transaction costs.}
I set the annual real return on liquid wealth to
$i_{\mathrm{ann}}=0.02$, the proportional selling cost to $\psi^s=0.06$,
and the labor-income tax rate to $\tau_{\mathrm{pay}}=0.179$, retaining
the external restrictions used in the housing-finance calibration
\citep{greaneyDynamicUrbanEconomics2025}. Annual housing depreciation is
$\delta_{\mathrm{ann}}=0.011$ and the annual property-tax rate is
$\tau^p_{\mathrm{ann}}=0.01$. Mortgage debt cannot exceed a share
$\phi=0.80$ of the value of the house, so a purchase requires at least
20 percent equity. Purchase eligibility accounts for current earnings as well
as existing resources. The model imposes no separate payment-to-income limit.
Payroll-tax revenue finances retirement benefits through the stationary pension
budget; the pension is therefore determined within each candidate equilibrium.

\paragraph{Income process.}
I impose a deterministic lifecycle profile of household earnings, $e_a$,
normalized to average one over working ages. Within age, log earnings follow
a persistent AR(1) process estimated from the PSID at the model's four-year
frequency. The working process has persistence $\rho_z=0.7346$ and innovation
standard deviation $\sigma_z=0.4838$ in log earnings. It has no fixed permanent
household types and is approximated with 15 income states. Entrants draw from
the stationary earnings-risk distribution.\footnote{The comparison with a
persistent process plus an independent transitory component is retained as an
alternative. Its four-year persistence is 0.7761, persistent innovation
standard deviation is 0.4374, and transitory standard deviation is 0.1650.
Neither adding transitory risk nor assuming stationary income risk at age 18
is an adopted resolution of the earnings specification.}

\paragraph{Housing products and supply.}
Housing is indivisible and available in a limited assortment of types and
sizes \citep{davisHousingFinanceMacroeconomy2015}. I represent this lumpiness
with five owner-occupied house sizes,
$\{H_k\}_{k=1}^5=\{2,4,6,8,10\}$ rooms. Renters choose housing continuously
up to $\bar h^R=6$ rooms. These menus summarize the tenure-specific distribution
of rooms. I retain a housing-supply elasticity of $\eta=0.63$; the supply
intercept $H_0$ is estimated jointly with household parameters.

\paragraph{Entry wealth and bequests.}
Households enter at age 18 with heterogeneous nonhousing wealth and debt,
constructed from the PSID wealth-to-family-income distribution of childless
renters aged 18--24. The joint entry distribution retains the reference
mapping between wealth and earnings.\footnote{The empirical family-income
denominator must be reconciled with the model's gross-earnings units. The
working experiments retain the inherited heterogeneous entry law; zero
entrant wealth is not imposed in this calibration.}
Bequest utility does not vary directly with the number of children. Children
affect estate accumulation through their effects on housing needs, tenure,
and saving.

\subsection{Internally Estimated Parameters}

I estimate nine parameters jointly using twelve moments describing
childlessness, fertility timing and family size, housing consumption, tenure,
and wealth accumulation. Separately, I choose the fertility utility scale
$\psi_0$ so that mean children ever born among households aged 46 and above
is 2.1 in the stationary economy. This is a normalization of the initial
steady state, not a period total fertility rate. Although the parameters are
chosen jointly, I organize the discussion into economic blocks and describe
which moments are most informative about each block.

\paragraph{Saving.}
The annual discount factor $\beta_{\mathrm{annual}}$ governs households'
willingness to carry resources across ages. Aggregate wealth relative to annual
gross earnings is particularly informative about it. The empirical target is
a ratio of aggregate wealth to aggregate earnings, rather than the average
of household wealth-to-earnings ratios.

\paragraph{Housing demand, tenure, and supply.}
I use housing-size differences by family composition to discipline housing
demand. The room response around the first birth is particularly informative
about the parenthood housing requirement $h_P$. The difference between
larger and smaller families provides additional information about housing
demand under the maintained equivalence scale. The floor is the same for
all households with dependent children, rather than increasing with each
additional child. Ownership rates and the ownership gap associated with
recent parenthood are particularly informative about the owner housing-service
multiplier $\chi$, while mean occupied rooms helps discipline the
housing-supply intercept $H_0$.\footnote{The alternative utility removes
$h_P$ and estimates two child-dependent housing-share parameters, a parenthood shift and a slope in the
number of dependent children. That specification
has ten jointly searched parameters and the same separate fertility
normalization.}

\paragraph{Fertility and family size.}
Childlessness is particularly informative about the first-birth utility cost
$\xi$. Mean age at first birth and the share of first births at age 30 or
older help discipline first-birth taste dispersion $\kappa_1$. The fraction
of mothers with exactly one child helps discipline later-birth taste dispersion
$\kappa_C$. These parameters are estimated jointly because the same family
outcomes respond to the costs, timing, and utility value of children.

\paragraph{Bequests.}
The bequest parameters $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ govern the strength and wealth
dependence of the bequest motive. Annual bequests relative to aggregate wealth
are particularly informative about $\theta_0$, while the p90-to-median ratio
of wealth relative to family income among households aged 76--84 provides
information about the wealth shift $\theta_1$. These moments also respond to
saving and tenure choices, so the two bequest parameters are disciplined
jointly with those blocks.

\subsection{Target Moments and Fit}

The target system combines children-ever-born distributions from the CPS,
first-birth timing from natality records, tenure and housing-size moments
from the ACS, housing responses around childbirth from the PSID event study,
and wealth moments from the PSID. Table~\ref{tab:quant_fit} reports the
working targets for the 2007 reference economy.\footnote{The empirical
first-birth room response and recent-parent ownership gap require a common
observation rule in the data and model before their fit can be interpreted.
The other population, timing, and weighting choices are being reviewed row
by row. Model moments and parameter estimates will be inserted for the chosen
specification; historical July fits and unadopted experimental fits are not
substituted for that comparison.}
