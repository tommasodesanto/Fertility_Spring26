\section{Quantification}
\label{sec:quant}

In this section, I describe the quantification of the model. Because
fertility rates vary a lot over time and the age distribution of the
population reflects many years of past fertility decisions, some assumptions
are needed to make the quantification tractable. To this end, I approximate
the United States economy of 2007 with a steady state in which births are at
replacement. I parameterize the model so that this steady state matches
fertility, housing and wealth moments of 2007. Holding these parameters
fixed, I then let the preference for children $\psi_t$ fall after 2007 so
that the model matches the decline in fertility until 2023, and I solve for
the transition of the economy as prices, family composition and household
entry adjust (Section~\ref{sec:quant-transition}). I treat this fall as an
exogenous shock to preferences and take it as given. The 2023 state of this
transition is the starting point of the policy experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:policy}.

\subsection{Quantification strategy}
\label{sec:quant-approach}

Two measures of fertility guide the quantification
(Figure~\ref{fig:quant-us-fertility}). Period fertility, or the total
fertility rate, is the number of children a woman would have if she lived
through her fertile years at the birth rates of a given year. Completed
fertility is the number of children a woman has had by the end of her fertile
years. I measure it at ages 40 to 44. Whether a population replaces itself
depends on completed fertility, and completed fertility is the guiding moment
of the quantification. In a steady state the two measures coincide. Out of
a steady state, completed fertility lags period fertility, because it adds up
the births of a cohort over two decades. In the United States, period
fertility peaked at about 2.1 in 2007 and fell to 1.62 by 2023, while
completed fertility was still 1.92 in 2024. If period fertility stayed at its
2023 level, completed fertility would fall to 1.62 within about two decades,
as the cohorts that had their children before 2007 age out. This is an
illustration, not a forecast. The same lag explains why the number of
households does not fall at once: fewer births lower the number of new
households only when the smaller cohorts reach adulthood, about two decades
later.

I quantify the model in three steps, which follow the logic of
Section~\ref{sec:m-analysis} and Figure~\ref{fig:m-illustration}. First, I
treat the 2007 economy as a stationary equilibrium, point $A$ in the figure,
with fertility at replacement. I choose the parameters so that this
equilibrium matches fertility, housing and wealth moments of 2007
(Sections~\ref{sec:quant-data} and~\ref{sec:quant-identification}). Second,
I hold these parameters fixed and let the preference for children $\psi_t$
fall after 2007. I choose the size of the fall so that period fertility in the
model matches the data in 2020--2023. I then solve for the transition as
prices, family composition and household entry adjust
(Section~\ref{sec:quant-transition}). On impact, fertility falls at about the
inherited price, from $A$ toward $A'$. As smaller cohorts enter, the price
falls and the economy moves toward $B$. Third, I start every policy
experiment of Section~\ref{sec:policy} from the 2023 state of this
transition. I compare each policy with the path the economy follows from
2023 without it.

I divide the parameters of the 2007 economy into two groups. The first
group is assigned from external evidence or measured directly in the data.
The second is chosen so that the model matches a set of moments in the data.
Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:quant-parameters} reports the moments, and Panel~B
the parameters.

\subsection{Assigned parameters}
\label{sec:quant-data}
\label{sec:quant-period}

\paragraph{Demographics and timing.}
A period in the model lasts four years. Households enter at 18, retire at 66
and live at most through 85. Survival is certain before retirement and,
after it, follows the 2023 period life table of the Social Security
Administration. Births are possible through age 45. The success of an
attempt falls with age following the exponential schedule of
\citet{sommerFertilityChoiceLife2016}, from 0.98 at 18 to 0.90 at 30, 0.80
at 35 and 0.62 at 40. A child leaves home with probability $\lambda=2/9$ per
period, so children stay at home for 18 years on average.%
% pi_a = 1 - 0.02 exp(0.134 (a - 18)); 0.50 at 42. Leridon (2004) four-year
% conception 0.91 / 0.84 / 0.64 at 30 / 35 / 40 (docs/model/e5_target_review_20260724.md).
% Survival: 2023 SSA period life table, four-year ratios
% (docs/model/quantification_note_reaudit_and_study_guide_20260720.md).
\footnote{A birth into the state of three or more children counts as 3.60
children in completed fertility.}
The unit of account is mean annual gross labor earnings of working-age
households, \$82,882 in the 2005 and 2007 PSID (2022 dollars).%
\footnote{Stocks, such as wealth and house prices, are in annual units;
flows, such as income, consumption and rent, accrue over the four years.
Interest, discounting and depreciation compound over the period, so
$R_b=1.02^4$ and $\beta=\beta_{\text{ann}}^4$, and the property tax is four
times its annual rate. A data statistic for an age window weights each
four-year model cell by the share of the cell inside the window; for the
mean age at first birth, model births are dated at the midpoint of their
cell and the natality data are grouped into the same cells.}

\paragraph{Preferences.}
Risk aversion is $\sigma=2$. The equivalence scale is that of
\citet{scholzSeshadriKhitatrakunSaving2006}, with $\omega=\nu=0.7$: a couple
with two children needs 1.45 times the consumption, and the housing services
above its requirement, of a childless couple to reach the same utility. The
consumption weight $\alpha_0=0.733$ is measured directly. A childless renter
has no space requirement and no owner multiplier, so its utility is
Cobb--Douglas in consumption and rented space, and it spends a share
$1-\alpha_0$ of its budget on rent. In the 2019--2023 CEX, childless renters
spend 26.7\% of their consumption expenditure on housing.

\paragraph{Housing finance and taxes.}
The annual real interest rate is 2\%, and selling a home costs 6\% of its
value, as in \citet{greaneyDynamicUrbanEconomics2025}. Buyers can finance at
most $\phi=0.80$ of the price, the conventional limit above which a mortgage
requires private insurance, and there is no limit on payments relative to
income. I measure depreciation and the property tax at the national level,
with the method of \citet{greaneyDynamicUrbanEconomics2025}. Depreciation,
1.4\% a year, is the 2007 depreciation of owner-occupied residential
structures in the BEA fixed-asset accounts, divided by the value of the
housing stock, land included, in the Financial Accounts of the Federal
Reserve. The property tax, 1.06\% of value, is the ratio of aggregate
property taxes to the aggregate value of owner-occupied homes in the
2007--2011 ACS. Retirees receive a common pension equal to 23\% of mean
earnings, the ratio in the CPS ASEC, and the payroll tax that finances it is
8\%.
% Depreciation 0.01416143718381309, property tax 0.010598360773872594: CALIBRATION_STATUS
% (Sept 23 national housing-cost inputs; calibration_archive/context_refresh_20261002/
% CALIBRATION_STATUS.before.md l.4956). Pension/gross-earnings 0.2294460119, payroll tax
% 0.0802807096 (CALIBRATION_STATUS, Demography and closure).

\paragraph{Housing.}
Owners choose among homes of 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 rooms, and renters any size
up to $\bar h^R=6$ rooms. The cap reflects the rental stock: in the 2023 AHS,
43\% of owner-occupied homes have seven or more rooms, against 7.5\% of
rented homes (Appendix~\ref{app:housing-stock}). The supply elasticity in
\eqref{eq:m-supply} is $\eta=0.63$, from the estimates of floor-space supply of
\citet{baum-snowMicrogeographyHousingSupply2024}; rooms are closer to floor
space than to housing units. A higher elasticity, $\eta=1.5$, as in
\citet{kaplanMitmanViolanteHousing2020}, is the alternative.

\paragraph{Entry wealth and bequests.}
New households draw their initial wealth from the PSID. I take the net worth
of childless renter heads aged 18 to 24 in 1984--2019, set negative values to
zero, and scale it to model units by the ratio of mean model earnings at
entry to mean income in the sample. Entrants receive these wealth levels by
rank of income, so that entrants with higher earnings also bring more
wealth. The bequest shifter is $\theta_1=0.008$, as in
\citet{greaneyDynamicUrbanEconomics2025}.

\subsection{Calibrated parameters}
\label{sec:quant-identification}

I choose the remaining parameters in two steps. I first estimate the
earnings process from the PSID alone. Given this process, I choose the other
ten parameters jointly, to match ten moments of the 2007 data.

\paragraph{Earnings.}
I estimate the earnings process on non-overlapping four-year means of
household earnings in the 1984--1997 PSID, the last years in which the
survey is annual. Net of age and year effects, log earnings follow the
AR(1) process of Section~\ref{sec:m-env}. I choose its persistence $\rho_z$
and innovation standard deviation $\sigma_z$ to match the variance of log
four-year earnings, 0.51, and their first autocovariance, 0.37. This gives
$\rho_z=0.735$ and $\sigma_z=0.484$, which I approximate with nine states.
The second autocovariance, which I do not target, is 0.29 in the data and
0.27 in the process.

\paragraph{Other parameters.}
I choose the first ten calibrated parameters in Panel~B to match jointly the
ten other moments in Panel~A, while the 2007 house price keeps completed
fertility at replacement, 2.1.%
\footnote{The parameters minimize a sum of squared gaps between model and
data moments, each divided by a tolerance for the moment
(Appendix~\ref{app:identification}). The supply scale $H_0$ is not free: it
is the scale at which the 2007 price clears the housing market with one unit
of households.}
The parameters are identified jointly, but some moments are more informative
about some of them, and I discuss each in turn.

I choose the first-birth cost $\xi=0.295$ and the curvature of the benefit
of children $\gamma=0.646$ to match how births are spread across women aged
40 to 44 in the June Fertility Supplement of the CPS, pooling 2004 and 2006:
19.8\% are childless, and 21.4\% of mothers have exactly one child.
Intuitively, the cost is paid only at the first birth, so a higher $\xi$
raises childlessness and leaves the value of later children unchanged. A
higher $\gamma$ makes the benefit of each additional child decline faster,
so the first child is worth more relative to later ones: fewer women stay
childless and more mothers stop at one.

I choose the scale of the first-birth taste shocks, $\kappa_1=0.050$, to
match the mean age of mothers at first birth, 26.0 years in the NCHS natality
files for 2003--2006. Intuitively, the larger $\kappa_1$, the less first
births follow the trade-off between fecundity and earnings of
Section~\ref{sec:m-analysis} and the more they follow idiosyncratic tastes,
so first births spread over the fertile window and their mean age rises. The scale for later births,
$\kappa_C=0.129$, plays the same role for second and higher births. No
moment measures the timing of later births directly, so $\kappa_C$ is pinned
through the first birth: the option of further births raises the value of
becoming a parent, so a higher $\kappa_C$ lowers childlessness and brings
first births earlier. Children ever born by age 25, 0.81 in the CPS with
births capped at three, carries information on early births, but no
parameter moves it much (Section~\ref{sec:quant-fit}).

I choose $\psi_0$ so that completed fertility equals replacement, 2.1.
Because $H_0$ adjusts so that any price clears the market, a higher $\psi_0$
together with a higher price also delivers 2.1. Housing demand selects the
price: households occupy less space when space is dearer. $\psi_0=0.177$ is
therefore the preference for children that delivers 2.1 at the price at
which mean rooms are 5.73, as in the 2007 AHS, and ownership is 67.6\%.
% psi_child is free in the loss; at each trial value the price is the root of
% adjusted_births/(2.1*entry)-1 (equilibrium.py) and H0 is implied at population one.
% 1% higher psi0: price +3.0%, mean rooms -1.9% (identification_newacct moments).

I choose the owner multiplier $\chi=1.112$ to match ownership among heads
aged 30 to 55 in the 2005--2006 ACS, 67.6\%.%
\footnote{Both ownership samples exclude mobile homes, boats and vans (ACS
structure codes 3 to 10), which have no counterpart in the model.}
Intuitively, an owner obtains $\chi$ times the services of the same home, so
a higher $\chi$ raises the value of owning relative to renting.

I choose the space requirement $h_P=0.89$ rooms and the tenure taste scale
$\kappa_T=0.013$ to match how housing differs between parents and other
households. In the same ACS sample, heads whose oldest child is under four
own at a rate 12.8 percentage points higher than heads with no children at
home. Intuitively, children at home raise the space a household needs by
$h_P$, and homes larger than $\bar h^R$ rooms are only for sale, so parents
move into ownership. A higher $h_P$ widens the gap, and so does a smaller
$\kappa_T$, which makes tenure follow these needs more closely. Mean rooms,
5.73 in the 2007 AHS for heads aged 18 to 85, separate the two: they rise
with $h_P$ and barely move with $\kappa_T$. These two parameters are the
least precisely determined in the calibration
(Appendix~\ref{app:identification}).
% Weakest direction of the loss: h_P and kappa_T together, +-5.9% and +-6.3% for a unit
% rise in the loss; every other parameter moves by less than 2% along it
% (identification_newacct results.json valley_fit.param_half_pct).

The room response to the first birth adds the change in housing within
households. Recall from Section~\ref{sec:emp-first-birth} that future parents
in the PSID live in 4.71 rooms two to three years before the first birth,
and three to four years after it occupy 1.44 more rooms, relative to the
change among adults who never have a child. I target this response on the
common support of the panel and the model, and apply the same estimator to
simulated households.%
\footnote{Model first births are dated from the birth event and model
housing is mapped to the PSID interview calendar, so model and data compare
the change in rooms over the same event-time windows.}

% measurement_bridge/proposed_measurement_contract.json and original/rooms_coefficients.csv;
% adopted in output/model/production/2007/PROMOTION_RECEIPT_20261011_7p917.md.
% Common-support A2h target 1.439960832044094; model 1.4755 at the new point
% (identification_newacct moments/center.json, birth_dated +3/+4).
In the model this response reflects both the space a child requires and the
rooms households add as their earnings and savings grow with age, so it is
not tied to one parameter; it responds most to $\beta$ and $\psi_0$.

I choose the discount factor $\beta=0.936$ (annual) to match aggregate net
worth of 4.46 times aggregate annual gross labor earnings in the 2005 and
2007 PSID.%
\footnote{Net worth of heads aged 18 to 85, excluding business and farm
equity and other real estate; gross labor earnings of heads aged 18 to 65.}
Wealth responds to $\beta$ and to little else. Because savings decide when a
household can pay for the space a child requires, $\beta$ also moves
fertility and housing. I choose the bequest scale $\theta_0=0.244$ to match
the annual flow of estates to children in the 2007 SCF, 0.73\% of aggregate
net worth. This flow is the only target that responds to $\theta_0$.

As Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:quant-parameters} shows, the model matches
these targets closely. Childlessness is 20.1\% in the model and 19.8\% in
the data, ownership 66.9\% and 67.6\%, and wealth 4.35 and 4.46 times annual
earnings. The exception is children ever born by age 25, 0.56 in the model
against 0.81, which accounts for most of the loss; I return to it in
Section~\ref{sec:quant-fit}.

\subsection{The fertility decline, 2007--2023}
\label{sec:quant-transition}

Starting from the 2007 steady state, I hold all estimated parameters fixed
and let the preference for children decline permanently from its 2007 level
$\psi_0$ toward a lower level $\psi'$,
\begin{equation}
\psi_t=\psi'+(\psi_0-\psi')\,\rho^{\,\tau},
\label{eq:psi-decay}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ counts four-year periods since 2007. Households learn the whole
path in 2007 and have perfect foresight about it and about prices. The house
price, the pension and the rebate clear their markets at every date
(Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}). I set $\rho=0.35$ and choose
$\psi'=0.148$ to match period fertility in 2020--2023,
1.646. The model then gives 1.74 in 2012--2015, against
1.86 in the data (Figure~\ref{fig:quant-transition-fit}).%
\footnote{A model period is dated by its first year and compared with the
average period fertility of the four following calendar years: the period
dated 2011 with 2012--2015, and the period dated 2019 with 2020--2023.} The decline in
$\psi_t$ summarizes changes in the incentives to have children that the
model does not otherwise capture; its role is to match the fall in period
fertility while prices, family composition and household entry adjust.
From the period dated 2023 on, period fertility is 1.63 and
rises slowly, to 1.73 by 2063.

Figure~\ref{fig:quant-full-transition} shows the whole transition. Period
fertility returns to replacement slowly: it is 2.09 by 2203, and by 2403 the
economy is at its new steady state, with the number of households at 49\%,
total housing at 67\% and the house price at 53\% of their 2007 levels.
Construction responds to the price within each period and stays positive
along the whole path, so the stock moves with the price along the supply
curve: in 2023 the price is 3.6\% below its 2007 level and the stock 2.3\%
below; in 2063 they are 19.3\% and 12.6\% below. The rent does not follow
the price down at first. Along a transition, rent equals the user cost net of
the expected capital gain, so a price that is expected to keep falling raises
the rent: in 2023 the rent per room is 2.0\% above its 2007 level.

The housing stock governs how the price responds. The dashed lines in
Figure~\ref{fig:quant-full-transition} show the same transition with the stock
held at its 2023 level from then on. With no construction to adjust, the price
falls much further, to 47\% of its 2007 level around 2111, below its
long-run level. At that price fertility overshoots replacement, reaching 2.18
around 2119, and the additional children fill the fixed stock a generation
later. As they do, the price recovers and fertility returns to replacement.
In the long run the price is the same as in the baseline, 53\% of its 2007
level, because fertility at replacement pins it; the stock determines only how
many households live at that price, 71\% of the 2007 level instead of 49\%.
