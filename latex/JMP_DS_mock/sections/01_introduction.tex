\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Fertility is falling almost everywhere. In the United States, the period
fertility rate fell from about 2.1 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in
2023, and the world as a whole is likely below replacement for the first
time \citep{worldBankWDI,fernandezVillaverdeNorrickTerraIncognita2026}. A lasting decline in
births means fewer workers to support each retiree and slower growth, and it
has become a central concern for policymakers. The causes of the decline are elusive,%
\footnote{\citet{kearneyPuzzleFallingUS2022} find no economic or policy
factor that accounts for the decline. \citet{kearneyLevineWhyFertilityLow2025}
argue for ``shifting priorities'' across cohorts, a change in norms around
work, parenting, gender roles and leisure, shaped in part by new technologies
such as the smartphone and social media.
\citet{doepkeEconomicsFertilityNew2023} emphasize how easily parents can
combine careers and family.} but housing is often at the center of the
discussion on how to raise fertility in the future, both in public debate and
in policy.\footnote{In a fourteen-country
survey, housing is the third most cited reason for having fewer children than
desired \citep{unfpaSWP2025}. Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Poland and
Spain subsidize or guarantee home loans for families, with terms that improve
with the number of children
\citep{kfw300,greeceSpitiMou2,hungaryCSOKPlus,italyFondoPrimaCasa,polandFamilyRepayment,spainICOJovenes};
South Korea lends at subsidized rates to households with a newborn
\citep{koreaNewbornLoan}, and Singapore gives families priority for public
flats \citep{singaporeFPPS}. In the 2024 US presidential campaign, Kamala
Harris proposed up to \$25,000 in down-payment assistance for first-time
buyers \citep{harrisCampaign2024}, and JD Vance argued that young people are
delaying families ``because they can't afford a place to raise a family''
\citep{vanceNewsNation2024}.} The logic is intuitive. Housing is the largest item in household budgets, a third of all spending
\citep{blsConsumerExpenditures2024}.  Children put pressure on this already high bill:  they need space, so becoming a
parent raises a household's demand for housing. On the other hand, prospective
parents are young, with lower earnings and little savings, so that space is
hardest to afford exactly when it is needed.

Many policy proposals aim to help young families buy a home, by lowering
the cost of borrowing or the down payment it requires. If saving for a down
payment is what keeps young couples out of a home with room for a child, or
if larger homes have to be bought,\footnote{In the United States, 43\% of
owned homes have seven or more rooms, against 7.5\% of rented homes (2023
American Housing Survey).} this help should raise births. Whether it does,
and by how much, is less clear. Easier credit lowers the down payment, but
not the cost of the extra rooms, which a family pays every year its children
live at home: whether it rents or owns, and in bad years as well as good
ones. Couples who can borrow more may buy, and have their children, earlier
without having more of them. It's also hard to understand the effects of such policies in a short run, as demographic phenomena transmit through generations. Children today become households a
generation later: a policy that raises births also raises the demand for
housing when those children look for homes of their own, and makes space
more expensive for the next generation of parents.

This paper asks how the cost of family space shapes fertility, and what
housing policy can do about it. I answer it with a quantitative life-cycle model in which households
choose whether to have children, whether to rent or own, how much space to
occupy and how much to save under uninsurable earnings risk. I find that
policy raises births when it lowers the cost of a child rather than the cost
of a home, and that its effects build up over a generation. Removing the down
payment for all households and expanding the housing supply raise births by
about the same amount on impact, 1.3\% and 1.4\%. The gain from credit is gone
within four years, while the gain from supply keeps growing, to 3.0\% by
2063. Credit available only to parents raises births by 5.1\% on impact and
by 16.6\% by 2063.

The reason is what each policy changes for a couple deciding whether to have
a child. The space a child needs costs about 2.9\% of mean annual earnings
every year, and a family pays it whether it rents or owns, since rents track
the cost of owning. Lowering the down payment makes a home easier to buy, not
a child cheaper to house: most of the couples who use it buy the small homes
they would otherwise rent. More housing supply lowers prices and rents, and
with them the cost of family space for every cohort that follows. Credit for
parents goes to the birth itself, and lets parents borrow against their home
while their children live with them.

These findings matter for a growing empirical literature that links both
housing costs and access to credit with births
\citep{dettlingHousePricesBirth2014,lovenheimFamilyWealthShocks2013,hacamoBabiesMortgageDeregulation2021,cummingMonetaryPolicyBirth2024,dettlingKearney2026,vandoornikFazioRamadoraiSkrastinsHousingFertility2025}.
My model is consistent with a positive response of births when credit becomes
easier, and it complements this evidence in two ways. First, a structural
general-equilibrium model can disentangle the forces at play. Because
households choose children, tenure, space and savings together, the model
separates the cost of the space a child needs from the savings needed to buy
it, and it can evaluate policies that have not been tried, such as credit
available only to parents. Second, it takes a long view. Empirical designs
typically compare households that obtain credit with households that do not,
over a few years and at given prices. Some studies look at longer horizons
\citep{dettlingKearney2026,vandoornikFazioRamadoraiSkrastinsHousingFertility2025},
but they are necessarily retrospective: it takes more than two decades for
the effect of a policy on completed fertility to show. The model instead allows me to look
forward. Starting from the economy of 2023, it follows each policy for several
decades, as cohorts age, prices adjust and the children born under the policy
form households of their own. When credit is available to everyone, house
prices and rents respond to the change in demand; when a policy raises
births, the extra children compete for housing with the next cohort of
parents a generation later. The model can therefore show what a policy does
over a generation before a generation has passed.

The paper builds on evidence on how housing changes at parenthood. In the Panel Study of Income
Dynamics, three to four years after their first birth parents live in 1.44
more rooms than two to three years before it, a 31\% increase, and they are
27 percentage points more likely to own their home. Moves for more space
become a third more likely after the birth, while moves for a better
neighborhood do not. The demand for space jumps when a child arrives, and
owning is the main route to a large home.

In the model, households live through a life cycle and face uninsurable
earnings risk. They save, decide whether to have children, whether to rent or
own, and how much space to live in; a child requires a minimum amount of space
while it lives at home. The largest homes are only for sale, buying requires
a down payment, and owners can borrow against their home. Housing is supplied
along an upward-sloping supply curve, so house prices rise with the number of
households, and fertility choices feed back into the price of space a
generation later.

I approximate the United States economy of
2007 with a steady state in which births are at replacement. I assign some
parameters from external evidence and choose the others so that this steady
state matches fertility, housing and wealth moments of 2007. Holding these
parameters fixed, I then let the preference for children fall after 2007 so
that the model matches the decline in fertility through 2023, and I solve for
the transition of the economy as prices, family composition and household
formation adjust.

Every policy I study starts from the 2023 state of this transition and is
compared with the path the economy follows from there without it. The results
show why the impact of a policy can mislead. The births that credit adds in
2023 are gone within four years, and births fall below the path without
policy in the 2030s, because many of the children born early would otherwise
have been born later. Births then recover, to about 1\% above that path by
the mid-2050s, as households that enter later gain more from credit while
housing becomes cheaper. A supply expansion with the same effect on impact
keeps raising births, to 3.0\% above the path without policy by 2063, as
each cohort that enters adulthood spends its fertile years with cheaper space
and the additional children form households of their own. Credit for parents
raises births by 5.1\% in 2023 and by 16.6\% by 2063. In the long run,
births per household return to replacement, so the lasting gains of a policy
show up as more households rather than more children per household: with
credit for parents, the house price is 33\% higher in the new steady state,
and there are 36\% more households. A rebated property tax, by contrast,
lowers the house price by 7.0\% but raises rents by 14.5\%, and births
barely move: a lower price is not cheaper space. Housing policy aimed at
births should therefore be judged by its long-run effects, not only by its
effect on impact.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This paper contributes to many strands of the literature on fertility, housing, and macroeconomics.

First, the paper contributes to a large empirical literature on how housing conditions shape childbearing. Studies show that fertility is lower where rent per room is higher \citep{simonTamuraHigherRents2009}, dwelling size predicts first births \citep{stromHousingFirstBirths2010}, and higher house prices raise births among owners but lower them among renters \citep{dettlingHousePricesBirth2014,lovenheimFamilyWealthShocks2013}. Two recent papers model housing and fertility jointly. \citet{khorunzhinaMillerAmericanDream2022} estimate a dynamic model of female labor supply, fertility and the first home purchase at given house prices, and \citet{couillardBuildBabyBuild2025} estimates a model of fertility, living arrangements and location in which rents are the cost of housing. My model combines elements of both: households save, choose whether to own and how much space to live in, buying requires a down payment, and house prices respond to the number of households. This lets me separate the recurring cost of family space from the equity needed to buy it, and compare credit and supply policies in the same economy.

Second, it relates to work on credit and fertility. Quasi-experimental studies find that credit raises births. Households exposed to the deregulation of home lending are more likely to buy a home and have a child \citep{hacamoBabiesMortgageDeregulation2021}, lower interest rates on home loans raise births among households whose rate resets \citep{cummingMonetaryPolicyBirth2024}, federally backed home loans raised births and completed fertility during the baby boom \citep{dettlingKearney2026}, and winners of housing-credit lotteries in Brazil have more children, especially at ages 20 to 25 \citep{vandoornikFazioRamadoraiSkrastinsHousingFertility2025}. In these studies credit comes together with a home. A quantitative literature finds that credit relaxation moves ownership but not the size of the homes households live in or house prices \citep{kaplanMitmanViolanteHousing2020}, and that removing the down payment requirement does little to make ownership attractive \citep{halketVasudevSaving2014}. I reconcile the two. In my model, relaxing credit for everyone lets young households buy the small homes they would otherwise rent, which brings births forward: the gain on impact is gone within a few years. Credit tied to children lowers the cost of the space a child requires, and raises completed fertility. Credit therefore matters for fertility only to the extent that it lowers the cost of family space.

Third, it relates to work on earnings risk and fertility. Because a child is an irreversible commitment, prudent parents who face more risk want fewer children \citep{fraserIncomeRisk2001}. Quantitative life-cycle models find that uninsurable earnings risk delays and reduces births \citep{sommerFertilityChoiceLife2016,ejrnaesJorgensenFamilyPlanning2020,nakakuniFertilityIncomeUncertainty2026}, and \citet{deLaCroixPommeretChildbearing2021} show that risk gives the postponement of a first birth an option value. Quasi-experimental studies find that job displacement and job insecurity lower fertility \citep{delBonoWeberWinterEbmer2012,clarkLepinteurJobInsecurity2022}. None of these papers includes housing. In my model, part of the commitment a child entails is the space it needs, paid for every period at a price set in the housing market. The response of fertility to earnings risk therefore depends on housing costs and on the down payment required to own family space.

Fourth, it builds on quantitative models of housing over the life cycle, which study tenure choice under earnings risk, down payments and transaction costs \citep{fisherGervaisHomeOwnership2011,halketVasudevSaving2014,boarGoreaMidriganLiquidity2022}, and the joint determination of house prices, rents and ownership \citep{kaplanMitmanViolanteHousing2020,greenwaldGurenCredit2025}. In this literature, rising earnings risk accounts for much of the decline in homeownership among young households \citep{fisherGervaisHomeOwnership2011,pazPardoHomeownership2024}. Household size, however, is exogenous or absent. I make fertility a choice. A birth raises the household's demand for space over its life cycle and, a generation later, the number of households that compete for housing. Housing policy therefore shapes both family size and the future demand for housing.

Finally, although this paper does not try to explain the decline in
fertility, it relates to the broad literature on its causes and
consequences. Work on the causes stresses the cost and timing of children,
bargaining within couples, shifting priorities across cohorts and the
compatibility of career and family
\citep{jonesSchoonbroodtTertilt2010,doepkeBargainingBabiesTheory2019,kearneyPuzzleFallingUS2022,doepkeEconomicsFertilityNew2023,kimStatusExternalitiesEducation2024,kearneyLevineWhyFertilityLow2025}.
Work on the consequences studies the efficiency of population growth and the
economics of a shrinking world
\citep{golosovjonestertilt2007,fernandezVillaverdeNorrickTerraIncognita2026}.
I take the decline as given and ask what housing policy can do in an economy
where it is under way.
