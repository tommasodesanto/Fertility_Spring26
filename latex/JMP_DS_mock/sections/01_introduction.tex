\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Fertility is falling almost everywhere. In the United States, the period
fertility rate fell from about 2.1 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in
2023, and the world as a whole is likely below replacement for the first
time \citep{worldBankWDI,fernandezVillaverdeNorrickTerraIncognita2026}. A lasting decline in
births means fewer workers to support each retiree and slower growth, and it
has become a central concern for policymakers. The causes of the decline are elusive,%
\footnote{\citet{kearneyPuzzleFallingUS2022} find no economic or policy
factor that accounts for the decline. \citet{kearneyLevineWhyFertilityLow2025}
argue for ``shifting priorities'' across cohorts, a change in norms around
work, parenting, gender roles and leisure, shaped in part by new technologies
such as the smartphone and social media.
\citet{doepkeEconomicsFertilityNew2023} emphasize how easily parents can
combine careers and family.} but housing is often at the center of the
discussion on how to raise fertility in the future, both in public debate and
in policy.\footnote{In a fourteen-country
survey, housing is the third most cited reason for having fewer children than
desired \citep{unfpaSWP2025}. Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Poland and
Spain subsidize or guarantee home loans for families, with terms that improve
with the number of children
\citep{kfw300,greeceSpitiMou2,hungaryCSOKPlus,italyFondoPrimaCasa,polandFamilyRepayment,spainICOJovenes};
South Korea lends at subsidized rates to households with a newborn
\citep{koreaNewbornLoan}, and Singapore gives families priority for public
flats \citep{singaporeFPPS}. In the 2024 US presidential campaign, Kamala
Harris proposed up to \$25,000 in down-payment assistance for first-time
buyers \citep{harrisCampaign2024}, and JD Vance argued that young people are
delaying families ``because they can't afford a place to raise a family''
\citep{vanceNewsNation2024}.} The logic is intuitive. Housing is the largest item in household budgets, a third of all spending
\citep{blsConsumerExpenditures2024}.  Children put pressure on this already high bill:  they need space, so becoming a
parent raises a household's demand for housing. On the other hand, prospective
parents are young, with lower earnings and little savings, so that space is
hardest to afford exactly when it is needed.

Many policy proposals aim to help young families buy a home, by lowering
the cost of borrowing or the down payment it requires. The idea behind them
is that family space is mostly for sale. In the United States, 43\% of owned
homes have seven or more rooms, against 7.5\% of rented
homes.\footnote{2023 American Housing Survey.} A couple that wants room for a
child usually has to buy, and buying requires savings that young households
do not have. Easier credit removes that obstacle. Whether it raises
fertility, however, is far from clear. The down payment is only the entry
cost of family space. Once in a larger home, a family pays for its extra
rooms every year its children live at home, whether it rents or owns, and it
keeps paying when earnings fall. Credit lowers the first cost, not the
second. It may let couples buy, and have children, sooner, without making a
child any cheaper, and so change when births happen more than how many there
are. Its effects also change over time. Births today become households a
generation later, and the demand they create raises the price of space for
the next generation of parents. A policy judged by its effect on births next
year may look very different a generation later.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over
what horizon.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:intro}

This paper contributes to three literatures: on housing costs and fertility,
on earnings risk and fertility, and on housing over the life cycle.

\paragraph{Housing costs and fertility.}
A large empirical literature finds that housing conditions shape
childbearing. Fertility is lower where rent per room is higher
\citep{simonTamuraHigherRents2009}, dwelling size predicts first births
\citep{stromHousingFirstBirths2010}, and higher house prices raise births
among owners but lower them among renters
\citep{dettlingHousePricesBirth2014,lovenheimFamilyWealthShocks2013}. A
second group of papers finds that access to mortgage credit raises births
\citep{hacamoBabiesMortgageDeregulation2021,cummingMonetaryPolicyBirth2024,dettlingKearneyModernMortgage2025,vandoornikFazioRamadoraiSkrastinsHousingFertility2025}.
Two recent papers model the joint choice of housing and children.
\citet{khorunzhinaMillerAmericanDream2022} estimate a dynamic model of
female labor supply, fertility and the first home purchase at given house
prices, and \citet{couillardBuildBabyBuild2025} estimates a model of
fertility, living arrangements and location in which rents are the cost of
housing. Relative to these papers, I study fertility together with saving,
the choice of space and tenure, and uninsurable earnings risk, in a housing
market with an equilibrium price. This separates the recurring cost of
family space from the equity needed to buy it, and allows me to compare
housing supply and mortgage credit within the same economy.

\paragraph{Earnings risk and fertility.}
A child is an irreversible commitment, and prudent parents facing more risk
want fewer children \citep{fraserIncomeRisk2001}. Quantitative life-cycle
models find that uninsurable earnings risk delays and reduces births
\citep{sommerFertilityChoiceLife2016,ejrnaesJorgensenFamilyPlanning2020,nakakuniFertilityIncomeUncertainty2026},
and \citet{deLaCroixPommeretChildbearing2021} show that risk gives the
postponement of a first birth an option value. Job displacement and job
insecurity lower fertility in quasi-experimental studies
\citep{delBonoWeberWinterEbmer2012,clarkLepinteurJobInsecurity2022}. None
of these papers includes housing. In this paper, part of the commitment a
child entails is the space it needs, which must be paid for every period at
a price set in the housing market. The fertility response to earnings risk
therefore depends on housing costs and on the down payment required to own
family space.

\paragraph{Housing over the life cycle.}
Quantitative models of housing study tenure choice under earnings risk,
down payments and transaction costs
\citep{fisherGervaisHomeOwnership2011,halketVasudevSaving2014,boarGoreaMidriganLiquidity2022},
and the joint determination of house prices, rents and ownership
\citep{kaplanMitmanViolanteHousing2020,greenwaldGurenCredit2025}. Rising
earnings risk accounts for much of the decline in homeownership among young
households
\citep{fisherGervaisHomeOwnership2011,pazPardoHomeownership2024}. In these
models, household size is exogenous or absent. I make fertility a choice. A
birth raises the household's demand for space over its life cycle and, a
generation later, the number of households competing for housing, so
housing policy shapes both family size and the future demand for housing.
