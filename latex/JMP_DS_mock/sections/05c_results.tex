\section{What a First Child Costs}
\label{sec:results}

Before turning to policy, this section describes how a first child changes a
household's choices in the calibrated model, and why the cost of the space a
child requires depends on earnings risk. All results in this section are for
the 2007 steady state at its equilibrium price. The section organizes around
one distinction. A child raises the space a household needs in every period
the child is at home. Changes in that per-period cost, or in the household's
ability to bear it, change how many children are born. Changes in the one-time
cost of getting into a home of a given size, such as the down payment, change
when households buy and when they have children.

\subsection{Household choices at the first birth}
\label{sec:results-policy-functions}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-policy} shows the choices of a childless renter aged
26 to 29 with middle earnings, as a function of its liquid wealth, in the
period in which it has no child and in the period in which a first child has
just been born. A first child raises the space the household occupies at
every level of wealth. Without savings, it raises rooms from 5.1 to 5.8 and
lowers the probability of owning from 54 to 37 percent: the household needs
more space at the moment it can least afford a down payment, so it rents
more of it. With savings, the household buys when the child arrives. The
value of trying for a child, net of the cost of becoming a parent, is negative
for a household without savings and turns positive once liquid wealth reaches
about a third of mean annual earnings. The probability of trying therefore
rises with wealth at low wealth and is flat above it.

\subsection{The housing cost of a first child}
\label{sec:results-cost}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-cost} shows the same comparison across the earnings
distribution, for childless renters aged 26 to 29 without savings. A first
child raises the space the household occupies by up to one room. Because
renters cannot borrow, the household pays for that room out of current
consumption: at low earnings, consumption falls by 11 percent in the period of
the birth. At high earnings, where the household buys a larger home, it does
not.

The cost is recurring. Because rent equals the user cost of owning,
$r=(R_b-1+\delta_H+\tau^p)P$ in a steady state, a room costs the same each
period whether it is rented or owned, and the space requirement $h_P$ costs
$r\,h_P$ every period the child is at home, for renters and owners alike. At
the 2007 price, the user cost is 18.0 percent of the price of a room per
four-year period, so the space requirement alone costs
$0.180\times0.681\times1.10=0.135$ per period, or 3.4 percent of mean annual
earnings each year; the additional rooms households choose cost more. Mortgage
credit changes how much equity a household needs to buy a home of a given
size, $(1-\phi)P_th_t$, not this recurring cost.

\subsection{Earnings risk and the cost of a child}
\label{sec:results-risk}

A simple calculation shows why earnings risk raises the cost of this
commitment. Consider a childless household whose income next period is
$y'=\bar y e^{\varepsilon}$, with $\E e^{\varepsilon}=1$, and suppose a child
commits it to a per-period outlay $F=r\,h_P$. Without savings, the utility
cost of the child next period is $K=\E\,g(y')$, with $g(c)=u(c)-u(c-F)$.
Since $g'(c)=u'(c)-u'(c-F)<0$ and $g''(c)=u''(c)-u''(c-F)>0$ whenever
$u'''>0$, the cost $K$ rises with any mean-preserving spread of income
(Kimball, 1990; Rothschild and Stiglitz, 1970). To second order,
\begin{equation}
 K(\sigma_\varepsilon)-K(0)\approx\tfrac12\,u'''(\bar y)\,F\,\bar y^{2}\,
 \sigma_\varepsilon^{2},
 \label{eq:r-risk-cost}
\end{equation}
which is proportional to the commitment $F$ and to the variance of income.
Three predictions follow. Risk raises the cost of a child most for households
without savings, since a buffer smooths $c'$. The effect is larger the larger
the commitment, so cheaper space and lower risk are complements. And because
a birth is irreversible and possible only during a fertile window, a higher
hurdle first delays births and then turns part of the delay into forgone
births (de la Croix and Pommeret, 2021). Figure~\ref{fig:results-risk-schematic}
illustrates the logic: a higher rent raises the per-period cost of a child,
and higher risk raises the earnings a household needs before a first birth is
worth having.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-risk} shows the prediction in the model. I lower the
standard deviation of the four-year earnings shock from 0.48 to 0.35, holding
mean earnings fixed, and solve for the new steady state at the same price.
For a childless renter aged 26 to 29 with middle earnings and no savings, the
probability of a first birth rises from 0.40 to 0.60. With a buffer, the two
curves meet: risk deters births mainly among households without savings.
Across all households, completed fertility rises by 18 percent, from 1.87 to
2.21, and childlessness falls from 18.7 to 7.6 percent.

How much of this effect runs through housing is not established by these
results. The effect of lower risk is about the same at calibrations whose
space requirement differs by a factor of two or more, which suggests that at
this calibration much of it runs through the consumption cost of a child, the
equivalence scale, and through a first-birth margin that responds strongly to
small changes in its value ($\kappa_1$ is small). A decomposition at fixed
prices, with $h_P$ at its value and at zero, would settle the point.

\subsection{Mortgage credit and family space}
\label{sec:results-credit}

Mortgage credit enters the household's problem only through the purchase
constraint. Let $E_t\equiv b_{t+1}+P_th_t$ be a buyer's net wealth after the
purchase. The collateral constraint \eqref{eq:m-ltv} requires
$E_t\geq(1-\phi)P_th_t$, so a household with equity $\bar E$ can buy any
owned size whose down payment is below $\bar E$. A higher financed share
$\phi'>\phi$ makes larger homes feasible at the same equity. Credit can
therefore raise fertility by unlocking housing that is more valuable with a
child. But if that space is no larger than the rental cap,
$h^F\leq\bar h^R$, it can also be rented, and its per-period cost $r_th^F$ is
the same whichever way it is obtained. At the 2007 calibration, the space a
family needs fits well within the six-room rental cap, and the smallest owned
home, two rooms, holds a family with $h_P=1.10$. Credit therefore mostly
changes who owns and when, not the per-period cost of a child's space. The
policy experiments of Section~\ref{sec:policy} test this prediction in
general equilibrium.
