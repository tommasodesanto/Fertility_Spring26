\section{Additional Policy Results}
\label{app:policy-more}

Table~\ref{tab:app-policy-more} reports the policy experiments that the main
text does not discuss, with the same design as Section~\ref{sec:policy}: every
arm starts from the 2023 state and is compared with the no-policy path at the
same earnings risk and under the same housing supply.

\paragraph{A rebated property tax.} The annual property tax rises from 1.06 to
2 percent of value from 2023 on, and the revenue is rebated equally to all
households. The tax lowers the price by 6.2 percent on impact, close to the
8.9 percent of the supply expansion, but births do not move ($-0.2$ percent)
and completed fertility rises by only 0.8 percent. The comparison is the
cleanest test of the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:results}. The tax lowers
the price but raises the user-cost rate from 18.0 to 21.8 percent of the price
per period, so the per-period cost of a room rises; and the rebate returns the
revenue per household, not per room, so a family that needs more space pays
more for it. Births follow the per-period cost of a room, not its price.
\mocktodo{read the rent per room along the 7.9 tax path from the saved run;
the 13 percent rise is a stationary calculation.}

\paragraph{Earnings insurance and a flat transfer.} The insurance arm pays
every working household a quarter of the shortfall of its earnings below the
median earnings state at its age. The flat arm pays the expected value of that
transfer at each age to every working household. Neither is financed within
the model. Insurance raises births by 14.8 percent on impact and completed
fertility by 13.8 percent; the flat transfer by 8.0 and 7.8 percent. The
difference, about 6 percentage points, is the value of insuring the downside
of earnings rather than paying the same amount to everyone, as Fraser (2001)
predicts for an irreversible commitment. Under lower earnings risk the
difference shrinks to 1.5 points on impact. Because neither transfer is
financed, these are upper bounds on what a budget-neutral program would do.

\paragraph{A fixed housing stock.} Holding the stock at its 2023 level,
instead of letting it follow the supply curve, lowers the price by 9.2 percent
in 2023 and 29 percent by 2063, as the population shrinks into the same stock;
completed fertility rises by 3.5 percent for the 2023 cohort and 8.6 percent
for the 2043 cohort. With the stock fixed, demand policies raise the price
more and births somewhat less. Mortgage credit raises the price by 2.1
percent on impact and leaves completed fertility unchanged ($+0.1$ percent),
the case in which credit is capitalized into prices. Credit for parents is the
exception: its effect on completed fertility is larger with a fixed stock
(7.8 against 6.3 percent), plausibly because the falling price makes ownership
cheaper, so access to ownership tied to a child is worth more. This reading is
inferred, not decomposed.

\paragraph{A more elastic supply.} With a supply elasticity of 1.5 from 2023 on,
a 10 percent shift in the supply curve lowers the price by 5.0 percent and
raises completed fertility by 2.6 percent; births per percentage point of
price decline are about the same as at $\eta=0.63$. Credit remains a matter of
timing at either elasticity.

\section{A CES Housing Composite}
\label{app:ces}

This appendix is from the previous calibration point (loss 14.40, on a
matched-state room target) and has not been redone at the current one. The
baseline combines a Cobb--Douglas composite with the space requirement
$h_P$. As a robustness check I replace both with a CES composite in which
children raise the rooms needed for given housing services:
\begin{equation}
 u_t=\frac{1}{1-\sigma}\left(\frac{Q_t}{e(m_t)}\right)^{1-\sigma}+\psi_t m_t^{1-\gamma},\qquad
 Q_t=\Bigl[\alpha_0\,c_t^{\rho}+(1-\alpha_0)\Bigl(\frac{\chi^{\1\{\mathrm{own}\}}\,h_t}{k(m_t)}\Bigr)^{\rho}\Bigr]^{1/\rho},
\end{equation}
with $\rho=(\varepsilon-1)/\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon=0.67$, and
$k(m_t)=\exp\bigl[\eta_1\1\{m_t\ge1\}+\eta_2\max(m_t-1,0)\bigr]$. Housing and
consumption are complements, and without a floor space is not a fixed entry
cost of a child. I estimate $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$, the rooms needed by the
first and each later child at home, and $\alpha_0$, so twelve parameters are
chosen to match thirteen moments. The new moments are the rent share of
renters aged 30--55 with no child at home (0.360, CE), the probability of
moving from renting to owning within four years at ages 22--41 (0.281, PSID),
and the share of mothers with three or more children (0.357, CPS). The rooms
added at the first birth are re-measured (1.025, PSID), the room gap between
larger and smaller families becomes a target, and the recent-parent
ownership gap becomes a validation moment.

\section{Solution Algorithms}
\label{app:algorithms}

\paragraph{Stationary equilibrium.} I solve for the house price $P$ and the
rebate $T$, given parameters, a fixed preference for children and the payroll
tax. The pension is set before the search: in a stationary population the
age distribution follows from survival and entry, so the pension that
balances the payroll tax is known in advance, and the balance is verified at
the end.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Guess $P$ and derive the rent $r$ from the user cost.
\item Solve the household problem backward over age and compute the choice
probabilities.
\item Construct the stationary distribution of households, iterating on the
rebate until $T$ times the number of households equals property-tax revenue.
In the calibration the number of households is one and $H_0$ is set so that
supply equals demand at $P$; in the counterfactuals $H_0$ is fixed and the
number of households equals housing supply divided by mean housing per
household.
\item Compute the renewal residual: births divided by 2.1, relative to the
number of entrants, minus one. It is zero when the population reproduces
itself.
\item Update $P$ by bracketing the root of the renewal residual and repeat
until the renewal and rebate residuals are below $10^{-6}$. Re-solve at the
selected price and require the same residuals, and verify that the pension
budget balances to the same tolerance.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Transition.} I solve for the paths $(P_t,\varpi_t,T_t)_{t\geq t_0}$,
given the inherited households and a preference believed permanent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Solve the new stationary equilibrium; it supplies the terminal value
function.
\item Guess the three paths and derive rents from \eqref{eq:m-rent}.
\item Solve the household problem backward in calendar time from the terminal
value.
\item Advance households and the queue of entrants forward from the inherited
state, and aggregate housing demand and taxes at each date.
\item Compute the three residuals at every date. The loss is the largest
residual across dates.
\item Update the paths jointly and repeat until the loss is below tolerance.
Extend the horizon to check that early responses are stable.
\end{enumerate}
The transitions in the paper use 100 four-year periods, from 2015 (or 2023
for the policy experiments) to 2411, with tolerances of $2\times10^{-4}$ on
the housing market and $2\times10^{-5}$ on the pension and the rebate, and
$10^{-6}$ on the renewal condition of the terminal steady state. Two
lower-risk endpoints with credit are accepted at the best evaluated price, with
births within 0.3 percent of replacement, because household tenure and credit
choices on the discrete grid make the renewal residual jump near the root;
their long-run values are reported only as approximately zero.
