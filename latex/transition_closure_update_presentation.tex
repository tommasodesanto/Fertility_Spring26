\begin{frame}{Today: from the population closure to an equilibrium transition}
\textbf{Last time:} we focused on the missing population closure:
\[
\text{births today}
\longrightarrow \text{new households later}
\longrightarrow \text{housing demand and prices}.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{Today:} I try to make that idea an equilibrium object:
\begin{itemize}
  \item build the simplest model with a well-defined steady state;
  \item separate the immediate response to a fertility-preference shock from the later demographic adjustment;
  \item add the same population accounting to the existing quantitative model;
  \item ask whether the 2007--2023 economy can be interpreted as a point along such a transition.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{The household problem remains the same. The update is an attempt to close the aggregate equilibrium around it.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{A toy economy with two generations and one housing market}
\small
To fix ideas, let's look at a very simplified economy. Each date contains a young generation and an old generation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $Y_t$ is the number of young households at date $t$; $O_t$ is the number of old households.
  \item A young household chooses consumption, housing services, and children. Next period it becomes old.
  \item The children chosen by today's young households determine the size of the next young generation.
  \item Young and old households purchase housing services in the same market at price $p_t$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
One period is one generation. The law of motion is
\[
Y_{t+1}=\frac{n_t}{\bar n}Y_t,
\qquad
O_{t+1}=Y_t,
\]
where $n_t$ is children per young household and $\bar n$ is the number needed to reproduce the young cohort.

\medskip
\textbf{Deliberate simplification:} housing is a flow service. There is no tenure choice, mortgage, saving, or bequest in this toy economy; those mechanisms remain in the quantitative model.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Young households choose housing and children}
A young household earns $y$ and chooses consumption $c$, housing services $h$, and children $n$:
\[
u(c,h,n)=c+\alpha\log h+v\log n,
\]
\[
c+ph+\big[q+a(p+\omega)\big]n=y.
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha$ is the value of adult housing; $v$ is the value of children.
  \item Each child requires $a$ units of housing and $q$ units of other goods.
  \item $\omega$ is an additional cost of accessing child-suitable housing.
  \item Hence the total resource cost of one child is $q+a(p+\omega)$.
\end{itemize}

Old households do not choose fertility. Their reduced-form housing demand is
\[
h_O(p)=\frac{\bar h_O}{p},
\]
where $\bar h_O$ measures their demand for housing services.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Housing costs jointly determine fertility and housing demand}
The young household's interior choices are
\[
n(p)=\frac{v}{q+a(p+\omega)},
\qquad
h_Y(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p}+a n(p).
\]

Thus a higher $p$ has two effects: it reduces adult housing demand and raises the housing cost of a child, reducing fertility.

\medskip
Aggregate housing supply is
\[
H^s(p)=\bar H p^\varepsilon,
\]
where $\bar H$ is the scale of housing supply and $\varepsilon\geq0$ is its elasticity. At each date, $p_t$ clears the market:
\[
Y_t h_Y(p_t)+O_t h_O(p_t)=H^s(p_t).
\]

\textbf{This one price therefore links household fertility to aggregate cohort sizes.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{A steady state keeps prices and cohort sizes constant}
\small
Let $\bar n$ denote the children per young household needed to reproduce the next young cohort. Then
\[
Y_{t+1}=\frac{n(p_t)}{\bar n}Y_t,
\qquad
O_{t+1}=Y_t.
\]

A steady state is a constant triple $(Y^*,O^*,p^*)$. It therefore requires
\[
n(p^*)=\bar n,
\qquad
Y^*=O^*.
\]
The child decision pins down the steady-state housing cost,
\[
p^*=\frac{v/\bar n-q}{a}-\omega,
\]
and housing clearing pins down the population scale $S^*=Y^*+O^*$:
\[
S^*=\frac{2\bar H(p^*)^{1+\varepsilon}}
{\alpha+\bar h_O+a\bar n p^*}.
\]

\textbf{Replacement determines the price; housing clearing determines how many households live at that price.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stage 1: the initial steady-state equilibrium}

\begin{column}{0.35\textwidth}
\footnotesize
With equal cohorts and fertility at replacement, housing clearing gives
\[
S^*(p)=
\frac{2\bar H p^{1+\varepsilon}}
{\alpha+\bar h_O+a\bar n p}.
\]
It slopes upward: supply weakly rises with $p$, while each household demands less housing.

The replacement condition in the right panel selects $p_0$; the left panel then selects the population $S_0$ supported at that price. Point $A$ is the initial steady state.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vspace{-0.55em}
\small
Along the steady-state schedule, direct differentiation gives
\[
\frac{dS^*}{dp}
=\frac{2\bar H p^\varepsilon
\big[(1+\varepsilon)(\alpha+\bar h_O)+\varepsilon a\bar n p\big]}
{(\alpha+\bar h_O+a\bar n p)^2}>0
\qquad\text{for }\varepsilon\geq0.
\]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stage 2: the impact of the preference shock}
\vspace{1.5em}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.63\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.46\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../output/model/fertility_population_housing_transition_note/partial_equilibrium_response.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.34\textwidth}
\footnotesize
Let the value of children fall permanently from $v_0$ to $v_1<v_0$.

\medskip
\textbf{On impact, cohort sizes cannot move:} $Y_t=Y_0$, $O_t=O_0$, and $S_t=S_0$.

\medskip
Young households want fewer children and less child-related space. At the inherited population $S_0$, housing clearing therefore lowers the price from $p_0$ to $\widetilde p$.

\medskip
On the new child-demand curve, fertility falls to $\widetilde n=n(\widetilde p;v_1)<\bar n$.

\textbf{Point $I$ is an impact equilibrium, not a steady state.}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Stage 3: demographic adjustment}
\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.63\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.49\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../output/model/fertility_population_housing_transition_note/demographic_adjustment.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.34\textwidth}
\footnotesize
At $I$, fertility is below replacement, so the next young cohort is smaller:
\[
Y_{t+1}=\frac{\widetilde n}{\bar n}Y_t<Y_t.
\]

Once the smaller cohorts dominate, adult population and housing demand fall. The housing cost moves from $\widetilde p$ toward $p_1$.

\medskip
The lower housing cost makes children cheaper, so fertility rises along the red child-demand curve.

\medskip
At $A'$, fertility again equals $\bar n$. This is the new steady state, with $p_1<p_0$ and $S_1<S_0$.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Below-replacement fertility need not lower population immediately}
Let $m_t=n(p_t)/\bar n$ be the replacement ratio and $\mu_t=O_t/Y_t$ the old-to-young cohort ratio. Population can rise with $m_t<1$ whenever
\[
\mu_t<m_t<1.
\]

\begin{block}{A numerical example}
\centering
\Large
$100$ young $+$ $70$ old
$\quad\longrightarrow\quad$
$80$ young $+$ $100$ old

\normalsize
\medskip
$m_t=0.8<1$, but total households rise from $170$ to $180$.
\end{block}

Housing costs can rise as well if the aging cohort retains more housing than is released by the shrinking young cohort:
\[
(1-\mu_t)h_O(p_t)>(1-m_t)h_Y(p_t).
\]

\vspace{-0.35em}
\textbf{U.S. interpretation:} inherited cohorts can keep population and housing costs rising even after fertility falls below replacement.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The household model itself is unchanged}
\small
The household state $x$ already contains wealth, income, location, tenure, house size, fertility history, and children at home. Households still solve the same sequential fertility, tenure, housing, saving, and moving problem.

\medskip
What changes is the distribution. Let
\[
g_t(j,x)
\]
be the mass of age-$j$ households in state $x$ at calendar date $t$. In the old stationary exercise, this distribution was normalized back to a fixed population. In the transition, its total mass and age composition are allowed to evolve.

\medskip
The household choices encoded in the transition operator $\mathcal Q_t$ determine where surviving households move across states:
\[
\text{age, fertility, tenure, housing, and wealth at }t
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{states at }t+1.
\]

\textbf{This preserves the intergenerational mechanism; it stops resetting the economy to the same cohort sizes.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Three equations add the aggregate feedback}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Birth-to-entry accounting.} Adjusted births $b_t$ generate future entrant households after twenty years:
  \[
  B_t=\frac{b_t}{2.1}.
  \]
  The factor $2.1$ combines sex composition, survival to entry age, and household formation.
  \item \textbf{A calendar-time distribution law.} Existing households age according to their lifecycle choices, while the delayed birth cohort enters:
  \[
  g_{t+1}=\mathcal Q_t g_t+\text{entrants}_{t+1}.
  \]
  \item \textbf{Housing clearing at every date.}
  \[
  H^s(P_t)=\int h_t(j,x)\,dg_t(j,x).
  \]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Sequential fertility, tenure, saving, and bequests are unchanged; only cohort masses now evolve over calendar time.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{We calibrate an economy moving from 2007 to 2023}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Construct an old reference economy.} Choose the initial fertility preference so completed fertility is $2.1$. This is a replacement normalization, not a measured 2007 target.
  \item \textbf{Construct the 2007 state.} Reweight the old distribution only by observed 2007 age shares, preserving wealth, tenure, and fertility states within each age.
  \item \textbf{Run the economy to 2023.} Feed in Census household totals and ACS age shares. Let the fertility preference decline linearly, and estimate the household parameters and total decline using twelve maintained moments, evaluated mainly in 2023.
  \item \textbf{Check the path.} Compare untargeted model movements with period fertility, birth timing, ownership, rooms, house prices, and rents.
  \item \textbf{Continue after 2023.} Hold preferences fixed and let the inherited household distribution and birth pipeline evolve.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\textbf{The population and age bridge are inputs, not successes of the model. The 2007 economy is a dated initial condition, not literally the old steady state.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What exactly is the post-2023 continuation?}
\small
Both exercises begin from the \textbf{same calibrated 2023 distribution}: the same ages, wealth, tenure, fertility histories, children at home, and birth pipeline. We then hold the 2023 fertility preference fixed and introduce no policy.

\medskip
The two paths differ only in how new households enter after 2023:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closed benchmark.} There is no outside entry. Future entrants come only from earlier birth cohorts.
  \item \textbf{Open sensitivity.} A fixed outside flow $M$ and a fixed local-retention rate $\rho$ continue from the old reference economy.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
Because births take twenty years to become entrant households, the first post-2023 years are largely predetermined. Inherited cohorts can make population and housing costs rise briefly even though reproduction is below replacement.

\medskip
\textbf{These are controlled equilibrium experiments, not forecasts.} The open case does not hold the outside-origin entrant \emph{share} fixed: $0.169$ only normalizes the old reference state.
\end{frame}

\footnotesize
At each stationary price $P$, let $E(P)$ be entrants and $B(P)$ the future households generated by births. A positive closed steady state requires $B(P)/E(P)=1$.

\smallskip
Instead, over the audited grid,
\[
0.552\leq B(P)/E(P)\leq0.678<1.
\]
Even almost free housing does not reproduce the entrant cohort.

\smallskip
\textbf{Therefore:} the closed continuation is a finite-horizon experiment, not a transition between two positive steady states.
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Where this leaves the paper}
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The theoretical contribution is compact.} The existing housing--fertility mechanism is embedded in a population closure. Lower fertility eventually reduces population and housing costs, while inherited cohorts can make both rise initially.
  \item \textbf{The quantitative exercise is a dated transition.} We initialize in 2007, fit the maintained 2023 moments, and keep imposed demographic inputs separate from historical holdouts.
  \item \textbf{The present fit is informative but incomplete.} Several 2023 moments fit well, but housing quantities, the ownership cycle, and the house-price path do not.
  \item \textbf{The equilibrium problem is substantive.} The current fertility response is too weak to restore replacement at any audited positive price, so the closed continuation has no positive terminal steady state.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{The immediate decision is narrow:} either present a finite closed benchmark with an explicit open sensitivity, or re-estimate the housing-cost-to-fertility slope under a closed-root requirement before claiming a transition between steady states.

\smallskip
Policy analysis comes only after this equilibrium choice is settled.
\end{frame}
