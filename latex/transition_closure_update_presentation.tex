\begin{frame}{Today: from the population closure to an equilibrium transition}
\textbf{Last time:} we focused on the missing population closure:
\[
\text{births today}
\longrightarrow \text{new households later}
\longrightarrow \text{housing demand and prices}.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{Today:} I try to make that idea an equilibrium object:
\begin{itemize}
  \item build the simplest model with a well-defined steady state;
  \item separate the immediate response to a fertility-preference shock from the later demographic adjustment;
  \item add the same population accounting to the existing quantitative model;
  \item ask whether the 2007--2023 economy can be interpreted as a point along such a transition.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{The update is small: the same household architecture, a population closure, and one nested first-child housing term.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{A toy economy}
\small
To fix ideas, let's look at a very simplified economy with young and old households:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $Y_t$ is the number of young households at date $t$; $O_t$ is the number of old households.
  \item Total adult-household population is $S_t=Y_t+O_t$.
  \item A young household chooses consumption, housing services, and $n_t$ \textbf{household children}. Next period it becomes old.
  \item In this unitary-household model, each household child becomes one young household in the next generation.
  \item Both generations purchase housing services in the same market at price $p_t$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
The cohort law is therefore
\[
Y_{t+1}=n_tY_t,
\qquad
O_{t+1}=Y_t,
\]
so $n_t=1$ is replacement.

\medskip
\textbf{Focus of the toy model:} fertility, cohort replacement, and the housing-services market. The quantitative model adds tenure choice, mortgages, saving, and bequests.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Household choices and the housing market}
\small
A young household earns $y$ and chooses consumption $c$, housing services $h$, and household children $n$:
\[
u(c,h,n)=c+\alpha\log h+v\log n,
\qquad
c+ph+\big[q+a(p+\omega)\big]n=y.
\]
Here $\alpha$ values adult housing, $v$ values children, and each household child requires $a$ units of housing plus $q$ other goods. The term $\omega$ is the extra cost of accessing child-suitable housing.

\medskip
The interior choices are
\[
n(p)=\frac{v}{q+a(p+\omega)},
\qquad
h_Y(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p}+a n(p).
\]
An old household has utility $c_O+\bar h_O\log h_O$, so its housing demand is $h_O(p)=\bar h_O/p$. Housing supply and market clearing are
\[
H^s(p)=\bar H p^\varepsilon,
\qquad
Y_t h_Y(p_t)+O_t h_O(p_t)=H^s(p_t).
\]
The parameter $\bar H$ scales housing supply and $\varepsilon$ is its price elasticity.

\textbf{A higher housing cost reduces both housing demand and fertility. Cohort sizes then feed back into the same market-clearing price.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Stage 1: the initial steady-state equilibrium}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.53\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../output/model/fertility_population_housing_transition_note/steady_state_equilibrium.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.35\textwidth}
\footnotesize
A steady state is a constant triple $(Y^*,O^*,p^*)$. It requires
\[
n(p^*)=1,
\qquad Y^*=O^*.
\]
Replacement selects
\[
p_0=\frac{v_0-q}{a}-\omega.
\]
Housing clearing then selects $S_0=S^*(p_0)$, where
\[
S^*(p)=
\frac{2\bar H p^{1+\varepsilon}}
{\alpha+\bar h_O+a p}.
\]
Point $A$ is the initial steady state.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vspace{-0.75em}
\small
Along the steady-state schedule, direct differentiation gives
\[
\frac{dS^*}{dp}
=\frac{2\bar H p^\varepsilon
\big[(1+\varepsilon)(\alpha+\bar h_O)+\varepsilon a p\big]}
{(\alpha+\bar h_O+a p)^2}>0
\qquad\text{for }\varepsilon\geq0.
\]
\end{frame}

\small
\textbf{The price response cushions the demographic contraction: falling $p$ raises $n$, and the larger entrant cohorts slow the subsequent fall in both $S$ and $p$.}

\begin{frame}{Below-replacement fertility need not lower population immediately}
Let $n_t=n(p_t)$ be household children per young household and $\mu_t=O_t/Y_t$ the old-to-young cohort ratio. Population can rise with $n_t<1$ whenever
\[
\mu_t<n_t<1.
\]

\begin{block}{A numerical example}
\centering
\Large
$100$ young $+$ $70$ old
$\quad\longrightarrow\quad$
$80$ young $+$ $100$ old

\normalsize
\medskip
$n_t=0.8<1$, but total households rise from $170$ to $180$.
\end{block}

Housing costs can rise as well if the aging cohort retains more housing than is released by the shrinking young cohort:
\[
(1-\mu_t)h_O(p_t)>(1-n_t)h_Y(p_t).
\]

\vspace{-0.35em}
\textbf{U.S. interpretation:} inherited cohorts can keep population and housing costs rising even after fertility falls below replacement.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The quantitative model keeps the existing household problem}
At date $t$, households differ in
\[
\text{age, wealth, income, location, tenure, house size, fertility history, and children at home.}
\]

They make the existing fertility, tenure, housing, saving, and moving choices. Let $G_t$ record the number of households at every combination of these characteristics.

\medskip
The population closure carries that distribution forward in levels:
\[
G_{t+1}
=\text{surviving households after their choices}
+\text{new entrants}.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{The change is that cohort masses now evolve in calendar time instead of being normalized back to a stationary distribution.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Births and the housing market close the aggregate transition}
\small
Two equations connect current choices to future population and current prices:
\[
E_{t+5}=\frac{b_t}{2.1},
\qquad
H^s(p_t)=D(G_t,p_t).
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item Adjusted births $b_t$ generate entrant households twenty years later.
  \item The factor $2.1$ converts child units into future household units.
  \item The price $p_t$ clears housing demand from the current distribution $G_t$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
Within the household problem, the first child can create a discrete housing need:
\[
\bar h(m)=\bar h_1\mathbf{1}\{m>0\}+\bar h_c m,
\]
where $m$ is children at home. The parameter $\bar h_1$ captures the first-child jump, while $\bar h_c$ captures the additional space required per child.

\medskip
\textbf{Housing costs shape fertility; births change future cohort masses; those cohorts feed back into housing costs.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{We calibrate an economy moving from 2007 to 2023}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Construct an old steady state.} Choose the initial fertility preference so completed fertility is $2.1$. This is a replacement normalization, not a measured 2007 target.
  \item \textbf{Construct the 2007 state.} Reweight the old distribution only by observed 2007 age shares, preserving wealth, tenure, and fertility states within each age.
  \item \textbf{Run the economy to 2023.} Feed in Census household totals and ACS age shares. Let the fertility preference decline linearly, and estimate ten household-and-supply parameters and the total preference decline using twelve maintained moments, evaluated mainly in 2023.
  \item \textbf{Check the path.} Compare untargeted model movements with period fertility, birth timing, ownership, rooms, house prices, and rents.
  \item \textbf{Continue after 2023.} Hold preferences fixed and let the inherited household distribution and birth pipeline evolve.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\textbf{The population and age bridge are inputs, not successes of the model. The 2007 economy is a dated initial condition, not literally the old steady state.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The fitted closed economy has no root on the audited grid}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.40\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../output/model/e5f_no_policy_transition_report_jump11_polish_r2_20260818/continuation_closed_stationary_renewal_schedule.pdf}
\par\vspace{-0.35em}{\tiny 25-point grid, $P/P_0\in[10^{-4},3]$; not a global theorem.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.35\textwidth}
\footnotesize
At each stationary price $P$, let $E(P)$ be entrants and $B(P)$ the future households generated by births. A positive closed steady state requires $B(P)/E(P)=1$.

\smallskip
Instead, over the audited grid,
\[
0.557\leq B(P)/E(P)\leq0.708<1.
\]
Even almost free housing does not reproduce the entrant cohort.

\medskip
\textbf{Therefore:} the closed continuation is a finite-horizon experiment, not a transition between two positive steady states.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\footnotesize
Starting from the immediately preceding calibration point, I change both fertility-logit noise scales by a common factor, re-normalize the old economy to replacement, and re-estimate the 2007--2023 preference decline so 2023 completed fertility remains on target.

$0.75$ & $69.0$ & $0.698$ \\
$1.00$ & $38.2$ & $0.713$ \\
$1.25$ & $48.2$ & $0.719$ \\

\textbf{Result:} none of the seven audited factors produces a closed steady-state root. The best transition fit remains near the original slope.

\smallskip
This rules out a simple common rescaling of the existing fertility response. It does not rule out a different fertility mechanism or a different population closure.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Where this leaves the paper}
\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The theoretical contribution is compact.} The existing housing--fertility mechanism is embedded in a population closure. Lower fertility eventually reduces population and housing costs, while inherited cohorts can make both rise initially.
  \item \textbf{The quantitative exercise is a dated transition.} We initialize in 2007, fit the maintained 2023 moments, and keep imposed demographic inputs separate from historical holdouts.
  \item \textbf{The present fit is informative but incomplete.} Several 2023 moments fit well, but housing quantities, the ownership cycle, and the house-price path do not.
  \item \textbf{The equilibrium problem is substantive.} The current fertility response is too weak to restore replacement at any audited positive price, so the closed continuation has no positive terminal steady state.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{The immediate decision is narrow:} present the closed path as a finite-horizon benchmark with an explicit open sensitivity, or change the model and calibration enough to impose a closed-root requirement. Reprofiling the existing fertility slope is not sufficient.

\smallskip
Policy analysis comes only after this equilibrium choice is settled.
\end{frame}
