\section{Housing and Additional Children: Evidence from Sibling Composition in the ACS}
\label{app:acs_iv}

This appendix asks how the housing a young mother occupies differs when she has
an additional child. We use two features of her children that the literature has
used as sources of variation in family size. The first is whether her two oldest
resident children are recorded at the same age, an age-only proxy for a twin
birth. The second is whether her two oldest resident children have the same sex,
which \citet{angrist1998children} show raises the probability of a third birth.
Each delivers a causal effect of the additional child only under assumptions we
state in Section~\ref{app:acs_iv_equations}. Housing is a different outcome from
the labor-market outcomes for which these designs were developed, and for the
sibling-sex design an awkward one: children of the same sex can share a bedroom,
so the composition of a family might change the housing it needs without changing
its size.

The two designs study different families at different margins, and they give
answers of opposite sign. Mothers whose two oldest children are recorded at the
same age live in about a third of a room more than comparable mothers, and are
about three percentage points more likely to own their home. Mothers whose two
oldest children have the same sex live in slightly \emph{fewer} rooms and fewer
bedrooms. Scaled by the first stage, the second design implies that a third child
reduces a family's dwelling by more than a full room. We report this estimate as
it is, set out what would have to be true for it to be an effect of the third
child, and describe the checks we ran on its measurement.

\subsection{Data and the mother sample}
\label{app:acs_iv_data}

The data are the one-year samples of the American Community Survey (ACS) for
2005--2019, covering the fifty states and the District of Columbia, as distributed
by IPUMS USA. Each survey year is a separate cross-section of households. A
person is identified by the survey year, the sample, the household serial number,
and the person's number within the household; a household is identified by the
first three of these. No person is followed across years, so every quantity below
is measured once, at the interview.

The ACS does not ask women how many children they have borne; it asks only whether
a woman gave birth in the past twelve months, and it records no birth histories.
We therefore reconstruct each woman's resident children from the household roster.
IPUMS assigns to each person the person number of a probable mother living in the
same household, using relationship, age, and marital information
\citep{ipumsusa_vardocs}. These links identify social as well as biological
relationships: a stepmother or adoptive mother is linked in the same way as a birth
mother. We keep every link in which the child's age is nonnegative and below the
mother's age. The household relationship variable, which records each person's
relationship to the householder rather than to the mother, cannot separate
biological from other links and is not used to filter them. Among all valid links
in the 2005--2019 records, before any restriction on ages, 91 percent of linked
children are recorded as the householder's child and 4 percent as the
householder's grandchild. Only 1,692 links fail because the recorded child is not
younger than the recorded mother.

For each woman we count her linked resident children, $m_i$, and order them by
age, $a_{i1}\ge a_{i2}\ge\cdots$, where ages are whole years at the interview. The
count $m_i$ is the number of her children living with her, not the number she has
borne. Children who have left home, died, or live with another parent are
missing, and a woman can be linked to a child she did not bear. Following
\citet[pp.~452--453]{angrist1998children}, we limit these errors by studying young
mothers: the base sample consists of women aged 21 to 35 at the interview whose
oldest linked child, if any, is younger than 18. Angrist and Evans additionally
drop mothers whose count of resident children differs from their reported
children ever born and mothers whose children's ages or sexes were imputed by the
Census Bureau. The ACS does not collect children ever born, and our extract does
not carry imputation flags, so neither restriction is applied here; imputed ages
and sexes, if any, enter the instruments as recorded.

Table~\ref{tab:acsiv_sample} reports the samples. The 59,046,776 person records in
the extract fall to 46,373,936 once the sample is limited to the 2005--2019
one-year files. The base sample contains 4,024,238 women living in 3,783,862
households. It includes women with no linked child, who enter neither design.
Because several eligible women can live in one household, household counts are
reported alongside counts of mothers throughout.

\subsection{Housing outcomes}
\label{app:acs_iv_outcomes}

We measure three features of the dwelling, all reported once per household and
assigned to every mother in it. The universe of each is housing units outside group
quarters.

\emph{Rooms} counts whole rooms used for living purposes, including kitchens and
excluding bathrooms, porches, balconies, foyers, halls, and half-rooms. IPUMS codes
valid counts from 1 to 27 and a top category of 30. In the 2009 file, 107 person
records carry the value 28, which the codebook does not define; they are treated as
missing. We cap the count at nine rooms because the Census Bureau imposed a
national top code of nine rooms through the 2007 survey \citep{ipumsusa_vardocs}.
Without the cap, a family's measured size would depend on the survey year in which
it was interviewed.

\emph{Bedrooms} counts rooms that would be listed as bedrooms if the unit were for
sale or rent. The IPUMS code minus one is the number of bedrooms; valid codes run
from 1 (no bedroom) to 22. We cap the count at five, again because a national top
code of five bedrooms applied through 2007. Bedrooms are a component of rooms, but
because the two counts are capped separately, capped rooms minus capped bedrooms is
not a count of the other rooms in the dwelling.

\emph{Ownership} equals one if the unit is owned or being bought, including units
held with a mortgage, and zero if it is rented, including units occupied without
payment of cash rent. Other codes are missing.

Every mother in both estimation samples has a valid tenure code and a valid bedroom
count. One mother in the same-sex sample lacks a valid room count, which is why the
room samples in that design are one observation smaller.

\subsection{The two instruments}
\label{app:acs_iv_instruments}

\paragraph{Same-age oldest pair.}
This design studies the move from one to two resident children among mothers whose
oldest child is young. The sample consists of base-sample women with at least one
linked child whose oldest linked child is aged 0 to 5. The instrument and the
treatment are
\begin{equation}
Z_i^{A}=\mathbf{1}\{m_i\ge 2,\ a_{i1}=a_{i2}\},
\qquad
D_i^{A}=\mathbf{1}\{m_i\ge 2\},
\label{eq:acsiv_twin_def}
\end{equation}
so the instrument indicates that the two oldest resident children are recorded at
the same age and the treatment indicates a second resident child. Every mother with
$Z_i^{A}=1$ has $D_i^{A}=1$ by construction. The event age is the oldest child's
age, $e_i=a_{i1}$. If that child is the mother's first birth, $e_i$ measures the
years since that birth; the data do not confirm that it is.

The instrument is an age-only proxy for a twin birth, not a twin indicator, and we
have not measured what share of same-age pairs are twins. Ages are recorded in whole
years and our extract carries no quarter of birth, so two children born within a
year of each other can share an age at the interview, and a step-sibling or adopted
child of the same age produces the same record. \citet[p.~454]{angrist1998children}
measure the resulting error in the 1980 Census: defining twins by age in years
alone yields a second-birth twin rate 35 percent higher than defining them by age
and quarter of birth, and for that reason they restrict their twins analysis to
1980. In our sample 1.9 percent of eligible mothers have a same-age pair, and 388
have three oldest children of the same age. Twin births are themselves not
random: \citet[p.~775]{angrist2010multiple} note that twinning varies with maternal
age and race and that twin births change child spacing and child health.

\paragraph{Same-sex oldest pair.}
This design studies the move from two to three resident children. The sample
consists of base-sample women with at least two linked children whose two oldest
children have different ages, whose second-oldest child is aged 0 to 5, and whose
two oldest children both have a recorded sex. The instrument and treatment are
\begin{equation}
Z_i^{B}=\mathbf{1}\{s_{i1}=s_{i2}\},
\qquad
D_i^{B}=\mathbf{1}\{m_i\ge 3\},
\label{eq:acsiv_samesex_def}
\end{equation}
where $s_{ik}$ is the sex of the $k$th oldest linked child. The event age is the
second child's age, $e_i=a_{i2}$, which proxies for the years since the second
birth, when the instrument is realized. The requirement that the two oldest
children differ in age removes the mothers who drive the first design and makes
the order of the first two children well defined. The sample is not restricted to
mothers with exactly two children and does not condition on the treatment. When the
second and third children share an age, the child labeled second is arbitrary;
Section~\ref{app:acs_iv_diagnostics} drops these mothers.

The instrument predicts a third resident child, and its first stage is positive:
mothers of two boys or two girls are more likely to have another child. Because the
instrument is realized at the second birth, no event age in this design precedes
the instrument, and event age 0 cannot serve as a period before exposure.

\subsection{Estimating equations and inference}
\label{app:acs_iv_equations}

For each design, outcome $Y_i$, and window of event ages, we estimate a reduced form
and a first stage on the same mothers:
\begin{equation}
Y_i=\delta Z_i+W_i'\gamma+u_i,
\qquad
D_i=\pi Z_i+W_i'\kappa+v_i .
\label{eq:acsiv_rf_fs}
\end{equation}
The reduced-form coefficient $\delta$ is the difference in housing between mothers
with and without the sibling feature, and $\pi$ is the corresponding difference in
the probability of the additional child. The controls $W_i$ are complete sets of
indicators for the mother's age at the event (her age at the interview minus
$e_i$), her race, the survey year, and the event age. We do not control for the
mother's current marital status, education, or state of residence, all of which can
respond to fertility; \citet[p.~465, footnote~12]{angrist1998children} exclude
education and husband's earnings for the same reason. Regressions are weighted by
the mother's ACS person weight. Standard errors are clustered by household, and each
outcome is estimated on the mothers for whom it is observed.

With one instrument and one endogenous variable, two-stage least squares is the
ratio of the two coefficients:
\begin{equation}
\hat\beta=\hat\delta/\hat\pi .
\label{eq:acsiv_ratio}
\end{equation}
The ratio is a causal effect under three assumptions: the instrument is as good as
randomly assigned given $W_i$; it affects housing only through the additional
child; and it moves every mother's fertility in the same direction. Under them,
$\beta$ is the average effect of the additional child for mothers whose fertility
responds to the instrument. For the first design these are mothers who, without a
same-age pair, would have one resident child at that event age. For the second they
are mothers who have a third child because their first two share a sex. The two
groups differ in age, in family size, and in the margin they cross, so the two
estimates answer different questions. We do not test these assumptions, and no
sign restriction on the housing effect is imposed or tested.

The baseline does not include separate indicators for the sex of the first and
second child. \citet[pp.~460--461]{angrist1998children} add them because the
same-sex indicator is an interaction of the two children's sexes: with slightly
more boys than girls born, it is mildly correlated with each child's sex, and a
child's sex may affect parents for reasons unrelated to family size. They report
that their estimates are virtually unchanged by these controls (pp.~466--467).
Section~\ref{app:acs_iv_diagnostics} reports the same check.

Confidence intervals for the reduced form, first stage, and two-stage estimate are
the estimate plus or minus 1.96 clustered standard errors. The first-stage $F$
statistic is the household-clustered Wald statistic
$(\hat\pi/\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\pi))^2$. With a single instrument and a single
endogenous variable, this is the statistic to which the Kleibergen--Paap robust
$F$ reduces \citep[pp.~16--17]{andrews2018weak}.

A large first stage protects against weak-instrument bias. It says nothing about
the exclusion restriction. Because inference on $\hat\beta$ can still be unreliable
when the first stage is imprecise, we also report the Anderson--Rubin test
\citep{anderson1949estimation}, which \citet[p.~3]{andrews2018weak} recommend
whenever there is a single instrument. The hypothesis $\beta=\beta_0$ says that, net
of the controls, the instrument has no effect on $Y_i-\beta_0D_i$. Its coefficient
on that variable is $\hat\delta-\beta_0\hat\pi$, and the test statistic is
\begin{equation}
AR(\beta_0)=\frac{(\hat\delta-\beta_0\hat\pi)^2}
{\hat V_\delta-2\beta_0\hat C_{\delta\pi}+\beta_0^2\hat V_\pi},
\label{eq:acsiv_ar}
\end{equation}
where $\hat V_\delta$, $\hat V_\pi$, and $\hat C_{\delta\pi}$ are the clustered
variances and covariance of the reduced-form and first-stage coefficients. The
statistic is compared with the 5 percent critical value of an $F$ distribution with
one numerator degree of freedom and the denominator degrees of freedom of the
clustered regressions. The size of this test does not depend on the strength of the
instrument.

We evaluate the test on a finite grid of candidate values: from $-3$ to $3$ in
steps of $0.1$ for rooms and bedrooms, and from $-50$ to $50$ percentage points in
steps of $2$ for ownership. The tables report the smallest and largest grid values
that are not rejected. In every cell with at least one accepted value, the accepted
grid values form one unbroken run. Values between grid points were not evaluated,
so the reported ranges describe the grid, not the exact endpoints of a continuous
confidence set. A single entry in braces means that one tested value was accepted
and its neighbors on the grid were rejected; it is not a zero-width interval.
``None'' means that no tested value was accepted.

\paragraph{Same-age oldest pair.}
The first stage is large, 66 percentage points, because a same-age pair guarantees
a second child: the coefficient is approximately one minus the probability that a
comparable mother without such a pair already has a second resident child. Mothers
with a same-age pair live in 0.33 more rooms (standard error 0.017) and 0.22 more
bedrooms, and they are 3.1 percentage points more likely to own. Scaled by the first
stage, a second child is associated with half a room, a third of a bedroom, and a
4.7-point higher ownership rate. The first stage falls from 52 points at event age
3 to 35 points at event age 5, as more mothers without a same-age pair have a second
child, while the room and bedroom reduced forms change little. The two-stage
estimates therefore rise with the event age, to 1.1 rooms and 15 points of
ownership at age 5. At event age 3 the ownership reduced form is 1.6 points and its
confidence interval includes zero. These magnitudes carry the qualifications
above: the proxy mixes twins with closely spaced and blended sibling pairs in an
unmeasured proportion, and twin births are associated with maternal
characteristics that may affect housing directly.

\paragraph{Same-sex oldest pair.}
The first stage is 3.3 percentage points pooled over event ages 0 to 5 and rises to
4.7 points at event age 3 and 6.0 points at event age 5. With more than 650,000
mothers it is precisely estimated, with an $F$ statistic of 715. The pooled figure
is smaller than the 6.2 points that \citet[p.~461]{angrist1998children} estimate in
the 1980 Census, but the samples differ: ours ends five years after the second
child's birth, whereas theirs includes mothers whose second child is up to
seventeen, and the two censuses are a generation apart. Mothers whose two oldest
children share a sex live in 0.038 fewer rooms (standard error 0.005) and 0.034
fewer bedrooms (0.003), and they are 0.25 percentage points less likely to own, a
difference whose confidence interval includes zero. The room and bedroom
differences are close in magnitude, but because the two counts are capped
separately we do not read their difference as a statement about other rooms, and we
do not test them against each other.

The two-stage estimate for rooms is $-1.15$, and the Anderson--Rubin test accepts
grid values from $-1.4$ to $-0.9$. It is large because the reduced form, though
small, is divided by a first stage of 0.033: $-0.0376/0.0327=-1.15$. Read under the
assumptions of Section~\ref{app:acs_iv_equations}, a third child reduces the family's
dwelling by more than a full room. The data alone cannot tell this reading apart
from a failure of the exclusion restriction. A genuine negative effect is possible
if a larger family has less to spend on housing, or delays a move it would otherwise
make. Alternatively, sibling sex may affect housing directly. Under the exclusion
restriction the reduced form equals $\pi\beta$. Any direct effect of sibling sex on
housing adds to the reduced form in full while leaving the first stage unchanged,
so it enters the two-stage estimate multiplied by $1/\pi$, about 31.
\citet{jones2015exclusion} shows how direct effects on mothers whose fertility does
not respond to the instrument bias the instrumental-variable estimand. Bedrooms are
a natural candidate channel, since two children of the same sex can share a bedroom
more easily than a boy and a girl. \citet[p.~775]{angrist2010multiple} raise
economies of scale through room or clothes sharing as a potential violation of the
same-sex exclusion restriction, attributing the concern to
\citet{rosenzweig2000natural}. In Israeli subsamples in which sex composition does
not predict family size, they find no reduced-form relation between sex composition
and their outcomes, which concern the children's schooling and later lives rather
than housing (p.~799). A negative bedroom reduced form is consistent with the
room-sharing hypothesis, but it does not identify it.

\subsection{Checks on the same-sex design}
\label{app:acs_iv_diagnostics}

We ran four checks on the same-sex estimates, all on the estimation sample and with
the specification of equation~\eqref{eq:acsiv_rf_fs} unless stated. They ask
whether the negative housing difference depends on how the instrument is coded,
which sibling compositions carry it, and when it appears after the second child's
birth. They check coding, measurement, and sensitivity. None of them tests the
exclusion restriction, and none certifies a causal reading in either direction.
Table~\ref{tab:acsiv_diag_specs} and Figure~\ref{fig:acsiv_diag_specs} report the
pooled estimates; Table~\ref{tab:acsiv_diag_ages} and
Figure~\ref{fig:acsiv_diag_ages} report them by the age of the second child;
Table~\ref{tab:acsiv_diag_cells} reports unadjusted means.

\paragraph{Sex of each child.}
Adding separate indicators for a boy first and a boy second, as
\citet{angrist1998children} do, leaves the estimates essentially unchanged. The room
reduced form moves from $-0.0376$ to $-0.0380$, the bedroom reduced form from
$-0.0341$ to $-0.0342$, and the first stage stays at 3.27 percentage points.

\paragraph{Tied second and third children.}
In 11,500 estimation-sample mothers the second and third linked children are
recorded at the same age, so which of them is labeled second, and therefore the
instrument and the event age, is arbitrary. Dropping these mothers also leaves the
estimates essentially unchanged: $-0.0378$ for rooms, $-0.0347$ for bedrooms, and a
first stage of 3.27 points on 645,486 mothers.

\paragraph{Two boys and two girls.}
Replacing the same-sex indicator with separate indicators for two boys and two
girls, both measured against the 327,012 mothers of one boy and one girl, shows
that both compositions carry the negative difference. For rooms the coefficients
are $-0.0306$ (standard error 0.0061) for two boys and $-0.0454$ (0.0063) for two
girls; for bedrooms they are $-0.0322$ (0.0033) and $-0.0363$ (0.0035). The first
stages are very similar, 3.28 and 3.27 points. The difference between the two boys
and two girls coefficients, with a standard error from their joint covariance, is
$0.0148$ rooms (0.0072), with a 95 percent confidence interval of $[0.0006,0.0289]$.
For bedrooms it is $0.0041$ (0.0039) and for ownership $0.09$ points (0.21), both
with intervals that include zero. The room difference is one of several contrasts
we examined, is not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and lies close to the
conventional threshold; we do not draw conclusions from it.

\paragraph{Age of the second child.}
At event age 0 the first stage is essentially zero, 0.09 percentage points
($F=0.9$), and the room and bedroom reduced forms are small, with confidence
intervals that include zero. This is the first year of exposure to the instrument,
not a period before it. The instrument has not yet shifted fertility, and little
time has passed for housing to adjust, so the small differences are not evidence
that sibling sex has no direct effect. By event age 1 the bedroom reduced form is already $-0.0278$
(0.0066), while the first stage is 1.20 points. From age 1 to age 5 the first stage
rises about fivefold, to 6.04 points, whereas the bedroom reduced form rises about
1.6-fold, to $-0.0450$. With a constant effect of the third child and no direct
effect, the two would move proportionally across ages. We read the gap only
descriptively. Each event age is a different cross-section of mothers, not the
same families followed over time, and both the composition of mothers and the
effect of a third child may differ across ages. The ownership reduced form is
imprecise at every age, and every one of its confidence intervals includes zero.

\paragraph{Unadjusted means.}
Table~\ref{tab:acsiv_diag_cells} reports the number of mothers and the weighted and
unweighted means of the treatment and the three outcomes for each of the four sex
compositions. The ordering matches the regressions. Mothers of two boys or two
girls are more likely to have a third resident child (0.245 and 0.246, weighted)
than mothers of a boy and a girl (0.212 and 0.214). They also live in slightly
fewer rooms and bedrooms. Ownership rates differ little across the four groups.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The two designs measure different objects, and their estimates should not be
averaged or set against each other. Under its assumptions, the same-age design
says that a second young child is associated with more space and a higher
likelihood of owning. The same-sex design yields a negative estimate for a third
child, and the checks show that the negative difference does not come from how the
sex of each child, tied ages, or a single sex composition is handled. Whether it
reflects an effect of the third child or a direct effect of sibling composition on
housing is not identified by these data.
