\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Doubling the rebated annual property-tax rate from 1 to 2 percent produces a substantial housing response in the E5f model but only a modest fertility response. Asset prices fall by 3--5 percent, ownership falls by 2--3 percentage points, and housing services per household fall by 6--8 percent. Annual births per household head rise by only 0.2--0.6 percent. An exact state-level decomposition shows that less than half of the peak fertility response comes from higher birth probabilities for otherwise comparable households; the rest comes from changes in the distribution of households across states. The central problem is not that housing is frozen. It is that the reform does not reproduce the child-relevant reallocation emphasized by Coven et al.: young childless households can become owners, but they consume smaller homes, while young parents lose both ownership and space. The current calibration also leaves prospective parents far from both the child-space floor and the fertility cutoff. The evidence therefore points to a combination of an incomplete housing-allocation first stage and a weakly identified housing-to-fertility transmission, rather than the absence of any theoretical link.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.4em}
\noindent\colorbox{accent!8}{\parbox{0.965\textwidth}{
\textbf{Main conclusion.} The model contains the intended housing--fertility mechanism and gives it the expected sign, but the current policy experiment does not deliver more family-sized housing to prospective parents. A positive tenure-choice taste smooths the numerics and changes incidence, but it cannot by itself supply the missing capitalization, financing, spatial, or family-housing margins.

\section{The result in plain terms}

The reform is not a null experiment. Doubling the property tax changes the housing equilibrium materially. It lowers the purchase price of housing, raises the annual cost of occupying it, reduces ownership, and induces households to consume less space. Fertility nevertheless rises by less than one percent throughout the transition.

The combination matters. A lower purchase price is helpful to a wealth-constrained buyer because it lowers the required down payment. But the same tax raises the recurring user cost of housing. In the present equilibrium, the recurring-cost effect is strong enough that housing consumption contracts for every young group inspected. The government rebates the extra revenue equally across households, creating a broad income effect that raises fertility slightly. The result is therefore a small positive net birth response, not a large ``cheaper houses permit children'' effect.

The diagnosis has three layers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Housing moves, but not in the family-relevant direction.} Aggregate ownership falls by 2--3 percentage points and housing services fall by 6--8 percent. Some childless young households become owners, but they own smaller bundles. Young parents lose both ownership and housing.
  \item \textbf{The within-household fertility response is positive but small.} At the peak aggregate response, only 0.258 percentage point comes from changes in birth behavior for comparable households; 0.347 point comes from changes in population composition.
  \item \textbf{The calibration supplies little local amplification.} The model underpredicts the increase in rooms after a first birth, overpredicts average rooms, places no positive-mass young-parent state at the child-space floor, and places almost no mass near birth indifference.
\end{enumerate}

This is partly a quantitative calibration problem and partly a structural first-stage problem. The model does not yet reproduce the allocation mechanism that makes the Coven et al. result powerful: a sufficiently large price capitalization, combined with binding financial constraints and age-specific release of owner housing, reallocates housing toward young constrained households.

\section{Experiment and numerical status}

The experiment compares two deterministic perfect-foresight paths beginning from the same reconstructed 2023 household and person distributions. The baseline has a rebated 1 percent annual property tax; the reform raises the rate to 2 percent. Both paths contain 128 four-year dates, from 2023 through 2531, use the same terminal demographic closure, and impose a housing-supply elasticity of 1.75.

Tenure choice after 2023 is smoothed with an extreme-value taste scale of
\[
\kappa_{\mathrm{tenure}}=0.0100175.
\]
This value was estimated in the older M5 calibration. It was not jointly estimated in E5f, whose maintained calibration sets the tenure taste scale to zero. The exercise is therefore a conditional sensitivity: it asks how the E5f transition behaves after adding a plausible amount of tenure smoothing while holding the remaining E5f parameters fixed.

Both policy paths pass the official equilibrium and accounting gates. The maximum market and fiscal residuals are, respectively, $1.4942\times10^{-4}$ and $1.4131\times10^{-5}$ under the 1 percent tax, and $1.1567\times10^{-4}$ and $1.6511\times10^{-5}$ under the 2 percent tax. These lie below the maintained thresholds of $2\times10^{-4}$ and $2.5\times10^{-5}$. History reproduction, feasibility, probability, accounting, and H128 terminal checks also pass. The results below are therefore interpretable equilibrium sensitivities, although they are not a production counterfactual from a fully recalibrated positive-tenure-taste model.

\section{Exact decomposition of the fertility response}

Let $s$ denote the complete state of an at-risk household: liquid wealth, origin tenure, age, income, parity, and the number and stage of children at home. Let $w_s^j$ be the mass of state $s$ per household head and $p_s^j$ be expected child units produced during the four-year model period, where $j=0$ denotes the 1 percent path and $j=1$ the 2 percent path. Annual births per household head are
\begin{equation}
F^j=\frac{1}{4}\sum_s w_s^j p_s^j.
\label{eq:births}
\end{equation}
The factor $1/4$ converts four-year birth units to an annual flow.

The exact symmetric two-factor decomposition is
\begin{align}
F^1-F^0
={}&\underbrace{\frac{1}{8}\sum_s(w_s^0+w_s^1)(p_s^1-p_s^0)}_{\text{within-state birth behavior}}
\\[-0.2em]
&+\underbrace{\frac{1}{8}\sum_s(p_s^0+p_s^1)(w_s^1-w_s^0)}_{\text{distribution and composition}}.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{align}
The first term asks whether an otherwise comparable household becomes more or less likely to have a child. The second asks whether the reform moves households toward states that already have higher or lower fertility. Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp} is an exact accounting identity, not a claim that its two components are independently identified causal mechanisms.

In 2079, ages 25--34 contribute 0.338 percentage point of the aggregate response, ages 35--44 contribute 0.176 point, and households under 25 contribute 0.091 point. These are contributions to the aggregate birth rate, not group-specific percentage changes.

\section{The missing family-housing first stage}

The most important diagnostic is the incidence of ownership and housing services among young households. By 2079, ownership among childless households aged 25--34 rises by 2.379 percentage points. Taken alone, this resembles the young-buyer channel in Coven et al. But housing services for this group fall by 7.437 percent. Among households aged 25--34 with dependent children, ownership falls by 1.081 percentage points and housing services fall by 6.200 percent. Among parents aged 35--44, ownership falls by 2.943 points and housing services fall by 6.590 percent.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\figroot/young_household_incidence.pdf}
\caption{Ownership and housing-service incidence among young households}

The reform can therefore move a young household from renting to owning while reducing the amount of housing it consumes. It reallocates tenure, but not family-sized space. That distinction is central: ownership is not itself the fertility input in the model. Housing services and the difference in housing needs between the birth and no-birth branches are what matter for fertility.

The state tables also show very little local pressure at the modeled family-space constraint. No positive-mass young-parent cell binds the child-space floor in either policy at any inspected date. No positive-mass at-risk state lies within 0.05 value units of birth indifference. Only 2.3--2.6 percent of at-risk mass lies within 0.25 units, and roughly 13--14 percent lies within 0.50 units.

The model therefore has two forms of slack at once. Prospective parents can reduce housing without reaching the child-space floor, and most are not close enough to birth indifference for a moderate change in the fertility value gap to switch much probability mass.

\section{Comparison with Coven et al.}

Coven, Golder, Gupta, and Ndiaye study how property taxes reallocate housing across generations under financial constraints. Their August 2026 paper does not contain endogenous fertility. Their phrase ``young families'' refers to young households facing housing-market constraints, not households choosing whether to have a child. Their contribution supplies a housing-allocation first stage that a fertility model can build upon.

Their mechanism has two complementary parts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Capitalization and down payments.} A higher recurring property tax lowers the purchase price. With an 80 percent loan-to-value ceiling, the required 20 percent equity payment falls with the price, helping low-wealth young buyers.
  \item \textbf{Recurring costs and old-owner release.} Higher property-tax payments increase the cost of remaining in a large owner-occupied home. A payment-to-income constraint, transaction costs, mortgages, capital-gains treatment, and bequest motives generate age-specific downsizing and release of owner housing.
\end{enumerate}

The full allocation result needs both forces. Coven et al. show that imposing only the counterfactual price and rent paths, while keeping the tax system fixed, raises aggregate ownership sharply but disproportionately among older households. Capitalization alone does not produce the desired generational reallocation.

Four differences explain why E5f currently delivers a weaker and less family-relevant first stage.

\textbf{Capitalization is smaller.} The supply elasticity of 1.75 makes the stock response larger and the asset-price response smaller than in the California calibration. A 3--5 percent price decline supplies much less down-payment relief than a 12.6 percent decline.

\textbf{The payment-to-income constraint is inactive.} E5f includes a 20 percent down payment because the financed share is 0.8, but the payment-to-income test is switched off. A higher property tax therefore cannot mechanically force a cash-flow-constrained older owner to downsize.

\textbf{Owner and renter space are close substitutes.} Households can obtain housing services as renters, and ownership has no child-specific service such as residential stability, school access, control over the dwelling, or reduced displacement risk. A tenure change need not relax the housing constraint relevant for family formation.

\textbf{The spatial sorting margin is absent.} Coven et al. emphasize migration and the geographic reallocation of younger and older households. A one-market transition cannot move prospective parents toward places where high taxes release larger owner homes.

\section{Why housing can move strongly while fertility moves little}

The fertility decision compares two optimized indirect values: the value of attempting a birth and the value of waiting. Housing prices, rents, wealth, and transfers affect both branches. Fertility responds to the difference between their exposures, not to the level of housing demand by itself.

This point is easiest to see through the envelope logic. If the attempt and wait branches choose similar interior housing bundles, a common increase in housing cost lowers both values by nearly the same amount. The common component cancels from the birth value gap. Housing becomes a first-order fertility margin when a child generates a sufficiently large extra need for space, when one branch is constrained and the other is not, or when tenure delivers a child-specific service.

The current calibration offers little of this local amplification. The modeled first-child housing floor is
\[
\bar h_1=0.19945+0.31527=0.51471
\]
room-equivalent units. Young parents consume roughly 5 units as renters and 8--9 as owners. The child-space increment still costs resources away from the floor, but it is small relative to the baseline bundle and never binds for a positive-mass young-parent group in the inspected paths.

The equal rebate adds another offset. The property-tax rate rises, the annual housing user cost rises, and housing contracts; at the same time, the additional revenue is returned equally to households. An earlier fixed-distribution diagnostic found the same qualitative signs: the tax-rate component lowered births, modest asset-price relief raised them slightly, and the equal rebate raised them enough to leave a small positive net effect. The dynamic decomposition presented here confirms that the final result is an equilibrium mixture rather than a pure affordability response.

\section{Calibration and identification}

The active E5f transition calibration has loss 36.099 under 12 dated targets and 11 free parameters. The two largest misses are precisely the moments most relevant for the proposed mechanism. The model predicts a 0.380-room increase after a first birth against a target of 0.720 rooms, and average occupied rooms of 6.710 against a target of 5.780. These two rows contribute 26.304 of the 36.099 objective points.

The first miss says that the model under-delivers the observed increase in space associated with family formation. The second says that households begin with too much housing on average. Together they make it easy to absorb a policy-induced housing contraction without strongly changing birth incentives.

There is also a deeper identification issue. The target set includes fertility levels and timing, housing responses to births, ownership, wealth, and bequests. It does not include a moment measuring fertility responses to plausibly exogenous variation in rents, house prices, down-payment requirements, housing supply, or property taxes. The policy elasticity of fertility with respect to housing costs is therefore model-implied rather than directly disciplined by the data.

This is not merely a semantic distinction. ``Housing after a birth'' and ``births after a housing-cost shock'' are different derivatives. A model can fit the former and still imply a small latter response if households substitute across tenure, housing size, consumption, and saving. Here the model does not yet fit the former well either.

\section{What positive tenure taste can and cannot accomplish}

Restoring a positive tenure-choice taste is economically and numerically sensible. Deterministic tenure choice creates discontinuous policy mappings when a positive-mass state crosses an owner--renter cutoff. The positive taste scale smooths this margin and produced clean, convergent perfect-foresight paths.

Jointly estimating the scale inside E5f could alter the mass of households near tenure margins and therefore change age-specific incidence. It should be part of a serious recalibration. But it is not a fertility mechanism by itself. It does not create a payment-to-income constraint, a spatial migration margin, segmented owner and rental stocks, stronger capitalization, or a child-specific value of tenure. Nor does it identify the response of births to housing costs.

The appropriate prior is therefore modest: joint positive-taste recalibration may move the result meaningfully and make it more stable, but it is unlikely by itself to transform a 0.2--0.6 percent fertility response into a several-percent response. Such a change would require a stronger first stage for young family housing, a stronger empirically disciplined housing-to-fertility transmission, or both.

\section{A disciplined next sequence}

The evidence suggests a sequential research strategy rather than one broad recalibration.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Establish the housing first stage.} Run bounded structural sensitivities for the housing-supply elasticity, an explicitly calibrated payment-to-income constraint, and owner--renter market segmentation. Report age-specific ownership and housing services, not only aggregate ownership.
  \item \textbf{Repair the family-housing fit.} Re-estimate the child-housing block so that the first-birth room response is not missed by 0.34 rooms and mean rooms are not high by 0.93 rooms. The identifying contract must remain at least as rich as the free parameter vector.
  \item \textbf{Add a reverse-link moment.} Discipline the fertility response to housing using plausibly exogenous variation in rents, prices, supply, down-payment relief, or property taxes. Without such a moment, a large fertility effect would be an assumption generated by the model architecture rather than an estimated elasticity.
  \item \textbf{Re-estimate tenure smoothing jointly.} Estimate the positive tenure taste with the revised E5f target system and report its covariance with the ownership premium, child-space parameters, and wealth process.
  \item \textbf{Add new theory only if the evidence requires it.} If prospective parents gain owner-occupied housing services and fertility still barely responds, then child-specific tenure services---stability, schools, landlord restrictions, displacement risk, or control over space---become serious candidates. Each should enter with a corresponding empirical moment.
\end{enumerate}

The first two steps separate the two diagnoses cleanly. If a stronger Coven-style housing reallocation reaches young parents but births remain insensitive, the fertility block is the bottleneck. If the reallocation still does not reach them, the missing object is the housing-market architecture.

\section{Implication for the paper}

The current result is not that housing is irrelevant for fertility. It is that housing affects fertility only when the households receiving child-relevant space are also near a birth margin. The property-tax reform studied here lowers asset prices, but it also raises recurring housing costs, contracts housing services, and rebates revenue broadly. The lower price is not large enough, and the financing and spatial margins are not rich enough, to reproduce the reallocation toward young constrained households emphasized by Coven et al.

That distinction creates a potentially strong paper contribution. Coven et al. establish an age reallocation of housing, not a fertility effect. A housing--fertility model can show when such reallocation reaches prospective parents, whether it relaxes the relevant housing margin, and how much family formation follows. The present calibration says that this transmission is small under the current closure. Because the calibration underfits first-birth housing adjustment and does not directly identify the housing-cost elasticity of fertility, this is a diagnostic result rather than the final quantitative conclusion.

\section{Reproducibility and interpretation}
