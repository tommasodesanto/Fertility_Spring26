\begin{center}
{\Large Housing Allocation and Fertility}\par
\smallskip
{\large An illustrative two-period model}\par
\smallskip
{\small Discussion draft for Tommaso De Santo \quad September 10, 2026}
\end{center}
\medskip

Young households need space for children, but may be unable to finance a large
home. Renting provides an alternative only up to the largest rental available.
We ask whether these two restrictions leave too little housing with the young,
and whether reallocating housing can increase fertility.

The comparison is between a competitive allocation and a planner who chooses
all current consumption and housing, giving each living household equal
weight. The planner can relax private financing restrictions. We first hold
fertility fixed, solve for the entire allocation, and give sufficient
conditions for housing to move toward the young. We then allow fertility to
respond. Both results permit the planner to give the young more housing while
taking some consumption from them. The direction of the housing transfer
requires a restriction on relative resources; having children alone does not
establish it.\footnote{This draft develops the proposed model with interest
paid during each age, a common positive retirement income, and the same adult
housing weight at both ages. Housing comes from a common stock, with larger
units available through ownership. Property taxes finance public services;
entering households' endowments are independent of estates. These assumptions
should be assessed together with the results.}

\section{Environment}

\textbf{Households.} Households live for two periods, young and old. A young
household has resources $w>0$, distributed according to $F$, and receives a
common income $y>0$ upon entering old age. Resources $w$ include income and
liquid wealth; all are available for consumption, housing, and saving. The
same distribution $F$ enters each cohort. Future income is known. Resource
and utility averages are assumed finite.

\textbf{Preferences.} A young household chooses consumption expenditure
$c^y$, housing $h^y$, and completed fertility $n>0$. Children require $\chi>0$
units of goods and $\kappa>0$ units of housing each. Utility at the two ages is:
\begin{align}
u^y(c^y,h^y,n)
 &=\log(c^y-\chi n)+\alpha\log(h^y-\kappa n)+\vartheta\log n,
 \label{eq:new_uy}\\
u^o(c^o,h^o,e)
 &=\log c^o+\alpha\log h^o+\omega_B\log e.
 \label{eq:new_uo}
\end{align}
Here $\alpha,\vartheta,\omega_B>0$, and the arguments of all logarithms are
positive. Households discount future utility at $\beta>0$. The estate $e$ is
financial wealth plus the proceeds from selling the house at death, net of
mortgage repayment. Parents value this estate directly. Entering households'
resources are specified independently of the estates left by their parents.

\textbf{Housing.} A fixed, divisible housing stock $\bar H>0$ supplies both
owned and rented homes. Rentals have maximum size $r>0$; owned homes can have
any size. Households choose tenure at each age. Converting between the two
uses does not consume resources, so the stock is common rather than divided
into separately fixed owner and rental stocks.

\textbf{Finance and taxes.} Goods and financial claims trade with the rest
of the world. The price of a claim to one unit of goods at the end of an age
is $q\in(0,1)$. At stationary prices, a unit of housing sells for $P>0$ and
owners pay property tax $\tau P$, with $\tau>0$. Competitive landlords charge
current rent:
\begin{equation}
p=(1+\tau-q)P.
\label{eq:new_usercost}
\end{equation}
Rent covers the purchase and tax cost less the discounted resale proceeds.
The return $1/q$ applies over one model period. Property-tax revenue finances
public services; their provision is held fixed in the allocation comparison.

A mortgage finances at most a share $\phi\in(0,1)$ of the purchase price.
The borrower pays interest during the age and repays principal when selling
the house at its end. The same secured borrowing rule applies at both ages.
Financial saving is nonnegative; borrowing requires housing collateral.
Old households can sell their previous home and choose its replacement.

\textbf{Demography.} There are $Y_t$ young and $O_t$ old households. Each
child produces $\nu>0$ entering households after survival and household
formation. If $\bar n_t$ is mean fertility, cohort masses evolve as:
\begin{equation}
Y_{t+1}=\nu\bar n_tY_t,\qquad O_{t+1}=Y_t.
\label{eq:new_demography}
\end{equation}
The stationary comparison below has equal young and old cohort masses.

\section{Household choices}

\subsection{Budgets and mortgage payments}

Let $z$ be total resources upon entering old age, including retirement income.
For an owner, let $L$ be mortgage principal and $k$ financial saving. Since
principal $L$ is repaid at sale, the current value of its interest payments
is $(1-q)L$. The purchase and current expenditure budget is:
\begin{equation}
c+(1+\tau)Ph+k+(1-q)L=w+L,
\qquad k\ge0,\quad 0\le L\le\phi Ph.
\label{eq:mortgage_cash}
\end{equation}
The household finances the deposit, consumption, tax, and interest from its
current resources. This is an interest-serviced mortgage, rather than a
mortgage whose interest is added to principal until retirement.

Define the net financial position $a=k-qL$. The owner's current budget and
retirement resources become:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
c+(1+\tau)Ph+a&=w,\\
z&=y+a/q+Ph,\\
a&\ge-q\phi Ph.
\end{split}
\label{eq:new_young_owner}
\end{equation}
The house sale repays the unchanged mortgage principal and releases the
remaining equity. A renter instead has $c+ph+a=w$ and $z=y+a/q$, with
$a\ge0$ and $h\le r$.

Eliminating $a$ gives the same lifetime budget in both tenures:
\begin{equation}
c+ph+qz=w+qy.
\label{eq:unified_budget}
\end{equation}
The difference is which combinations of housing and retirement resources are
available. Define the positive coefficient
$\Gamma=q(1-\phi)/(1+\tau-q)$. The two financing restrictions are:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{lll}
\text{Renting:}&z\ge y,&h\le r,\\[2pt]
\text{Owning:}&q(z-y)\ge\Gamma ph.&
\end{array}
\label{eq:tenure_constraints}
\end{equation}
For an owner, the last condition is equivalently
$z-y\ge(1-\phi)Ph$: the equity left in the house at sale must cover the
unfinanced share of its price. Retirement income can pay for old-age
consumption but cannot be pledged as unsecured borrowing when young.

\subsection{The old household's problem}

An old household enters with resources $z^o$, after receiving income and
settling its previous financial and housing positions. It chooses current
consumption, housing, and its net estate. Its budget is:
\begin{equation}
c^o+ph^o+qe=z^o.
\label{eq:old_budget}
\end{equation}
For an owner, the estate satisfies $e=a^o/q+Ph^o$ with
$a^o\ge-q\phi Ph^o$; for a renter, $e=a^o/q$ with $a^o\ge0$.
Thus the old household solves:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
V^o(z^o)=\max_{c^o,h^o,e}\;&\log c^o+\alpha\log h^o+\omega_B\log e,\\
\text{subject to }&c^o+ph^o+qe=z^o,\\
&\left\{h^o\le r,\ e\ge0\right\}
\quad\text{or}\quad\left\{qe\ge\Gamma ph^o\right\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:new_old_owner}
\end{equation}
The alternatives are renting and owning. Estate utility does not describe
consumption after death: it is the household's valuation, while alive, of
what it leaves.

Write $K=1+\alpha+\omega_B$. Throughout the results, assume:
\begin{equation}
\omega_B\ge\alpha\Gamma.
\tag{A1}\label{ass:old}
\end{equation}
Under this condition the old household's desired estate is large enough to
satisfy the equity requirement on its desired home. Its solution is:
\begin{equation}
c^o=\frac{z^o}{K},\qquad
h^o=\frac{\alpha z^o}{Kp},\qquad
qe=\frac{\omega_B z^o}{K}.
\label{eq:old_solution}
\end{equation}
If the home exceeds $r$, ownership implements this allocation. Otherwise
renting does as well. The housing choice is therefore unrestricted in real
terms, and $V^o(z)=K\log z$ plus a term depending only on prices. Equality
in \eqref{ass:old} is allowed.

\subsection{The young household's problem}

A young household chooses fertility, current consumption and housing, and
resources for retirement, taking prices as given:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\max_{c,h,n,z}\;&\log(c-\chi n)+\alpha\log(h-\kappa n)
                  +\vartheta\log n+\beta V^o(z),\\
\text{subject to }&c+ph+qz=w+qy,\\
&\left\{z\ge y,\ h\le r\right\}
\quad\text{or}\quad\left\{q(z-y)\ge\Gamma ph\right\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:young_problem}
\end{equation}
The household compares the best rental and ownership plans, including their
fertility and saving choices. The results do not prescribe which young
households rent or own.

\section{Stationary equilibrium}

A stationary equilibrium links the housing choices of both ages to the same
stock. Old households' resources are generated by their choices when young.
For any variable indexed by entering type, a bar denotes its average under $F$.

\begin{definition}
A positive stationary competitive equilibrium consists of prices $P,p$,
cohort mass $N>0$, and household choices
$\{c^y(w),h^y(w),n(w),z(w),c^o(w),h^o(w),e(w)\}$ and tenures, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Young households solve \eqref{eq:young_problem}, and old households
solve \eqref{eq:new_old_owner} with resources $z^o(w)=z(w)$ inherited from
the preceding cohort's choices.
\item Landlords satisfy \eqref{eq:new_usercost} and the housing market clears:
\begin{equation}
N(\bar h^y+\bar h^o)=\bar H.
\label{eq:clearing}
\end{equation}
\item Fertility replaces the entering cohort, $\nu\bar n=1$, and
public-service spending equals property-tax revenue $\tau P\bar H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
Financial claims are settled with the external financial sector. Every young
plan has $z\ge y$, so the resulting old resource distribution also satisfies
$z^o\ge y$.

We take such an equilibrium as the allocation to be assessed. The propositions
below hold at any stationary equilibrium satisfying their stated inequalities.
The rental price $p$ in those inequalities is an equilibrium object. The
results do not rely on a calibrated reference equilibrium, but also do not
assert global existence or uniqueness for arbitrary primitives.

\section{The planner's allocation at fixed fertility}

Would a planner give the young more housing? Hold each parent's fertility at
its competitive choice $n_i$. The planner chooses all current consumption and
housing, keeping each young household's retirement resources $z_i$ and each
old household's net estate $e_j$ fixed. It can relax private finance and
reassign tenure; a home larger than $r$ must be owned. Total goods and housing
remain those of the competitive allocation.

Let $\mathcal C=\bar c^{y,E}+\bar c^{o,E}$ and
$\mathcal H=\bar h^{y,E}+\bar h^{o,E}=\bar H/N$ be these totals per young
household, including both ages. Give every living household weight one on its
remaining utility. Because future resources, continuation prices, and estates
are fixed, the terms involving future utility and estates are constant.
The planner's problem is therefore:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\max_{\{c_i^y,h_i^y,c_j^o,h_j^o\}}\quad
 &\avg{\log(c_i^y-\chi n_i)+\alpha\log(h_i^y-\kappa n_i)}
   +\avg{\log c_j^o+\alpha\log h_j^o},\\
\text{subject to}\quad
 &\bar c^y+\bar c^o=\mathcal C,\qquad
   \bar h^y+\bar h^o=\mathcal H.
\end{split}
\label{eq:dated_planner}
\end{equation}
All adult consumption and space must be positive. This compares allocations
for the living households at one date, including the initial old.

The planner divides adult goods and space equally and then adds children's
needs. In particular, define:
\begin{equation}
X=\frac{\mathcal C-\chi\bar n}{2},\qquad
S=\frac{\mathcal H-\kappa\bar n}{2}.
\label{eq:adult_allocation}
\end{equation}
Strict concavity gives the unique real allocation:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(c_i^{y,F},h_i^{y,F})&=(X+\chi n_i,\ S+\kappa n_i),\\
(c_j^{o,F},h_j^{o,F})&=(X,\ S).
\end{aligned}}
\label{eq:full_allocation}
\end{equation}
Each parent receives $\kappa n_i$ more housing than an old household in this
allocation. To compare with equilibrium, however, we must establish which
age initially has more space available to adults.

\subsection{When housing moves toward the young}

Let $J=1+\alpha+\vartheta$, the sum of current utility weights for a young
household. Consider young types satisfying these two inequalities:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\quad
w<\frac{Jqy}{\beta K},\qquad
\frac{w}{J}\left(\frac{\alpha}{p}
       +\frac{\vartheta\kappa}{\chi+\kappa p}\right)>r.
\quad}
\tag{A2}\label{ass:affected}
\end{equation}
The first says the household would like to borrow against retirement income
if it could rent a home of any size. The second says its preferred rental,
subject to nonnegative saving, would exceed the rental ceiling. Ownership is
available, but requires it to carry positive equity into retirement.
Appendix~\ref{app:households} shows that these inequalities imply a strict
housing distortion whichever tenure the household chooses. They are a
simple sufficient test; the appendix gives the wider exact income interval.

The last condition compares the resources of the two ages:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\frac{y}{K}\ge\frac{\bar w}{J}.}
\tag{A3}\label{ass:resources}
\end{equation}
The left side is a floor on old consumption, since every old household has
resources at least $y$. The right side bounds young current expenditure per
unit of current utility weight, before subtracting saving and equity.

\begin{proposition}[Housing allocation]\label{prop:housing}
Suppose \eqref{ass:old} holds and a positive share of young households satisfies
\eqref{ass:affected}. The competitive allocation does not maximize the
planner's objective \eqref{eq:dated_planner}. If, in addition,
\eqref{ass:resources} holds, its maximizing allocation gives the young more
housing in aggregate:
\begin{equation}
\bar h^{y,F}-\bar h^{y,E}
 =\frac{\bar h^{o,E}-\avg{h^{y,E}-\kappa n}}{2}>0.
\label{eq:housing_change}
\end{equation}
An individual young household receives more housing exactly when
$h_i^{y,E}-\kappa n_i<S$.
\end{proposition}

The borrowing restriction and rental ceiling establish a difference in
marginal housing values. Condition~\eqref{ass:resources} additionally makes
the full equal-weight allocation move housing toward the young as a group.
The distinction matters because the planner also redistributes resources
across rich and poor households within each age.

Here is the argument. Let $x_i=c_i^{y,E}-\chi n_i$ be young adult goods,
$s_i=h_i^{y,E}-\kappa n_i$ adult space, and $B=\bar c^{o,E}$ mean old
consumption. Define $v_i=ps_i/\alpha$ and the expenditure index
$\widehat x_i=(c_i^{y,E}+ph_i^{y,E})/J$. Household optimality gives:
\begin{equation}
v_i\le\widehat x_i\le x_i,\qquad
\widehat x_i=\frac{w_i-q(z_i-y)}{J}\le\frac{w_i}{J}.
\label{eq:comparison_chain}
\end{equation}
The first two inequalities are strict for the affected households. Old
optimality gives $ph_j^{o,E}/\alpha=c_j^{o,E}$ and $B\ge y/K$.
Consequently, \eqref{ass:resources} implies $B>\bar v$, which is exactly
the housing comparison in \eqref{eq:housing_change}.

This also shows a less restrictive sufficient condition than
\eqref{ass:resources}: $B\ge\avg{\widehat x}$. The mean housing change can
be written as:
\begin{equation}
\bar h^{y,F}-\bar h^{y,E}
 =\frac{\alpha}{2p}
   \left[\bigl(B-\avg{\widehat x}\bigr)
          +\bigl(\avg{\widehat x}-\bar v\bigr)\right].
\label{eq:housing_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term compares the resources available to the two ages; the second
comes from the young households' distorted division between goods and space.

Young consumption can fall even as housing rises. Its mean change is:
\begin{equation}
\bar c^{y,F}-\bar c^{y,E}=\frac{B-\bar x}{2}.
\label{eq:consumption_change}
\end{equation}
For example, at $B=\avg{\widehat x}$, the strict inequalities in
\eqref{eq:comparison_chain} imply more young housing and less young
consumption. The allocation corrects the balance between the two goods.

\subsection{What the conditions require}

Condition~\eqref{ass:old} ensures that old housing demand has the usual ratio
$h^o/c^o=\alpha/p$. It is a restriction on estate preferences relative to
the required equity. If it fails, the old may also face a housing distortion
and the comparison must be rederived.

Condition~\eqref{ass:affected} identifies affected households from income,
retirement resources, and access to rental space. It does not assume that
they rent, or that they already have smaller homes than the old. Its first
inequality is less likely to hold for patient households at given income.
There is no imposed ordering between $\beta$ and $q$, but this does not
remove patience from the conditions.

Condition~\eqref{ass:resources} is sufficient rather than necessary. Written
as an income ratio, it requires:
\begin{equation}
\frac{y}{\bar w}\ge\frac{1+\alpha+\omega_B}{1+\alpha+\vartheta}.
\label{eq:income_ratio}
\end{equation}
If $\omega_B\le\vartheta$, then $y\ge\bar w$ suffices. The condition can
be conservative because old resources include accumulated assets and young
current expenditure excludes saving. It remains a substantive restriction,
and these two-period endowments have not been mapped into annual income
measures or calibration targets.

\section{Fertility}

\subsection{The full allocation when fertility can change}

Allow the planner to choose fertility as well as every consumption and
housing bundle. It values existing parents' utility, including their taste
for children. The set of living adults, individual retirement resources,
and net estates remains fixed. For any mean fertility, equal adult goods
and space maximize utility; strict concavity of $\log n$ also makes fertility
the same across parents. The full problem therefore reduces to:
\begin{equation}
\max_{0<n<\min\{\mathcal C/\chi,\mathcal H/\kappa\}}
 2\log X(n)+2\alpha\log S(n)+\vartheta\log n,
\quad
X(n)=\frac{\mathcal C-\chi n}{2},\quad
S(n)=\frac{\mathcal H-\kappa n}{2}.
\label{eq:joint_problem}
\end{equation}
The objective is strictly concave and approaches minus infinity at the
feasible boundaries. Its unique solution $n^F$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\vartheta}{n^F}
 =\frac{\chi}{X(n^F)}+\frac{\alpha\kappa}{S(n^F)}.
\label{eq:joint_foc}
\end{equation}
The marginal utility of another child equals the goods and housing it uses.
Appendix~\ref{app:fertility} gives the explicit solution of this quadratic.

Every bundle at the optimum is then determined:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(c^{y,F},h^{y,F})
 &=\left(\frac{\mathcal C+\chi n^F}{2},
         \frac{\mathcal H+\kappa n^F}{2}\right),\\
(c^{o,F},h^{o,F})
 &=\left(\frac{\mathcal C-\chi n^F}{2},
         \frac{\mathcal H-\kappa n^F}{2}\right).
\end{aligned}}
\label{eq:joint_allocation}
\end{equation}
The change in fertility changes the resources reserved for children as well
as the resources shared by adults.

To sign the fertility comparison, assume:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\kappa p\ge\alpha\chi.}
\tag{A4}\label{ass:child_cost}
\end{equation}
Children's housing-to-goods expenditure ratio, $\kappa p/\chi$, must be at
least the unrestricted adult ratio $\alpha$. This condition concerns the
composition of children's needs, rather than the size of the welfare gain.

\begin{proposition}[Fertility]\label{prop:fertility}
Under the conditions for the aggregate housing result in
Proposition~\ref{prop:housing} and \eqref{ass:child_cost}, the full planner
chooses fertility above its competitive mean: $n^F>\bar n^E$. Aggregate
housing assigned to the young also rises. Moreover, each parent voluntarily
chooses $n^F$ when given the fixed consumption and housing bundle in
\eqref{eq:joint_allocation}, with continuation resources held fixed.
\end{proposition}

The private fertility condition helps explain the result. At any competitive
bundle it is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\vartheta}{n_i}
 =\frac{\chi}{x_i}+\frac{\kappa p}{v_i}.
\label{eq:private_foc}
\end{equation}
Restricted housing makes space expensive at the margin. Under
\eqref{ass:child_cost}, correcting the division of a parent's expenditure
between goods and space increases fertility. Condition~\eqref{ass:resources}
ensures that the full allocation has enough resources to preserve this gain
in the aggregate, even though young consumption may fall. The proof uses
\eqref{eq:comparison_chain} and the concavity of fertility as a function of
adult goods and space.

The voluntary-choice statement uses the final bundles in
\eqref{eq:joint_allocation}. Give each parent those totals before it chooses
fertility. At $n^F$, its residual adult bundle is $(X(n^F),S(n^F))$, so its
private first-order condition is precisely \eqref{eq:joint_foc}. Strict
concavity makes this its unique choice. The bundle need not be conditional
on realized births.

This differs from first computing \eqref{eq:full_allocation} at each parent's
competitive fertility and then allowing parents to choose again. That
sequence assigns different total bundles to parents with different initial
fertility; it need not increase mean fertility. Proposition~\ref{prop:fertility}
instead computes the best allocation anticipating parents' choices.

\subsection{A compensated housing transfer}

The same argument has a local version that does not require the average
income condition. For a household satisfying \eqref{ass:affected}, its
marginal housing value in consumption units exceeds that of any old household:
\begin{equation}
\frac{u_h^y}{u_c^y}=\frac{\alpha x_i}{s_i}>p
 =\frac{\alpha c_j^o}{h_j^o}=\frac{u_h^o}{u_c^o}.
\label{eq:local_mrs}
\end{equation}
Transfer a little housing to that parent and enough goods in the opposite
direction to compensate the old household. The parent gains after paying
this compensation, even at its original fertility. Finance is relaxed as in
the planner comparison, and retirement resources and estates are preserved.

If the parent subsequently chooses fertility at its new fixed bundle, the
first-order fertility response is positive precisely when:
\begin{equation}
\kappa p\left(\frac{x_i}{v_i}\right)^2>\alpha\chi.
\label{eq:local_fertility}
\end{equation}
Since $x_i/v_i>1$ for the affected parent, the weak inequality
\eqref{ass:child_cost} suffices. The calculation is in
Appendix~\ref{app:local}.

\section{From allocation to policy and transition}

The results establish a static allocation benchmark. They give the full
planner allocation, sufficient conditions for housing to move toward the
young, and a fertility comparison based on parents' own utility. The planner
can assign fixed consumption and housing bundles while parents choose fertility
at those bundles.

A policy using transfers while households freely choose housing, tenure, and
saving is a different problem. Its effect depends on prices and on which
constraints bind after the transfers. The direct allocation permits a
relaxation of finance; the results above are therefore not a theorem of
constrained inefficiency under the original borrowing limits.

Likewise, applying the fertility result to a dated allocation does not establish
an equilibrium transition. To compare the paths following a fertility shock
and a housing policy, one must specify that policy, solve for changing house
prices and inherited wealth, and follow cohort entry. A new stationary
population must satisfy replacement fertility, $\nu\bar n=1$; its level also
depends on housing per household through market clearing. Higher fertility at
one allocation does not by itself rank those stationary population levels.

\appendix
\section{Household choices and the affected incomes}\label{app:households}

\subsection{Old households}

Ignoring tenure temporarily, the old household allocates expenditure across
three log arguments. The first-order conditions imply expenditure shares
$1/K$, $\alpha/K$, and $\omega_B/K$, giving \eqref{eq:old_solution}. For
that allocation, the owner restriction is:
\begin{equation}
qe-\Gamma ph^o=\frac{(\omega_B-\alpha\Gamma)z^o}{K}\ge0.
\end{equation}
Thus it is feasible under \eqref{ass:old}, at any desired home size. It
maximizes utility over the unrestricted budget set and hence over the union
of the two tenure sets.

\subsection{Young households}

Write $x=c-\chi n$, $s=h-\kappa n$, $t=qz$, $Y=qy$,
$\delta=\beta K$, and $C(p)=\chi+\kappa p$. Dropping terms constant in the
choice variables, the young household's problem is:
\begin{equation}
\max\{\log x+\alpha\log s+\vartheta\log n+\delta\log t\},
\qquad x+ps+C(p)n+t=w+Y.
\label{eq:appendix_young}
\end{equation}
Renting adds $t\ge Y$ and $s+\kappa n\le r$; owning adds
$t\ge Y+\Gamma p(s+\kappa n)$. Each conditional problem is strictly
concave on a convex feasible set.

Let $\lambda$ be the budget multiplier. If $\eta\ge0$ is the rental-ceiling
multiplier and $\mu\ge0$ the owner's equity multiplier, the consumption
and housing conditions are:
\begin{equation}
\lambda=1/x,\qquad
\frac{\alpha}{s}=\begin{cases}
p/x+\eta,&\text{renting},\\
p/x+\Gamma p\mu,&\text{owning}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:young_housing_foc}
\end{equation}
Every optimal plan therefore has $\alpha x/s\ge p$. This remains true at
a tie between tenure values.

Under the first inequality in \eqref{ass:affected}, the uncapped rental
solution has $t=Y$, $x=w/J$, and:
\begin{equation}
s=\frac{\alpha w}{Jp},\qquad
n=\frac{\vartheta w}{JC(p)},\qquad
h=\frac{w}{J}\left(\frac{\alpha}{p}
                    +\frac{\vartheta\kappa}{C(p)}\right).
\end{equation}
The second inequality in \eqref{ass:affected} makes this home too large to
rent, so the actual rental optimum has $\eta>0$. For ownership, a zero
multiplier would give the unrestricted lifetime solution
$t=\delta(w+Y)/(J+\delta)<Y$, contradicting owner finance. Hence
$\mu>0$ at the owner optimum too. Either optimal tenure has a strict
housing distortion.

For completeness, the exact affected-income interval can be calculated
without solving for the tenure cutoff. Define:
\begin{equation}
A(p)=\alpha+\frac{\vartheta\kappa p}{C(p)},\qquad
x_r(p)=\frac{pr}{A(p)},\qquad
w_r(p)=Jx_r(p)+\max\{\delta x_r(p)-Y,0\}.
\end{equation}
Here $w_r$ is the income where the uncapped rental optimum first reaches
$r$; $\max$ denotes the larger of its two arguments. The unrestricted
lifetime allocation can be financed through ownership once income reaches:
\begin{equation}
w_O(p)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{Y[J+\Gamma A(p)]}{\delta-\Gamma A(p)},
 &\delta>\Gamma A(p),\\[5pt]
+\infty,&\delta\le\Gamma A(p).
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Consequently, every globally optimal tenure plan has a strict housing
distortion exactly for $w_r(p)<w<w_O(p)$.

To see both directions, below $w_r$ the best uncapped rental is feasible.
The owner's feasible set is contained in the uncapped rental set, so an
owner cannot improve on it. At or above $w_O$, the fully unrestricted
lifetime optimum is feasible through ownership. Between the thresholds,
neither conditional optimum can have a zero housing multiplier. Conversely,
a zero rental multiplier would make the uncapped rental feasible, and a
zero owner multiplier would make the unrestricted lifetime optimum feasible.
These statements include tenure ties and weak endpoint equalities. The
interval is nonempty exactly when $\delta pr<A(p)[Y+\Gamma pr]$.

\section{The allocation comparison}\label{app:allocation}

\subsection{Current expenditure and adult space}

Retain $x_i,s_i$ and $v_i=ps_i/\alpha$. Write $t_i=x_i/v_i\ge1$ and
$\lambda_c=\chi/(\kappa p)>0$. The private fertility condition gives:
\begin{equation}
n_i=\frac{\vartheta v_i t_i}{\kappa p(\lambda_c+t_i)},\qquad
\widehat x_i=\frac{x_i+\alpha v_i+C(p)n_i}{J}.
\label{eq:index_identity}
\end{equation}
Subtracting $v_i$ and subtracting this index from $x_i$ gives:
\begin{align}
\widehat x_i-v_i
 &=\frac{v_i(t_i-1)}{J}
   \left(1+\frac{\vartheta\lambda_c}{\lambda_c+t_i}\right),\\
x_i-\widehat x_i
 &=\frac{v_i(t_i-1)}{J}
   \left(\alpha+\frac{\vartheta t_i}{\lambda_c+t_i}\right).
\end{align}
Both expressions are nonnegative, and strictly positive when $t_i>1$.
This proves the first two inequalities in \eqref{eq:comparison_chain};
its last inequality follows from $z_i\ge y$ and the budget.

\subsection{The full maximizing allocation}

At fixed fertility the planner's goods and housing first-order conditions
respectively equalize $1/(c_i^y-\chi n_i)$ with $1/c_j^o$, and
$\alpha/(h_i^y-\kappa n_i)$ with $\alpha/h_j^o$, across all households.
Resource feasibility gives \eqref{eq:adult_allocation}. Strict concavity
establishes the unique real optimum \eqref{eq:full_allocation}.

Since old housing is $\alpha c^o/p$, the adult allocations can also be
written:
\begin{equation}
X=\frac{\bar x+B}{2},\qquad
S=\frac{\alpha}{p}\frac{\bar v+B}{2}.
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{ass:resources}, \eqref{eq:comparison_chain}, and a positive
share with $t_i>1$ imply $B\ge y/K\ge\bar w/J\ge
\avg{\widehat x}>\bar v$. Substitution proves the aggregate housing result
and its individual and consumption comparisons.

\subsection{Preserving retirement resources and estates}

The allocation can be settled without changing the promised future resources.
For a young household assigned an owned home $h_i'$, choose its net financial
position as $a_i'=q(z_i-y)-qPh_i'$. For an old household with fixed estate
$e_j$, use $a_j'=qe_j-qPh_j'$. Their current transfers are:
\begin{equation}
T_\ell=\Delta c_\ell+p\Delta h_\ell,\qquad
\bar T^y+\bar T^o=0.
\end{equation}
The transfers balance because total current consumption and housing are fixed.
Allowing mortgages up to the full house value makes these financial positions
feasible: $a_i'\ge-qPh_i'$ and $a_j'>-qPh_j'$ since $z_i\ge y$ and
$e_j>0$. Rental assignments use the corresponding nonnegative saving
positions. Intermediaries adjust their counterpart positions, and the tax
base $P\bar H$ is unchanged. This verifies feasibility for a planner allowed
to relax finance; it does not implement the allocation under the original
mortgage limit and unrestricted household reoptimization.

\section{Fertility and the explicit optimum}\label{app:fertility}

\subsection{The aggregate fertility comparison}

Define $g(x,v)=(\chi/x+\kappa p/v)^{-1}$, so private fertility is
$n_i=\vartheta g(x_i,v_i)$. Direct substitution in
\eqref{eq:index_identity} yields:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\widehat x_i}{C(p)}-g(x_i,v_i)
 =\frac{v_i(t_i-1)(t_i-\alpha\lambda_c)}
 {JC(p)(\lambda_c+t_i)}.
\label{eq:fertility_index}
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{ass:child_cost}, $\alpha\lambda_c\le1$, so this expression
is nonnegative for every type and strictly positive for affected types.
Thus $B/C(p)\ge\avg{\widehat x}/C(p)>\bar n/\vartheta$.

The function $g$ is increasing and concave on positive adult bundles. Its
second differential is:
\begin{equation}
\dd^2g=-\frac{2\chi\kappa p\,(v\,\dd x-x\,\dd v)^2}
 {(\chi v+\kappa p x)^3}\le0.
\end{equation}
At the fixed-fertility planner allocation, let
$V=pS/\alpha=(\bar v+B)/2$. Concavity then implies:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
g(X,V)
 &\ge\frac{g(\bar x,\bar v)+g(B,B)}{2}\\
 &\ge\frac{\avg{g(x_i,v_i)}+B/C(p)}{2}
 >\frac{\bar n}{\vartheta}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:jensen}
\end{equation}
At $n=\bar n$, the derivative of \eqref{eq:joint_problem} is therefore
$\vartheta/\bar n-1/g(X,V)>0$. Strict concavity proves $n^F>\bar n$.
Jointly changing fertility adds $\kappa(n^F-\bar n)/2$ to mean young
housing relative to the fixed-fertility allocation. It adds
$\chi(n^F-\bar n)/2$ to mean young consumption, so a consumption decline
in the first comparison need not persist in the joint optimum.

\subsection{Closed-form solution}

Multiplying \eqref{eq:joint_foc} by its positive denominators gives a
quadratic. With $D=\vartheta+2(1+\alpha)$ and
$\mathcal B=\kappa\mathcal C(\vartheta+2\alpha)
+\chi\mathcal H(\vartheta+2)$, it is:
\begin{equation}
\chi\kappa Dn^2-\mathcal B n+\vartheta\mathcal C\mathcal H=0.
\end{equation}
The smaller root is the unique feasible optimum:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{n^F=
\frac{2\vartheta\mathcal C\mathcal H}
 {\mathcal B+\sqrt{\mathcal B^2
        -4\chi\kappa D\vartheta\mathcal C\mathcal H}}}.
\label{eq:fertility_root}
\end{equation}
Its discriminant is strictly positive because it equals:
\begin{equation}
\left[\kappa\mathcal C(\vartheta+2\alpha)
      -\chi\mathcal H(\vartheta+2)\right]^2
      +16\alpha\chi\kappa\mathcal C\mathcal H.
\end{equation}
The derivative in \eqref{eq:joint_foc} decreases from positive to negative
infinity over the feasible interval, proving that exactly one root lies
there; the quadratic is positive at zero, so that root is the smaller one.
This is a closed-form solution for the planner conditional on the resource
totals. It is not a claim that every tenure choice and competitive price has
an elementary closed form.

\subsection{Voluntary fertility at the final bundles}

Give every parent the fixed bundle in \eqref{eq:joint_allocation}. Its
fertility objective, after dropping fixed continuation terms, is:
\begin{equation}
\log(c^{y,F}-\chi n)+\alpha\log(h^{y,F}-\kappa n)
   +\vartheta\log n.
\end{equation}
At $n=n^F$, its first derivative is zero by \eqref{eq:joint_foc}, and
its second derivative is
$-\chi^2/(c^{y,F}-\chi n)^2-\alpha\kappa^2/(h^{y,F}-\kappa n)^2
-\vartheta/n^2<0$. It therefore chooses $n^F$ uniquely. Any feasible
allocation induced by assigning fixed bundles and anticipating private
fertility is feasible in the joint planner problem. The joint optimum is
itself induced in this way, so it also maximizes welfare among those
assignments.

\section{Compensation and the local fertility response}\label{app:local}

Take housing $\epsilon>0$ from an old household with bundle $(c_o,h_o)$,
and compensate it with goods. Holding its estate fixed, exact indifference
requires:
\begin{equation}
\Delta c_o=c_o\left[\left(\frac{h_o}{h_o-\epsilon}\right)^\alpha-1\right]
 =p\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).
\end{equation}
The equality uses old optimality, $\alpha c_o/h_o=p$. The receiving parent
pays $\Delta c_o$ and receives $\epsilon$ housing. At its original fertility,
its first-order utility gain is $(\alpha/s_i-p/x_i)\epsilon>0$.
Small transfers preserve positivity. Applied to positive shares of young and
old households, this is a strict improvement in the planner's objective
without \eqref{ass:resources}.

If the parent chooses fertility at its new total bundle, differentiate its
private condition. Since $\dd c=-p\,\dd h$ to first order:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd n}{\dd h}
 =\frac{\alpha\kappa/s_i^2-\chi p/x_i^2}
 {\vartheta/n_i^2+\chi^2/x_i^2+\alpha\kappa^2/s_i^2}.
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive. Substituting $s_i=\alpha v_i/p$ gives
\eqref{eq:local_fertility}, completing the local comparison.
