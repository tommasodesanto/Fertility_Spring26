\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries Fertility, Population, and House Prices}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\large A simple model}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small August 8, 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{-3pt}
This note asks whether a lasting fall in the desire for children can move a
closed economy to a steady state with fewer people and cheaper housing. It can.
The fall in desired fertility initially puts fertility below replacement.
Population then contracts, which lowers the house price. Because children use
housing, the lower price raises fertility until replacement is restored. The
new steady state has the same replacement rate, but a smaller population and a
lower house price.

\section*{The model}

Time is discrete. In period $t$ there are $N_t$ identical adult families, so
$N_t$ is population measured in family units. A family has income $y$ and
chooses consumption $c$, housing for the adults $h$, and the number of children
$n$. Each child also requires $a>0$ units of housing and $b\geq0$ units of the
consumption good. Housing lasts for one period; this removes stock dynamics and
keeps the argument transparent.

The representative family solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{c,h,n}\; c+\alpha\log h+\beta\log n
 \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
 c+ph+(b+ap)n=y .
 \tag{1}
\end{equation}
Here $p$ is the house price, $\alpha>0$ measures the value of housing, and
$\beta>0$ measures the desire for children. The term $b+ap$ is the full cost of
a child: $b$ is the direct cost and $ap$ is the cost of the extra housing the
child requires. The experiment is a permanent fall from $\beta_0$ to
$\beta_1<\beta_0$.

The choices implied by (1) are
\begin{equation}
 h(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p},
 \qquad
 n(p;\beta)=\frac{\beta}{b+ap},
 \qquad
 c=y-\alpha-\beta .
 \tag{2}
\end{equation}
We assume $y>\alpha+\beta_0$, so consumption is positive before and after the
change. Equation (2) contains the feedback of interest: a lower house price
makes the housing needed for children cheaper and therefore raises fertility.

Housing supply is
\begin{equation}
 H^s(p)=A p^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad A>0,\quad \varepsilon\geq0.
 \tag{3}
\end{equation}
The case $\varepsilon=0$ is a fixed supply. Because a family occupies $h+an$
units of housing, market clearing in period $t$ is
\begin{equation}
 A p_t^{\varepsilon}
 =N_t\left[\frac{\alpha}{p_t}
 +\frac{a\beta}{b+ap_t}\right].
 \tag{4}
\end{equation}
For a given population, this equation determines one house price. More families
raise the price: supply rises with $p$, while housing demanded by each family
falls with $p$.

\section*{The steady state}

Let $\bar n>0$ be the number of children per family needed for replacement. The
population law is
\begin{equation}
 N_{t+1}=N_t\frac{n(p_t;\beta)}{\bar n}.
 \tag{5}
\end{equation}
A positive steady state therefore requires $n(p;\beta)=\bar n$. Using (2), the
house price consistent with replacement is
\begin{equation}
 p^*(\beta)=\frac{\beta/\bar n-b}{a}.
 \tag{6}
\end{equation}
This price is positive when $\beta>b\bar n$. We assume
$\beta_1>b\bar n$, so both steady states are positive. A fall in $\beta$ lowers
$p^*$: when families want fewer children, housing must become cheaper before
they again choose replacement fertility.

At replacement, each family uses $\alpha/p+a\bar n$ units of housing. Combining
this demand with housing supply gives the population that can be supported at
each price:
\begin{equation}
 N(p)=\frac{A p^{\varepsilon}}
 {\alpha/p+a\bar n}
 =\frac{A p^{\varepsilon+1}}
 {\alpha+a\bar n p}.
 \tag{7}
\end{equation}
This function is increasing. A higher price brings forth weakly more housing
and reduces housing used by each family. Equations (6) and (7) deliver the
result:
\[
 \beta\ \text{falls}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p^*\ \text{falls}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 N^*=N(p^*)\ \text{falls}.
\]

The adjustment is equally simple. At the old price, the new value $\beta_1$
would imply fertility below replacement. Market clearing makes the price jump
down immediately, which cushions the fertility decline. But population is
still above its new steady-state level, so the price remains above $p^*(\beta_1)$
and fertility remains below replacement. Population then falls, the price falls
further, and fertility rises. If population were to fall below its new
steady-state level, the inequalities would reverse. Thus the feedback moves the
economy toward the new steady state.
