\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries Fertility, Population, and House Prices}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\large A reproductive steady-state closure}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small August 11, 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{-3pt}
This note develops an alternative population closure for the housing--fertility
model. The economy is closed: every new adult household descends from a child
born inside the economy, and there is no outside inflow or entry decision. A
positive steady state must therefore reproduce itself. A permanent decline in
the desire for children initially puts fertility below replacement. Population
then contracts, reducing housing demand and the housing price. Because children
require housing, the lower price raises fertility until reproduction is restored.
In the simple model below, the new steady state has a smaller population and a
lower housing price. In the full lifecycle model, that population sign is an
equilibrium implication to verify rather than an assumption.

The claim is about a \emph{transition between reproductive steady states}. The
preference change is permanent; below-replacement fertility is not. The simple
model establishes the two endpoints and a one-state adjustment mechanism. The
last part of the note shows how the same closure can be stated using the objects
of the full lifecycle model and what would still be required to compute the
calendar-time transition.

\section*{1. A simple closed economy}

\textbf{Families and demography.} At date $t$ there is a mass $L_t$ of adult
families. Population is measured in family units. Each family chooses the number
of children $n$. The number $\bar n>0$ is effective replacement fertility: it
includes any survival and household-formation adjustment needed for one adult
family to reproduce itself.

\textbf{Preferences.} A family has income $y$ and values consumption $c$,
housing for the adults $h$, and children according to
\begin{equation}
 u(c,h,n;\beta)=c+\alpha\log h+\beta\log n,
 \qquad \alpha>0,\quad \beta>0.
 \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\beta$ measures the desire for children. The experiment is a
permanent decline from $\beta_0$ to $\beta_1<\beta_0$. Quasi-linearity is a
deliberate simplification: it removes income effects and exposes the housing
price channel in closed form.

\textbf{Housing requirements.} Each child uses $a>0$ additional units of
housing and $b\geq0$ units of the consumption good. If $p$ is the price of one
unit of housing services, the full resource cost of a child is $b+ap$. A family
that chooses $(h,n)$ occupies $h+an$ units in total.

\textbf{Housing supply.} Housing services are supplied according to
\begin{equation}
 H^s(p)=A p^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad A>0,\quad \varepsilon\geq0.
 \label{eq:housing_supply}
\end{equation}
The case $\varepsilon=0$ is a fixed stock. In this baseline, $p$ is the
one-period price of housing services. We call it the housing price below to keep
the diagrams readable. With a fixed user-cost wedge, the asset price moves in
the same direction; when a policy changes that wedge, the two prices must be
kept separate. The toy symbols are local to this note, and Section 5 translates
them into the paper's notation.

\section*{2. The family's problem}

Taking the housing price as given, a family solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{c,h,n}\; c+\alpha\log h+\beta\log n
 \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
 c+ph+(b+ap)n=y.
 \label{eq:family_problem}
\end{equation}
The term $ap$ is the housing bill associated with one more child, so the price
of housing enters the child decision directly.

The interior choices are
\begin{equation}
 h(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p},
 \qquad
 n(p;\beta)=\frac{\beta}{b+ap},
 \qquad
 c=y-\alpha-\beta.
 \label{eq:family_demands}
\end{equation}
\newpage
We assume $y>\alpha+\beta_0$, so consumption is positive before and after the
change. A lower $\beta$ reduces children at every price; holding $\beta$ fixed,
a lower housing price raises children.

\section*{3. Housing clearing and reproductive stationarity}

At population $L$, total housing demand is the number of families times the
housing used by each family. Market clearing requires
\begin{equation}
 A p^{\varepsilon}
 =L\left[\frac{\alpha}{p}
 +\frac{a\beta}{b+ap}\right].
 \label{eq:housing_clearing}
\end{equation}
For any $(L,\beta)$, this equation determines one price, denoted by
$p=P(L;\beta)$. More families raise the price, while a weaker desire for
children reduces child-related housing demand and shifts the entire price
schedule downward:
\begin{equation}
 P_L(L;\beta)>0,
 \qquad
 P_\beta(L;\beta)>0.
 \label{eq:price_schedule_signs}
\end{equation}
The first panel of Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} is this housing-market mapping from
current population into the housing price.

Population adjusts according to the gap between chosen and replacement
fertility. A one-state representation of that adjustment is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dot L_t}{L_t}
 =\lambda\left[
 \frac{n(P(L_t;\beta);\beta)}{\bar n}-1
 \right],
 \qquad \lambda>0.
 \label{eq:population_law}
\end{equation}
This reduced law compresses cohort aging into the parameter $\lambda$. It is
useful for the direction of adjustment, but it is not the age-structured law of
motion in the full lifecycle model.

A positive steady state must reproduce itself, so $n(p^*;\beta)=\bar n$.
The child decision therefore selects the steady-state price:
\begin{equation}
 p^*(\beta)=\frac{\beta/\bar n-b}{a}.
 \label{eq:steady_price}
\end{equation}
This price is positive when $\beta>b\bar n$. We assume
$\beta_1>b\bar n$. If this condition fails, even free housing cannot restore
replacement fertility and no positive closed-economy steady state exists.

At replacement, each family uses $\alpha/p^*+a\bar n$ units of housing.
Housing clearing then selects the population level:
\begin{equation}
 L^*(\beta)
 =\frac{A[p^*(\beta)]^{\varepsilon}}
 {\alpha/p^*(\beta)+a\bar n}.
 \label{eq:steady_population}
\end{equation}
The population supported by the housing market is increasing in $p$: a higher
price brings forth weakly more housing and reduces adult housing used by each
family. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \beta_1<\beta_0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p_1<p_0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 L_1<L_0,
 \qquad n_1=n_0=\bar n.
 \label{eq:long_run_comparison}
\end{equation}
The permanent preference change is capitalized into the long-run housing price
and population level. Fertility is below replacement only while the economy is
moving between the two steady states.

\section*{4. Interpreting the transition}

Suppose the economy begins at the old steady state
$E_0=(L_0,p_0,\bar n)$. The experiment has three distinct stages.

\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Preference change.} The permanent fall from $\beta_0$ to
 $\beta_1$ shifts the child decision down at every housing price.
 \item \textbf{Impact.} Population cannot jump. With one-period housing supply,
 the market reprices at $L_0$ to
 $\widetilde p=P(L_0;\beta_1)<p_0$. The price decline cushions the fertility
 decline, but does not eliminate it:
 \begin{equation}
  p_1<\widetilde p<p_0,
  \qquad
  \widetilde n\equiv n(\widetilde p;\beta_1)<\bar n.
  \label{eq:impact_ordering}
 \end{equation}
 \item \textbf{Demographic adjustment.} Below-replacement fertility reduces
 population. The housing price falls along the new market-clearing schedule,
 and the lower price raises fertility along the new child-decision curve.
 Adjustment stops at $E_1=(L_1,p_1,\bar n)$.
\end{enumerate}

There are two different claims in this description. The endpoints
$(E_0,E_1)$ are equilibrium comparative statics. Equation
\eqref{eq:population_law} additionally makes the transition monotone and the new
steady state globally stable in the one-state model. The full lifecycle model
can adopt the same reproductive closure without inheriting that transition
result: cohort aging and a durable housing stock introduce additional state
variables and lags.

\section*{5. The same closure in the full lifecycle model}

The full model replaces the representative family with a distribution of
households over age, income, wealth, tenure, completed fertility, and children
at home. Throughout this section, $\Theta$ includes all preference and policy
parameters, including the user-cost wedge $\rho(\Theta)$, and the asset-price
relation $P=r/\rho(\Theta)$ is substituted into the household problem. The
alternative closure then requires four aggregate objects at a candidate housing
user cost $r$:

\begin{itemize}
 \item $g(r;\Theta)$ is the normalized stationary lifecycle distribution.
 \item $D(r;\Theta)$ is physical housing demand per adult household after
 integrating the housing and fertility policies over $g$.
 \item $B(r;\Theta)$ is the flow of children who mature into new adult
 households per unit of adult population.
 \item $E(r;\Theta)$ is the inflow of young adults required to reproduce one
 unit of the stationary adult distribution.
\end{itemize}

The closed-economy replacement condition is
\begin{equation}
 B(r^*;\Theta)=E(r^*;\Theta).
 \label{eq:full_reproduction}
\end{equation}
This equation replaces the outside-option and fixed-inflow closures. There is
no immigration flow, retention parameter, outside value, or entry elasticity.
The correct empirical object is effective net reproduction, not necessarily a
conventional TFR of $2.1$: the equality must use the model's units for children,
survival, maturation, and household formation.

Once \eqref{eq:full_reproduction} selects $r^*$, level housing clearing selects
adult population:
\begin{equation}
 S^*(\Theta)
 =\frac{H^S(r^*;\Theta)}{D(r^*;\Theta)}.
 \label{eq:full_scale}
\end{equation}
Thus the full equilibrium has the same order as the simple model. Reproduction
selects the housing cost; housing clearing selects population scale. The asset
price is then obtained from the user-cost relation,
$P^*=r^*/\rho(\Theta)$.

The household mechanism is also the same, although it is no longer summarized
by one representative demand curve. In the analytical model, the full price of
a child contains the goods requirement $\chi$ and the child's space requirement
$\kappa$ valued at the household's effective marginal housing cost:
\begin{equation}
 \text{full price of a child}
 =\chi+\kappa\bigl(r^m+\zeta_i^m\bigr).
 \label{eq:full_child_price}
\end{equation}
Here $r^m$ is the tenure-specific housing cost and $\zeta_i^m$ is the goods-value
of a binding rental, menu, or financing constraint. The simple formula
$b+ap$ is the unconstrained representative-family version of this object. In
the quantitative model, the corresponding ``child decision'' in Panel (b) is
an aggregate of discrete birth attempts and housing choices, not a literal
continuous demand function.

For existence, the reproduction residual
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R(r;\Theta)\equiv B(r;\Theta)-E(r;\Theta)
 \label{eq:reproduction_residual}
\end{equation}
must cross zero. A sufficient set of conditions for a unique reproductive price
is that $\mathcal R$ is continuous, positive at a low housing cost, negative at
a high housing cost, and strictly decreasing in $r$. Assume the preference
change shifts the whole residual downward,
$\mathcal R(r;\Theta_1)<\mathcal R(r;\Theta_0)$, over the relevant range. If
$\mathcal R_r<0$, the new root has $r_1<r_0$.

To state the population comparison precisely, define the scale selected by
housing clearing as
\begin{equation}
 F(r,\Theta)\equiv \frac{H^S(r;\Theta)}{D(r;\Theta)}.
 \label{eq:scale_mapping}
\end{equation}
The simple model gives $S_1<S_0$ analytically. In the full model, the same sign
requires the cross-regime inequality
$F(r_1,\Theta_1)<F(r_0,\Theta_0)$. The condition $F_r>0$ alone is not enough:
the preference change can directly alter housing demand, tenure, and lifecycle
composition. A quantitative implementation should therefore separate the
effect of the lower price from this direct preference-and-composition effect.

\section*{6. What is the economic interpretation?}

\textbf{As a theory result.} The simple model compares two closed-economy
reproductive steady states. A weaker desire for children puts fertility below
replacement on impact; population and the housing price then fall until the
housing feedback restores reproduction. The permanent differences are a
smaller population and cheaper housing. The full model preserves the
reproductive condition, but its population sign requires the cross-regime test
in \eqref{eq:scale_mapping}.

\textbf{As an interpretation of currently low fertility.} One may conjecture
that an observed below-replacement economy is partway between the two steady
states: preferences or other child costs have changed, while population, the
age distribution, and the housing stock still reflect earlier cohorts. Under
this interpretation, fertility may recover as population and housing demand
fall. This is a motivating hypothesis, not a result of the stationary model;
testing it requires locating the observed economy along a demographic
transition. What must return to one is effective net reproduction, not
necessarily conventional TFR.

\textbf{As a policy model.} In the simple model, a permanent increase in the
desire for children puts fertility above replacement at the old price;
population and the price rise until $n=\bar n$ again. In the full model, every
positive reproductive steady state instead has $B/E=1$. If a policy shifts
$\mathcal R$ upward and $\mathcal R_r<0$, its reproductive root has a higher
$r^*$. Population rises only if the corresponding $F(r,\Theta)$ rises across
regimes. Because $B/E=1$ is an accounting condition on effective reproduction,
it does not fix conventional TFR, completed-fertility composition, or aggregate
births. The quantitative exercise should report these objects separately;
existing stationary policy rows use a different closure and cannot simply be
relabeled.

\section*{7. Relation to the earlier theoretical sketch}

The two sketches are complements. The earlier value-gap and housing-slack
sketch describes the microeconomic birth decision: prices, rental limits,
owner rungs, and down-payment constraints change the value of the parent branch
relative to the childless branch. This note supplies the aggregate closure:
births reproduce population, population determines housing demand, and housing
costs feed back into births.

The natural synthesis for the paper is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item use the value gap and the effective price in
 \eqref{eq:full_child_price} to explain the household fertility margin;
 \item use \eqref{eq:full_reproduction} to define reproductive stationarity; and
 \item use \eqref{eq:full_scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} to determine and
 illustrate population through housing clearing.
\end{enumerate}
The simple utility block is therefore an analytical illustration, not a second
quantitative household model.

\section*{8. What the quantitative audit finds}

The companion memo \emph{Reproductive Closure in the Full Lifecycle Model}
implements the stationary checks and specifies the unsolved transition. Its
main result is that the retained E5b parameter vector is not reproductive:
$B/E=0.82784$. A favorable reweighting of the top-coded $3+$ state raises the
diagnostic ratio to $0.90319$, still below replacement. Over fixed asset prices
from one percent to twice the maintained price, $B/E$ ranges only from $0.83600$
to $0.82034$. The residual has the proposed slope but never crosses zero. The
maintained estimate therefore has no positive closed reproductive steady state
on the tested grid.

The scale map $F(r,\Theta)=H^S/D$ is increasing, as the simple model requires.
But without a reproductive root there are no maintained full-model endpoints
across which to assert or decompose a population change. Point $I$ and the
arrows in Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} remain intuition until the age distribution
and durable housing stock are evolved in calendar time.

With durable housing, a full transition must evolve both the lifecycle
distribution and the housing stock while distinguishing service cost $r_t$ from
asset price $P_t$. Its endpoint matches the static supply curve only if the
investment technology reproduces $H^S(r)$ in steady state.
