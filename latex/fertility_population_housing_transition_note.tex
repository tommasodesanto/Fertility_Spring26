\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries Fertility, Population, and House Prices}\par
\vspace{2pt}
{\large A simple household-demand and housing-supply model}\par
\vspace{2pt}
{\small August 8, 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{-3pt}
\textbf{Question.} Can a permanent decline in the desire for children move a
closed economy from one positive steady state to another with fewer households
and a lower house price? The answer is yes when children require housing. At the
old price, the preference shift pushes fertility below replacement and population
begins to fall. The resulting decline in housing demand lowers the house price,
which reduces the full cost of a child. At the new steady state, the lower price
exactly offsets the lower taste for children: fertility is again at replacement,
but population and the house price are both lower.

\section*{Environment}

\textbf{Agents.} There is a mass $N_t$ of identical adult households. Each
household has income $y>0$ and chooses numeraire consumption $c$, total housing
$h$, and completed fertility $\ell$. Fertility is measured in effective
replacement units: $\ell=\ellbar$ reproduces one adult household. One can set
$\ellbar=1$, or retain an appropriate survival and household-formation adjustment
when interpreting raw births.

\textbf{Preferences.} A household values consumption, discretionary housing,
and children according to
\begin{equation}
 u(c,h,\ell;\chi)
 =\log c+\alpha\log(h-\nu\ell)+\chi\log\ell,
 \qquad \alpha>0,\quad \nu>0,\quad \chi>0.
 \label{eq:preferences}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu$ is the housing requirement per child, so $h-\nu\ell>0$ is housing
left after accommodating children. The parameter $\chi$ measures the desire for
children. The experiment is a permanent decline from $\chi_0$ to
$\chi_1<\chi_0$.

\textbf{Prices and resources.} The nonhousing resource cost of a child is
$k>0$. The price $p_h$ is the stationary user price of one unit of housing.
With a fixed interest rate and depreciation rate, it is proportional to the
asset price, so the steady-state comparative statics have the same sign for the
house price.

\textbf{Housing supply.} The long-run supply of usable housing is
\begin{equation}
 H^S(p_h)=\Omega p_h^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad \Omega>0,\quad \varepsilon\geq0.
 \label{eq:supply}
\end{equation}
The case $\varepsilon=0$ is a fixed long-run stock. When $\varepsilon>0$,
lower prices reduce replacement construction and the stock contracts as old
units depreciate. Equation \eqref{eq:supply} describes steady states; it does
not impose an instantaneous physical adjustment of the housing stock.

\section*{The household's problem}

Taking $p_h$ as given, the representative household solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{c,h,\ell}\ \log c+\alpha\log(h-\nu\ell)+\chi\log\ell
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad
 c+p_hh+k\ell=y.
 \label{eq:household_problem}
\end{equation}
Define discretionary housing $x\equiv h-\nu\ell$. Substitution into the budget
constraint exposes the economic mechanism:
\begin{equation}
 c+p_hx+\underbrace{(k+\nu p_h)}_{\text{full price of a child}}\ell=y.
 \label{eq:full_child_price}
\end{equation}
Housing enters fertility because every child requires $\nu$ additional units.
The direct cost is $k$, while the housing component of the cost is $\nu p_h$.

Let $D(\chi)\equiv1+\alpha+\chi$. The unique interior demands are
\begin{equation}
 c^d=\frac{y}{D(\chi)},\qquad
 x^d=\frac{\alpha y}{D(\chi)p_h},\qquad
 \ell^d(p_h;\chi)=
 \frac{\chi y}{D(\chi)(k+\nu p_h)},\qquad
 h^d=x^d+\nu\ell^d.
 \label{eq:demands}
\end{equation}
Thus $\partial\ell^d/\partial p_h<0$ and
$\partial\ell^d/\partial\chi>0$. The inverse fertility schedule---the house
price consistent with a chosen $\ell$---is
\begin{equation}
 p_h^F(\ell;\chi)=\frac{1}{\nu}
 \left[\frac{\chi}{D(\chi)}\frac{y}{\ell}-k\right].
 \label{eq:inverse_fertility}
\end{equation}
It slopes downward in $\ell$. A decline in $\chi$ shifts the entire schedule
down because a lower house price is required to support any given fertility
level.

\section*{Steady-state equilibrium}

For a fixed preference parameter $\chi$, a positive steady-state equilibrium is
a vector $(c^*,h^*,\ell^*,H^*,N^*,p_h^*)$ such that households choose the
demands in \eqref{eq:demands}, the housing market clears, and fertility replaces
the adult population:
\begin{equation}
 H^*=N^*h^d(p_h^*;\chi)=\Omega(p_h^*)^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad
 \ell^d(p_h^*;\chi)=\ellbar.
 \label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
The replacement condition first selects a house price. Housing demand and supply
then determine how many households that price can support.

\textbf{The fertility-to-price map.} Imposing $\ell^d=\ellbar$ in household
demand gives
\begin{equation}
 p_h^*(\chi)=p_h^F(\ellbar;\chi)
 =\frac{1}{\nu}\left[
 \frac{\chi}{D(\chi)}\frac{y}{\ellbar}-k\right].
 \label{eq:steady_price}
\end{equation}
A positive steady-state price exists when
$[\chi/D(\chi)]y>k\ellbar$, equivalently when
$y>k\ellbar$ and
$\chi>k\ellbar(1+\alpha)/(y-k\ellbar)$. If this condition fails, fertility is
below replacement even when housing is free, so a closed population cannot
converge to a positive steady state.

\textbf{The price-to-population map.} Conditional on replacement fertility,
the household allocates its resources net of child costs between consumption
and discretionary housing. Its total housing demand can therefore be written
without $\chi$:
\begin{equation}
 h^R(p_h)=
 \nu\ellbar+
 \frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
 \frac{y-(k+\nu p_h)\ellbar}{p_h}
 =\frac{\alpha(y-k\ellbar)}{(1+\alpha)p_h}
 +\frac{\nu\ellbar}{1+\alpha}.
\label{eq:replacement_housing}
\end{equation}
This expression applies on
$0<p_h<(y-k\ellbar)/(\nu\ellbar)$, where resources left after accommodating
replacement fertility are positive.
Equating $N h^R(p_h)$ to long-run supply gives the population supported by
each house price:
\begin{equation}
 N^H(p_h)=\frac{H^S(p_h)}{h^R(p_h)}
 =\frac{(1+\alpha)\Omega p_h^{\varepsilon+1}}
 {\alpha(y-k\ellbar)+\nu\ellbar p_h}.
 \label{eq:population_locus}
\end{equation}
This locus is increasing: a larger population requires a higher price to clear
the housing market.

\textbf{Comparative static.} For any positive steady state,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d p_h^*}{d\chi}
 =\frac{y(1+\alpha)}
 {\nu\ellbar[D(\chi)]^2}>0,
 \qquad
 \frac{d\log N^H}{d\log p_h}
 =\varepsilon+
 \frac{\alpha(y-k\ellbar)}
 {\alpha(y-k\ellbar)+\nu\ellbar p_h}>0.
 \label{eq:comparative_statics}
\end{equation}
Hence a permanent decline in $\chi$ lowers $p_h^*$ through household fertility
demand and then lowers $N^*=N^H(p_h^*)$ through housing-market clearing. If
$\varepsilon>0$, the aggregate housing stock $H^*=\Omega(p_h^*)^\varepsilon$
also falls; if $\varepsilon=0$, the stock is fixed and the price absorbs the
entire housing-demand adjustment.

\section*{Adjustment to the new steady state}

The two diagrams establish the long-run comparison. To verify convergence
without treating the long-run housing-supply curve as an instantaneous stock,
let the physical housing stock adjust gradually. Population growth depends on
fertility relative to replacement:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dot N_t}{N_t}=\lambda
 \left[\frac{\ell^d(p_{h,t};\chi)}{\ellbar}-1\right],
 \qquad \lambda>0.
 \label{eq:population_law}
\end{equation}
At each date, the existing stock $H_t$ clears against household demand:
\begin{equation}
 H_t=N_t h^d(p_{h,t};\chi).
 \label{eq:transition_clearing}
\end{equation}
Construction and depreciation move the stock toward the long-run supply curve.
Around the new steady state, write this partial-adjustment law as
$\dot{\widehat H}_t=\delta[\varepsilon\widehat p_{h,t}-\widehat H_t]$,
where hats are log deviations and $\delta>0$ is the adjustment rate.

Let
$\eta_\ell\equiv-\partial\log\ell^d/\partial\log p_h>0$ and
$\zeta_h\equiv-\partial\log h^d/\partial\log p_h>0$, evaluated at the new
steady state. Log-linearizing \eqref{eq:transition_clearing} gives
$\widehat p_{h,t}=(\widehat N_t-\widehat H_t)/\zeta_h$. The joint adjustment
system is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \begin{pmatrix}\dot{\widehat N}_t\\[1pt]\dot{\widehat H}_t\end{pmatrix}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}
 -a & a\\
 \delta\varepsilon/\zeta_h & -\delta(1+\varepsilon/\zeta_h)
 \end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}\widehat N_t\\[1pt]\widehat H_t\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 a\equiv\frac{\lambda\eta_\ell}{\zeta_h}>0.
 \label{eq:local_dynamics}
\end{equation}
The matrix has trace
$-a-\delta(1+\varepsilon/\zeta_h)<0$ and determinant $a\delta>0$, so both
adjustment roots have negative real parts. The lower steady state is locally
stable for every $\varepsilon\geq0$ and every positive demographic and
housing-stock adjustment rate.

Immediately after $\chi$ falls, the old house price implies
$\ell^d(p_{h,0}^*;\chi_1)<\ellbar$, so population starts to decline. The house
price and physical stock then adjust jointly toward $(p_{h,1}^*,H_1^*)$; the
local stability result does not require the price path to be monotone. The
permanent change is in $\chi$, not in realized fertility: realized fertility is
below replacement during adjustment and returns to replacement in the new
positive steady state.

\section*{What the toy model establishes---and what it does not}

The model provides an explicit closed-economy closure for the feedback discussed
in the meeting:
\begin{equation*}
 \chi\downarrow
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \ell_t<\ellbar
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 N_t\downarrow
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p_{h,t}\downarrow
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \ell_t\uparrow\text{ toward }\ellbar.
\end{equation*}
It is deliberately a steady-state mechanism, not a quantitative transition.
The full model adds an age structure, delayed household formation, tenure and
down-payment choices, and a detailed durable-housing sector. Those features can
slow the population response and make the asset-price path nonmonotone even
though the long-run comparison remains lower $N$ and lower $p_h$. In a
quantitative extension, the reduced-form stock adjustment in
\eqref{eq:local_dynamics} would be replaced by an explicit construction and
depreciation law, $\dot H_t=X(p_{h,t})-\delta H_t$.

This is also an alternative to the current quota closure, not a
reinterpretation of it. Under a fixed outside inflow, permanently
subreplacement native fertility can coexist with a positive stationary
population. In the closed economy here, the positive steady state instead
exists because the housing-price feedback makes fertility endogenous and
restores replacement.

\section*{Literature map}

\href{https://www.nber.org/papers/w29480}{Delventhal,
Fern\'andez-Villaverde, and Guner (2021)} provide the closest
Fern\'andez-Villaverde reference: their model has endogenous fertility and an
analytically characterized replacement-fertility steady state, but fixed land
enters production rather than a household housing market. It does not derive
the two loci in Figure \ref{fig:comparative_statics}.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1086/427465}{Glaeser and Gyourko (2005)} supply the
durable-housing logic behind gradual stock contraction after a negative demand
shock. \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econmod.2025.107125}{Lee and Suh
(2025)} show why a richer OLG transition can be nonmonotone: aging and interest
rates may initially offset the eventual negative effect of very low fertility
on house prices.
