\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries Fertility, Population, and House Prices}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\large A transition between steady states}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small August 8, 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{-3pt}
This note isolates one feedback. A lasting decline in the desire for children
initially puts fertility below replacement. Population then contracts, which
reduces housing demand and lowers the house price. Because children require
housing, the lower price makes children cheaper and fertility recovers. The
question is whether this process stops at a positive new steady state. In the
model below it does: long-run fertility returns to replacement, but population
and the house price are both lower.

\section*{Environment}

\textbf{Families and demography.} At date $t$ there is a mass $L_t$ of adult
families. Population is therefore measured in family units. Each family chooses
the number of children $n$. The number $\bar n>0$ is replacement fertility; it
can incorporate survival and the rate at which children form adult families.

\textbf{Preferences.} A family has income $y$ and values consumption $c$,
housing for the adults $h$, and children according to
\begin{equation}
 u(c,h,n;\beta)=c+\alpha\log h+\beta\log n,
 \qquad \alpha>0,\quad \beta>0.
 \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\beta$ measures the desire for children. The experiment is a
permanent decline from $\beta_0$ to $\beta_1<\beta_0$. Quasi-linearity is a
deliberate simplification: it shuts down income effects and leaves the
housing-price channel visible in one line.

\textbf{Housing requirements.} Each child uses $a>0$ additional units of
housing and $b\geq0$ units of the consumption good. If $p$ is the price of one
unit of housing, the full cost of a child is therefore $b+ap$. A family that
chooses $(h,n)$ occupies $h+an$ units in total.

\textbf{Housing supply.} The baseline treats housing as a one-period good, so
its service price and purchase price coincide. Suppliers provide
\begin{equation}
 H^s(p)=A p^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad A>0,\quad \varepsilon\geq0.
 \label{eq:housing_supply}
\end{equation}
The case $\varepsilon=0$ is a fixed supply. A durable housing stock is discussed
after the main argument.

\section*{The family's problem}

Taking the house price as given, a family solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{c,h,n}\; c+\alpha\log h+\beta\log n
 \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
 c+ph+(b+ap)n=y.
 \label{eq:family_problem}
\end{equation}
The budget makes the mechanism explicit: housing enters the child decision
through $ap$, the housing cost of accommodating one more child.

The interior choices are
\begin{equation}
 h(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p},
 \qquad
 n(p;\beta)=\frac{\beta}{b+ap},
 \qquad
 c=y-\alpha-\beta.
 \label{eq:family_demands}
\end{equation}
We assume $y>\alpha+\beta_0$, so consumption is positive before and after the
change. The child decision in \eqref{eq:family_demands} has the two signs the
argument needs. A lower $\beta$ reduces children at every price; holding
$\beta$ fixed, a lower house price raises children.

\newpage
\section*{The housing market}

At population $L$, total housing demand is the number of families times the
housing used by each family. Market clearing therefore requires
\begin{equation}
 A p^{\varepsilon}
 =L\left[\frac{\alpha}{p}
 +\frac{a\beta}{b+ap}\right].
 \label{eq:housing_clearing}
\end{equation}
The first term inside brackets is adult housing; the second is housing used by
children. For any $(L,\beta)$, equation \eqref{eq:housing_clearing} determines
one price. Denote it by $p=P(L;\beta)$.

This price schedule has two simple properties:
\begin{equation}
 P_L(L;\beta)>0,
 \qquad
 P_\beta(L;\beta)>0.
 \label{eq:price_schedule_signs}
\end{equation}
More families raise housing demand and therefore the price. A stronger desire
for children also raises the price because each family demands more
child-related housing. Thus the fall from $\beta_0$ to $\beta_1$ shifts the
entire price schedule downward. This is the first panel of Figure 1: for every
current population $L$, the housing market supplies a price $p$.

\section*{Population dynamics and steady states}

The fertility decision changes population slowly. The following reduced-form
law captures that demographic adjustment:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dot L_t}{L_t}
 =\lambda\left[
 \frac{n(P(L_t;\beta);\beta)}{\bar n}-1
 \right],
 \qquad \lambda>0.
 \label{eq:population_law}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\lambda$ determines the speed at which a fertility gap changes
the adult population. At each date, the housing market first maps $L_t$ into
$p_t=P(L_t;\beta)$; the family then maps $p_t$ into $n_t=n(p_t;\beta)$; and
fertility relative to $\bar n$ determines the direction of population change.

A positive steady state requires $n(p^*;\beta)=\bar n$. The child decision
therefore determines the steady-state price:
\begin{equation}
 p^*(\beta)=\frac{\beta/\bar n-b}{a}.
 \label{eq:steady_price}
\end{equation}
This price is positive when $\beta>b\bar n$. We assume
$\beta_1>b\bar n$, so a positive steady state exists after the preference
change. If this condition fails, even free housing cannot restore replacement
fertility and the closed population eventually disappears.

At replacement, a family uses $\alpha/p^*+a\bar n$ units of housing. Housing
clearing then determines the steady-state population:
\begin{equation}
 L^*(\beta)
 =\frac{A[p^*(\beta)]^{\varepsilon}}
 {\alpha/p^*(\beta)+a\bar n}.
 \label{eq:steady_population}
\end{equation}
The population supported by the housing market is increasing in the price: a
higher price brings forth weakly more housing and reduces adult housing used by
each family. Hence the permanent preference change has the long-run effects
\begin{equation}
 \beta_1<\beta_0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p_1<p_0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 L_1<L_0.
 \label{eq:long_run_comparison}
\end{equation}

\newpage
\section*{The transition and the two diagrams}

Suppose the economy begins at the old steady state
$E_0=(L_0,p_0,\bar n)$. When $\beta$ falls, population cannot jump, but the
housing market can reprice immediately. At the fixed population $L_0$, lower
child-related housing demand moves the price to
$\widetilde p=P(L_0;\beta_1)<p_0$.

The impact price does not fall all the way to its new steady-state value. Since
$L_0>L_1$ and the new price schedule slopes upward,
$\widetilde p=P(L_0;\beta_1)>P(L_1;\beta_1)=p_1$. The new child decision is
therefore still below replacement:
\begin{equation}
 p_1<\widetilde p<p_0,
 \qquad
 \widetilde n\equiv n(\widetilde p;\beta_1)<\bar n.
 \label{eq:impact_ordering}
\end{equation}
The initial price decline cushions the fertility decline but does not eliminate
it. Population consequently begins to fall. As $L_t$ falls, the economy moves
down the new housing-market schedule, so $p_t$ falls further. Moving left along
the new child-demand curve raises fertility from $\widetilde n$ toward
$\bar n$. If population were below $L_1$, all signs would reverse. The new
steady state is therefore globally stable in this one-state version of the
model.

\newpage
\section*{Reading the figure}

The panels contain different economic objects. Panel (a) is a market-clearing
schedule: it combines housing demand and supply and reports the equilibrium
price associated with each population. Panel (b) is the family's child demand
itself: the house price is on the horizontal axis, children are on the vertical
axis, and the curve slopes downward. Price is therefore the output of the first
panel and the input into the second.

Figure \ref{fig:feedback} separates three logically different movements:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{The preference shift.} In Panel (b), lower $\beta$ shifts the
 child decision down at every price. In Panel (a), the same change reduces
 child-related housing demand and shifts the market-clearing price down at
 every population.
 \item \textbf{The impact response.} Population is still $L_0$, so Panel (a)
 gives the intermediate price $\widetilde p$. Panel (b) then gives
 $\widetilde n<\bar n$. This is point $I$.
 \item \textbf{The demographic response.} Below-replacement fertility reduces
 $L$. The price moves down the new curve in Panel (a); the lower price moves
 fertility up the new curve in Panel (b). Adjustment stops at $E_1$, where
 fertility again equals $\bar n$.
\end{enumerate}

The diagrams can also be read backward to compare steady states. Replacement
fertility in Panel (b) selects $p_0$ before the change and $p_1$ after it.
Those two prices select $L_0$ and $L_1$ in Panel (a). This is why the same figure
shows both the transition mechanism and the long-run comparative static.

\section*{Durable housing}

The impact jump in Figure \ref{fig:feedback} uses the one-period housing supply
in \eqref{eq:housing_supply}. A durable model must distinguish the service
price $r_t$, which enters the family's decisions, from the asset price $q_t$,
which governs construction. With a predetermined stock $H_t$, the corresponding
conditions would be
\begin{equation}
 H_t=L_t\left[h(r_t)+a n(r_t;\beta)\right],
 \qquad
 \dot H_t=X(q_t)-\delta H_t,
 \qquad
 r^*=(\rho+\delta)q^*.
 \label{eq:durable_extension}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho$ is the interest rate and the last equality is the stationary user
cost relation. In a steady state, $H=X(q)/\delta$ and service and asset prices
move in the same direction. Choosing stationary construction so that the
resulting supply of services is $A r^\varepsilon$ leaves
\eqref{eq:steady_price}--\eqref{eq:steady_population} unchanged. The transition
is no longer summarized by a single curve $P(L;\beta)$ because both $L_t$ and
$H_t$ are predetermined states. Thus the simple model establishes the two
steady states and the feedback sign, not the exact time path of a durable asset
price.
