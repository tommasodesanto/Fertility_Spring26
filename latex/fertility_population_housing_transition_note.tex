\begin{center}
{\LARGE\bfseries Fertility, Population, and House Prices}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\large A simple theory of reproductive equilibrium}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small August 12, 2026}
\end{center}

\vspace{-2pt}
House prices and fertility are jointly determined in the long run. Children
need space, so expensive housing reduces the demand for children. But children
become the next generation of households, so fertility also determines future
housing demand. Closing this loop changes the incidence of permanent shocks.
A decline in the desire for children produces below-replacement fertility only
during the adjustment: the new steady state has effective replacement again,
but a smaller population and a lower house price. Conversely, an expansion of
housing supply raises fertility only during the adjustment: in the new steady
state the price returns to its old level and the additional housing is occupied
by a larger population.

The model below makes this argument with one family problem and one market. It
delivers three results. First, reproductive stationarity selects the house
price and housing clearing selects population. Second, a positive steady state
exists only when the part of child costs that cheaper housing can remove is
large enough relative to the fertility shortfall. Third, permanent shocks are
``demographically capitalized'': child-demand shocks move prices and
population rather than long-run fertility, while housing-supply shocks move
population rather than the long-run price. None of these results depends on a
particular calibration.

\section*{1. Environment}

\textbf{Families.} At date $t$ there is a mass $L_t$ of adult families. Each
family chooses consumption $c$, housing for the adults $h$, and the number of
children $n$. Population is measured in family units.

\textbf{Preferences.} A family has income $y$ and preferences
\begin{equation}
 u(c,h,n)=c+\alpha\log h+\theta\log n,
 \qquad \alpha>0,\quad \theta>0.
 \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\theta$ measures the value of children. Quasi-linearity removes
income effects and leaves the housing mechanism visible in closed form.

\textbf{Child requirements.} Each child costs $q\geq0$ units of ordinary goods
and requires $a>0$ additional units of housing. A family with choices $(h,n)$
therefore occupies $h+an$ units of housing. If $p$ is the price of one unit of
housing services, the full resource cost of a child is $q+ap$.

\textbf{Housing.} Aggregate housing services are supplied according to
\begin{equation}
 H^s(p)=A p^{\varepsilon},
 \qquad A>0,\quad \varepsilon\geq0.
 \label{eq:supply}
\end{equation}
The case $\varepsilon=0$ is a fixed stock. The baseline treats $p$ as a
one-period housing cost. If the user-cost wedge is fixed, the asset price of a
house moves in the same direction.

\textbf{Demography.} The number $\bar n>0$ is effective replacement fertility:
after survival and household formation, a family that has $\bar n$ children
produces exactly one family in the next generation. There is no immigration or
outside entry in the baseline economy.

\section*{2. The family's problem}

Taking $p$ as given, a family solves
\begin{equation}
 \max_{c,h,n}\;c+\alpha\log h+\theta\log n
 \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
 c+ph+(q+ap)n=y.
 \label{eq:family_problem}
\end{equation}
The term $ap$ is the housing bill generated by one additional child. The
interior choices are
\begin{equation}
 h(p)=\frac{\alpha}{p},
 \qquad
 n(p;\theta)=\frac{\theta}{q+ap},
 \qquad
 c=y-\alpha-\theta.
 \label{eq:demands}
\end{equation}
We assume $y>\alpha+\theta$. A higher house price reduces both adult space and
children; a lower value of children shifts the child decision down at every
price. Total housing demand per family is
\begin{equation}
 d(p;\theta)=\frac{\alpha}{p}+\frac{a\theta}{q+ap}.
 \label{eq:per_family_demand}
\end{equation}

\section*{3. Reproductive equilibrium}

For a given population $L$, the house price clears the housing market:
\begin{equation}
 A p^{\varepsilon}=L\left[
 \frac{\alpha}{p}+\frac{a\theta}{q+ap}
 \right].
 \label{eq:housing_clearing}
\end{equation}
Supply rises and demand falls with $p$, so for every $(L,\theta)$ there is one
market-clearing price, denoted $p=P(L;\theta)$. More families raise this price,
while a lower value of children reduces child-related housing demand:
\begin{equation}
 P_L(L;\theta)>0,
 \qquad
 P_\theta(L;\theta)>0.
 \label{eq:price_schedule_signs}
\end{equation}
Thus equation~\eqref{eq:housing_clearing} is a map from population into the
house price, not yet a condition that determines population.

\begin{definition}[Reproductive equilibrium]
A reproductive equilibrium is a house price $p^*>0$, a population $L^*>0$,
and family choices $(c^*,h^*,n^*)$ such that families optimize, the housing
market clears, and fertility is at replacement, $n^*=\bar n$.
\end{definition}

The order of equilibrium determination is the key. Reproduction selects the
price at which a family is willing to have exactly $\bar n$ children. Housing
clearing then determines how many families can live at that price.

\begin{proposition}[Existence, uniqueness, and comparative statics]
\label{prop:equilibrium}
A unique positive reproductive equilibrium exists if and only if
$\theta>q\bar n$. It is
\begin{equation}
 p^*(\theta)=\frac{\theta/\bar n-q}{a},
 \qquad
 L^*(\theta)=
 \frac{A[p^*(\theta)]^{\varepsilon+1}}
 {\alpha+a\bar n p^*(\theta)}.
 \label{eq:steady_state}
\end{equation}
A permanent decline in $\theta$ that preserves existence lowers both $p^*$ and
$L^*$, while fertility remains $\bar n$.
\end{proposition}

The existence condition has a direct meaning. Even if housing were free, a
child would still cost $q$. If $\theta\leq q\bar n$, eliminating the entire
housing bill cannot restore replacement fertility. When an equilibrium does
exist, the population supported by the housing market is increasing in its
reproductive price:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dL^*}{dp^*}
 =\frac{A(p^*)^\varepsilon
 \bigl[(\varepsilon+1)\alpha+\varepsilon a\bar n p^*\bigr]}
 {(\alpha+a\bar n p^*)^2}>0.
 \label{eq:scale_slope}
\end{equation}
A lower price brings forth weakly less housing and induces each family to use
more adult space. Both forces reduce the population that clears the market.

\section*{4. From one steady state to another}

To describe adjustment without adding cohort structure, let population change
with the gap between chosen and replacement fertility:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dot L_t}{L_t}
 =\lambda\left[
 \frac{n(P(L_t;\theta);\theta)}{\bar n}-1
 \right],
 \qquad \lambda>0.
 \label{eq:population_law}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\lambda$ compresses the generational lag. This law is useful for
the direction of adjustment; it is not a substitute for an age-structured
transition.

\begin{proposition}[Impact and adjustment]
\label{prop:dynamics}
Suppose the economy begins at the reproductive equilibrium associated with
$\theta_0$, and $\theta$ falls permanently to $\theta_1<\theta_0$, with both
values satisfying Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}. Let
$\widetilde p=P(L_0;\theta_1)$ be the market-clearing impact price, before
population changes. Then
\begin{equation}
 p_1<\widetilde p<p_0,
 \qquad
 n(\widetilde p;\theta_1)<\bar n,
 \qquad
 L_1<L_0.
 \label{eq:impact_ordering}
\end{equation}
Under equation~\eqref{eq:population_law}, population and the house price fall
monotonically from the impact point to the new equilibrium, while fertility
rises monotonically back to replacement. The new equilibrium is globally
stable for every initial $L>0$.
\end{proposition}

Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} displays the two maps separately. The left panel
answers: for any current population, what price clears the housing market? The
right panel answers: at that price, how many children does a family choose?

With durable housing, the stock cannot jump and expected capital gains enter
the user cost. The exact impact response and the path may then overshoot or
display cohort echoes. The steady-state results survive if the construction
technology reproduces equation~\eqref{eq:supply} in the long run; the monotone
arrows in Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} belong to the one-state baseline.

The timing convention also matters for stability. If children replace families
one generation at a time, the analogue of~\eqref{eq:population_law} is
$L_{t+1}=L_tR(P(L_t))$, where $R=1$ at replacement. The local stability
condition is
\begin{equation}
 \left|1+\varepsilon_{R,p}\varepsilon_{p,L}\right|<1,
 \qquad
 \varepsilon_{R,p}<0,\qquad \varepsilon_{p,L}>0.
 \label{eq:discrete_stability}
\end{equation}
Both elasticities are evaluated at the steady state. Convergence alternates
when $-2<\varepsilon_{R,p}\varepsilon_{p,L}<-1$, and the equilibrium loses
local stability when $\varepsilon_{R,p}\varepsilon_{p,L}\leq-2$. Monotone
convergence is a result of the continuous-time illustration, not a general
prediction.

\section*{5. Can cheaper housing restore replacement?}

The existence condition can be stated in terms of two economically meaningful
objects. Suppose that, at the inherited price $p_0$, the new child decision is
below replacement by the fraction $\gamma$:
\begin{equation}
 n(p_0;\theta_1)=(1-\gamma)\bar n,
 \qquad 0<\gamma<1.
 \label{eq:fertility_gap}
\end{equation}
At that same price, housing represents the following share of the full resource
cost of a child:
\begin{equation}
 s_H\equiv\frac{ap_0}{q+ap_0}.
 \label{eq:housing_share}
\end{equation}
The share $s_H$ is the largest fraction of the child's cost that a decline in
the house price can eliminate.

\begin{proposition}[The stabilization threshold]
\label{prop:threshold}
After the change in equation~\eqref{eq:fertility_gap}, a positive reproductive
equilibrium exists if and only if
\begin{equation}
 \gamma<s_H.
 \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
When it exists, the price required to restore replacement is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{p_1}{p_0}=1-\frac{\gamma}{s_H}.
 \label{eq:price_gap_formula}
\end{equation}
If $p_0$ was the old reproductive price, the associated population change is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{L_1}{L_0}
 =\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^{\varepsilon+1}
 \frac{\alpha+a\bar n p_0}{\alpha+a\bar n p_1}.
 \label{eq:population_ratio}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This result separates the sign of the mechanism from its strength. Cheaper
housing always raises fertility, but it can stabilize a closed population only
if housing is a sufficiently large part of the marginal child's cost. For
example, if housing represents 25 percent of that cost and the direct fertility
shortfall is 10 percent, restoring replacement requires a 40 percent price
decline: $p_1/p_0=1-0.10/0.25=0.60$. If the fertility shortfall were 30 percent,
no nonnegative house price could restore replacement. The economy would need
another adjustment margin, such as immigration, a change in goods costs, or a
further change in preferences.

\section*{6. Demographic capitalization}

Reproductive equilibrium reverses the usual way permanent policy effects are
reported. Long-run fertility cannot absorb a permanent shock because it must
return to replacement. Prices and population absorb it instead.

\begin{proposition}[Incidence of supply and child-cost changes]
\label{prop:capitalization}
Consider an initial reproductive equilibrium.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item A pure increase in housing supply from $A_0$ to $A_1$ leaves the
 reproductive price, family choices, and fertility unchanged in the long run,
 while population changes in direct proportion:
 \begin{equation}
  p_1^*=p_0^*,
  \qquad n_1^*=n_0^*=\bar n,
  \qquad \frac{L_1^*}{L_0^*}=\frac{A_1}{A_0}.
  \label{eq:supply_incidence}
 \end{equation}
 \item A per-child subsidy $s$ that lowers the goods cost from $q$ to $q-s$
 raises the reproductive house price one for one with the subsidy per unit of
 child space:
 \begin{equation}
  p^*(s)=\frac{\theta/\bar n-q+s}{a},
  \qquad \frac{dp^*}{ds}=\frac{1}{a}.
  \label{eq:subsidy_incidence}
 \end{equation}
 The full child cost at the reproductive equilibrium,
 $q-s+ap^*(s)=\theta/\bar n$, is unchanged.
 Because $L^*$ rises with its reproductive price, the reduction in the
 nonhousing cost of children also raises long-run population.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The first part is a demographic analogue of an open-city result. More housing
makes the economy temporarily cheaper; families respond with above-replacement
fertility; population grows until the original price is restored. In the long
run, housing construction is a population policy rather than an affordability
policy. The second part is a Malthusian capitalization result. Making children
cheaper raises fertility on impact, but population and housing demand expand
until the subsidy is absorbed by a higher house price.

These are incidence statements, not welfare theorems. Comparing total welfare
across economies with different numbers of people requires a population-ethics
criterion. The propositions instead describe prices, quantities, and family
choices for the families present in each equilibrium.

\section*{7. What survives in a richer lifecycle model?}

The representative family is not essential to the equilibrium logic. In a
lifecycle model, let $\mathcal M(p;z)$ be the demographic transition operator
at housing cost $p$: it ages surviving households and adds the mature entrants
generated by their fertility choices. The variable $z$ collects preferences
or policies. Let $R(p;z)$ be the dominant eigenvalue of $\mathcal M(p;z)$.
This is effective net reproduction: $R=1$, rather than a particular total
fertility rate, is the condition for a stationary population. The associated
eigenvector gives the normalized age, family-size, tenure, and wealth
distribution.

Let $D(p;z)$ be housing demand per adult family in that normalized distribution
and let $H^s(p)$ be aggregate housing supply. A reproductive equilibrium solves
only two aggregate equations:
\begin{equation}
 R(p^*;z)=1,
 \qquad
 L^*D(p^*;z)=H^s(p^*).
 \label{eq:general_closure}
\end{equation}
Survival, the timing of births, child maturation, and household formation all
belong inside $\mathcal M$. Tenure choice, borrowing constraints, and
family-size composition affect both $R$ and $D$. The first equation selects the
price and the shape of the stationary distribution; the second scales that
distribution to clear the housing market.

The operator condition has an exact entrant-flow representation in the
single-entry-age architecture used by the quantitative model. Let $s_j$ be the
cumulative probability that an entrant survives from entry through adult age
$j$, with $s_0=1$. Let $B(p;z)$ be the mature entrant households generated per
adult family and per period when the unique normalized fixed-price distribution
is sustained by its required entrant flow $E$.

\begin{lemma}[Entrant-flow representation]
\label{lem:entrant_flow}
Suppose adult survival depends only on age, all households enter at age zero,
and locally generated mature entrants have the same normalized entry-state
distribution as the entrants used to construct the fixed-price distribution.
The entrant flow required per adult family is
\begin{equation}
 E=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}s_j\right)^{-1}.
 \label{eq:entrant_requirement}
\end{equation}
For every $z$ that preserves these assumptions, the stationary conditions
$R(p;z)=1$ and $B(p;z)=E$ are equivalent.
\end{lemma}

The lemma connects the general theory directly to model accounting. $E$ is the
entrant input needed to maintain one unit of adult population, while $B$ is the
entrant output produced by that population. Neither object is conventional
total fertility: survival, birth timing, maturation, and household formation
all intervene between births and mature entrants.
If entrant wealth, income, or location shares are instead imposed externally,
$B=E$ closes aggregate population accounting but does not by itself show that
the entire entrant-state distribution reproduces itself.

The closure yields one exact invariance, but its object is effective
reproduction. Conditional on existence, $R=1$---equivalently, $B=E$ under the
lemma's assumptions---in every reproductive steady state. If adult survival and
the mapping from births into mature entrant households are invariant across
policies, aggregate lifetime births per entering household are also pinned at
model-specific replacement. Absent those restrictions, neither conventional
total fertility nor completed fertility is fixed. Even when aggregate lifetime
births are fixed, the accounting identity alone does not pin birth timing,
childlessness, family-size composition, measured fertility, or the stock of
children living at home.

If $R$ is continuous, crosses one, and is strictly decreasing in the housing
cost, the first equation has a unique root. A shock that shifts reproduction
down at every price lowers that root. The population effect is
\begin{equation}
 d\log L^*
 =\bigl(\eta_S+\eta_D\bigr)d\log p^*
 -\left.\frac{\partial\log D}{\partial z}\right|_{p}dz,
 \label{eq:general_population_change}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_S=d\log H^s/d\log p\geq0$ and
$\eta_D=-\partial\log D/\partial\log p\geq0$. The first term is the mechanism
in the simple model. The second is a direct composition effect: a preference
or policy change can alter housing demand even at a fixed price. Thus the price
result is general, while the population sign must be verified when household
heterogeneity is rich.

The root can be theoretically well defined and still weakly disciplined
quantitatively. Consider a scalar preference or policy shifter $\zeta$ that does
not alter survival, entry timing, or the maintained entry-state distribution.
If $R$ and $B$ are continuously differentiable and $R_p$ and $B_p$ are nonzero,
Lemma~\ref{lem:entrant_flow} implies that both equations define the same local
root. Implicit differentiation therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dp^*}{d\zeta}
 =-\frac{R_\zeta(p^*;z)}{R_p(p^*;z)}
 =-\frac{B_\zeta(p^*;z)}{B_p(p^*;z)},
 \label{eq:root_identification}
\end{equation}
where the second equality uses Lemma~\ref{lem:entrant_flow}. Even if stationary
fertility moments pin the height of the reproduction schedule at the observed
price, they do not generally pin its slope. Credible variation in housing costs
that measures a fertility response directly disciplines $R_p$ and therefore
the distance and comparative statics of the reproductive root.

One result is especially robust. If housing supply does not enter household
decisions except through $p$, and wages, fiscal transfers, and local amenities
do not depend on population scale, a pure shift of $H^s$ cannot change the
first equation in~\eqref{eq:general_closure}. It changes long-run population
but not the reproductive price or normalized household distribution. This is
the general version of Proposition~\ref{prop:capitalization}. If instead land
rents are rebated, agglomeration changes wages, or policy costs vary with
population, that triangular separation can fail.

\subsection*{A small outside inflow}

The closed economy can be nested in a broader demographic balance. Let
$\varrho\in(0,1]$ be the retention rate of locally generated entrants and let
$M\geq0$ be an outside entrant flow in levels. Stationarity requires
\begin{equation}
 L E=\varrho L B(p;z)+M.
 \label{eq:inflow_balance}
\end{equation}
With positive inflow, this equation and housing clearing jointly determine
population and the housing cost. The national closed economy is the special
case $(\varrho,M)=(1,0)$, in which population cancels from
equation~\eqref{eq:inflow_balance} and reproduction selects the price.

\begin{proposition}[The closed economy as a regular limit]
\label{prop:inflow_limit}
Set $\varrho=1$ and write the housing-clearing scale as
$C(p)=H^s(p)/D(p;z)$. Suppose $B$ is continuous and strictly decreasing,
$B(p^*;z)=E$, and $C$ is continuous, positive, and strictly increasing on a
nondegenerate compact interval $[p^*,\bar p]$. For every sufficiently small
$M>0$, the inflow economy has a unique equilibrium $(p_M,L_M)$ on that interval,
with
\begin{equation}
 (p_M,L_M)\longrightarrow\bigl(p^*,C(p^*)\bigr)
 \qquad\text{as}\qquad M\downarrow0.
 \label{eq:inflow_limit}
\end{equation}
If instead $B(p;z)\leq E-\delta$ at every feasible price for some $\delta>0$,
then every inflow equilibrium satisfies $L_M\leq M/\delta$ and population
converges to zero as inflow vanishes.
\end{proposition}

Thus a small inflow is a regular perturbation of the closed economy only when
a positive reproductive root exists. If cheaper housing can never restore
replacement, removing the inflow produces demographic collapse rather than a
nearby positive steady state. If $\varrho<1$ is held fixed as $M$ vanishes, the
limiting condition is instead $\varrho B(p;z)=E$; a literally closed national
economy requires retention to converge to one as well.

The geographic boundary must also be explicit. At the national level, or in a
closed region, reproduction can determine long-run population. In an open city,
migration normally adjusts much faster and adds a separate spatial-equilibrium
condition; the two-equation closure does not apply unchanged.

\section*{8. Interpretation and contribution}

The feedback itself has a close antecedent. In the spatial lifecycle model of
\citet{coeurdacierCombesGobillonOswaldFertility2023}, children require housing,
households choose fertility and location, and demographic composition evolves
recursively; the authors describe the housing market as an automatic stabilizer
of fertility. Their public 2023 model studies rich multicity transition
dynamics. The narrower purpose here is analytical: isolate the stationary
equilibrium order, obtain a closed-form existence threshold, and characterize
the long-run incidence of child-cost and supply changes. These results must be
checked against any later version of that unpublished project before making a
priority claim.

Earlier analytical models already place children inside housing demand and let
house prices clear endogenously \citep{dayFertilityHousing2015,
dayGuestFertilityFemaleWages2016}. They establish the household price--fertility
mechanism and show why housing-supply elasticity matters. They do not use
reproductive stationarity to determine aggregate population, which is the
additional equilibrium margin isolated here.

The model combines three familiar ideas. Fertility theory makes children a
household choice
\citep{barroFertilityChoiceModel1989,sommerFertilityChoiceLife2016}; housing
models make population and housing supply determine prices
\citep{glaeserEconomicImplicationsHousing2018}; and spatial equilibrium lets
an extensive population margin absorb local shocks
\citep{rosenWageBasedIndexes1979,robackWagesRentsQuality1982}. Here the
extensive margin is national reproduction rather than migration. Replacement
fertility plays the role usually played by an outside utility: it selects the
price at which the population is stationary, while the housing market selects
the scale of that population.

The analytical contribution would therefore be narrower, but sharper, than the
feedback itself:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item it separates reproductive stationarity, which selects the housing cost,
 from housing clearing, which selects population scale;
 \item it turns a direct fertility shortfall and the housing share of child
 costs into the sharp stabilization condition~\eqref{eq:threshold}; and
 \item it changes the long-run interpretation of policy: child-cost policy is
 capitalized into housing prices and population, while housing supply is
 capitalized into population.
\end{enumerate}

The interpretation of currently low fertility remains disciplined. The model
allows it to be a transition toward a lower reproductive steady state, but it
does not establish that this is the transition observed in the data. That
reading requires the preference or child-cost change to be recent, the
stabilization inequality to hold, and the inherited age distribution and
housing stock to be consistent with the proposed path. The theory contributes
the equilibrium endpoints, the direction of the feedback, and a condition for
whether a positive endpoint exists. A calendar-time quantitative transition is
a separate exercise.

Durable housing is the most important extension for that exercise. As
\citet{glaeserGyourkoUrbanDecline2005} show, negative demand shocks can lower
prices much faster than the housing stock and population decline. Their result
reinforces the distinction in this note: the reproductive endpoints may be
analytically simple even when the transition between them is slow and
asymmetric.

\section*{Appendix. Proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}]
The first-order conditions of~\eqref{eq:family_problem} give
$h=\alpha/p$ and $n=\theta/(q+ap)$. Reproductive stationarity therefore
requires $p^*=(\theta/\bar n-q)/a$, which is positive if and only if
$\theta>q\bar n$. Substituting $n=\bar n$ and $h=\alpha/p^*$ into housing
clearing gives the expression for $L^*$. It is positive and unique. Finally,
$p^*$ rises with $\theta$, and equation~\eqref{eq:scale_slope} shows that
$L^*$ rises with $p^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:dynamics}]
In~\eqref{eq:housing_clearing}, supply is increasing and per-family demand is
decreasing in $p$. Hence the clearing price is unique, rises with $L$, and rises
with $\theta$. At the inherited population, $\theta_1<\theta_0$ implies
$\widetilde p<p_0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium} gives $L_1<L_0$; because
$P$ rises with population, $\widetilde p=P(L_0;\theta_1)>
P(L_1;\theta_1)=p_1$. Since $n$ falls with $p$ and
$n(p_1;\theta_1)=\bar n$, it follows that
$n(\widetilde p;\theta_1)<\bar n$.

For stability, define
$\Phi(L)=n(P(L;\theta_1);\theta_1)/\bar n-1$. The price schedule rises with
$L$ and child demand falls with price, so $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing. It is
zero only at $L_1$, positive below $L_1$, and negative above it. Equation
\eqref{eq:population_law} therefore keeps a solution between its initial value
and $L_1$ and moves it monotonically. Its limit must be the unique zero $L_1$.
The monotonicity of $p$ and $n$ follows from their respective slopes.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}]
Equation~\eqref{eq:fertility_gap} implies
$\theta_1=(1-\gamma)\bar n(q+ap_0)$. At the new reproductive price,
$\theta_1=\bar n(q+ap_1)$. Equating the two expressions and using
$s_H=ap_0/(q+ap_0)$ gives
$p_1/p_0=1-\gamma/s_H$. The price is positive if and only if
$\gamma<s_H$. Substituting $p_0$ and $p_1$ into
equation~\eqref{eq:steady_state} gives equation~\eqref{eq:population_ratio}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:capitalization}]
The reproductive price in~\eqref{eq:steady_state} does not contain the housing
supply shifter $A$, while population is proportional to $A$. This proves the
first part. Replacing $q$ by $q-s$ in the same reproductive condition gives
equation~\eqref{eq:subsidy_incidence}. Substitution shows that the full child
cost remains $\theta/\bar n$. Equation~\eqref{eq:scale_slope} and
$dp^*/ds>0$ imply that population rises.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:entrant_flow}]
With entrant flow $E$ and age-only survival, the mass of adult families of age
$j$ is $E s_j$. Normalizing the total adult mass to one gives
$1=E\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}s_j$, which proves
equation~\eqref{eq:entrant_requirement}. At a fixed price, the normalized
distribution can reproduce itself without an outside injection if and only if
its mature entrant output $B$ equals its required entrant input $E$ and, by the
matching-entry-state assumption, has the same composition. This is equivalent
to the demographic operator mapping that positive distribution into itself.
Under the maintained uniqueness condition, its dominant eigenvalue is therefore
one. The converse follows by normalizing the positive eigenvector associated
with $R=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:inflow_limit}]
For $p>p^*$, the demographic balance defines
$L_d(p)=M/[E-B(p;z)]$. It is strictly decreasing and diverges as
$p\downarrow p^*$, while $C(p)$ is positive and strictly increasing. At the
upper end of the compact interval, $L_d<C$ for all sufficiently small $M$.
Continuity and the opposing slopes therefore give a unique intersection.
That intersection cannot remain bounded away from $p^*$ as $M$ vanishes,
because $L_d$ would then converge to zero while $C$ remains positive. Hence
$p_M\to p^*$ and housing clearing gives $L_M\to C(p^*)$. In the no-root case,
equation~\eqref{eq:inflow_balance} gives
$L_M=M/[E-B(p_M;z)]\leq M/\delta$, proving the second claim.
\end{proof}
