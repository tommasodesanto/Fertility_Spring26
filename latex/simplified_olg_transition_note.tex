\section{Environment}

\textbf{Agents.} Time is discrete and one period is one generation. At date
$t$, the economy contains $Y_t$ young households and $O_t$ old households. A
young household has type $i=(y_i,b_i)$, drawn from a fixed distribution $F$ of
income and liquid wealth. A young renter becomes an old renter, a young owner
becomes an old incumbent, and every old household exits at the end of the
period. Entry wealth is exogenous: estates are not assigned to particular
descendants.

\textbf{Preferences.} A young household values consumption, space net of its
children's needs, and completed fertility:
\begin{equation}
 u_t^Y(c,h,n)=\log(c-\chi n)+\alpha\log(h-\kappa n)
 +\vartheta_t\log n .
 \label{eq:young_utility}
\end{equation}
Each child requires $\chi>0$ units of goods and $\kappa>0$ units of space. An
old household values consumption, housing, and a warm-glow estate:
\begin{equation}
 u^O(c^O,h^O,e)=\log c^O+\gamma\log h^O+\omega_B\log e .
 \label{eq:old_utility}
\end{equation}
The estate affects the old household's utility but does not determine the
number or wealth of next period's young households.

\textbf{Housing.} There is a fixed stock $\bar H$ of divisible floorspace. A
renter can occupy at most $\bar h^R$, while an owner can occupy at most
$\bar h^O>\bar h^R$. The rental price is $r_t$ and the asset price is $P_t$.
With one-period bond price $q$, depreciation $\delta$, and property-tax rate
$\tau_t^p$, no arbitrage requires:
\begin{equation}
 r_t=\bigl(q^{-1}+\delta+\tau_t^p\bigr)P_t-P_{t+1}.
 \label{eq:dynamic_user_cost}
\end{equation}
The familiar stationary relation
$r=(q^{-1}-1+\delta+\tau^p)P$ is the special case $P_{t+1}=P_t$.

\textbf{Housing institutions.} A buyer can finance at most a share
$\phi\in(0,1)$ of the purchase price and must therefore post
$(1-\phi)P_t h$ from current liquid wealth. Selling in old age realizes a
nominal capital gain, while death receives stepped-up basis. If nominal prices
grow at rate $\varpi$, the real tax due per unit sold at date $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
 \ell_t=\tau^g\left[P_t-\frac{P_{t-1}}{1+\varpi}\right]_+ .
 \label{eq:dynamic_gain}
\end{equation}
At a stationary real price this becomes
$\bar\ell=\tau^gP\varpi/(1+\varpi)$, exactly the wedge in the July model.
Tax receipts accrue to the government; any rebate is absorbed into the
exogenous nonhousing resources $y_i$ and does not alter the marginal
conditions below.

\textbf{Demography.} One child produces $\nu>0$ future young households after
survival and household formation. The scalar $\nu$ is the simple model's
counterpart of the quantitative model's person-to-household conversion.

\section{Household problems}

\subsection{Young households}

Conditional on tenure $m\in\{R,O\}$, a young household chooses consumption,
housing, completed fertility, and bond expenditure:
\begin{align}
 W_t^m(i)=\max_{c,h,n,b'}\;&u_t^Y(c,h,n)
 +\beta\left[\1\{m=R\}V_{t+1}^R(b_{t+1})
 +\1\{m=O\}V_{t+1}^O(b_{t+1},h)\right]
 \label{eq:young_problem}\\
 \text{subject to}\quad
 &c+r_t h+b'=y_i+b_i, \nonumber\\
 &h\leq \bar h^m,\qquad b'\geq0, \nonumber\\
 &\1\{m=O\}(1-\phi)P_t h\leq b_i, \nonumber\\
 &b_{t+1}=b'/q-\1\{m=O\}r_{t+1}h. \nonumber
\end{align}
The household chooses the tenure with the higher value. Its effective
family-housing caps are:
\begin{equation}
 H_t^R=\bar h^R,
 \qquad
 H_t^O(i)=\min\left\{\bar h^O,
 \frac{b_i}{(1-\phi)P_t}\right\}.
 \label{eq:effective_caps}
\end{equation}
Thus current liquid wealth, rather than the ability to service the flow cost,
can prevent a household from occupying family-sized owner housing.

\subsection{Old households}

An old incumbent arrives with liquid wealth $b$, a house $H^0$, and the tax
basis established when young. She chooses how much of the house to retain:
\begin{align}
 V_t^O(b,H^0)=\max_{c^O,h^O,e}\;&u^O(c^O,h^O,e)
 \label{eq:old_owner_problem}\\
 \text{subject to}\quad
 &c^O+e+r_t h^O=b+r_tH^0-\ell_t(H^0-h^O),
 \qquad 0\leq h^O\leq H^0. \nonumber
\end{align}
At an interior retention choice, her marginal value of retained space is
$r_t-\ell_t$. An old renter instead solves the same utility problem subject to
$c^O+e+r_th^O=b$ and values marginal housing at $r_t$.

The old owner's budget can be written as
$c^O+e+(r_t-\ell_t)h^O=b+(r_t-\ell_t)H^0$. Define her effective resources by
$\Omega_t^O=b+(r_t-\ell_t)H^0$ and let
$A=1+\gamma+\omega_B$. When the retention limit is slack and $r_t>\ell_t$, the log problem has
the closed-form allocation:
\begin{equation}
 c_t^O=\frac{\Omega_t^O}{A},
 \qquad
 h_t^O=\frac{\gamma\Omega_t^O}{A(r_t-\ell_t)},
 \qquad
 e_t=\frac{\omega_B\Omega_t^O}{A}.
 \label{eq:old_closed_form}
\end{equation}
The old incumbent behaves as if retained housing carried price
$r_t-\ell_t$. For an old renter, equation~\eqref{eq:old_closed_form} applies
with $\Omega_t^R=b$, housing price $r_t$, and no carried house.

\subsection{Young first-order conditions}

The link between the two periods is clearest after substituting the old
problem into the young problem. A young owner's next-period effective
resources are:
\begin{equation}
 \Omega_{t+1}^O
 =\underbrace{\left(\frac{b'}q-r_{t+1}h\right)}_{b_{t+1}}
 +\underbrace{(r_{t+1}-\ell_{t+1})h}_{
 \text{value of carried housing}}
 =\frac{b'}q-\ell_{t+1}h.
 \label{eq:owner_old_resources}
\end{equation}
The future housing service cancels the flow-equivalent liability carried into
old age; only the tax on a sale remains. A young renter instead has
$\Omega_{t+1}^R=b'/q$.

Let $\Lambda=\beta A$ be old-age claims on one unit of young marginal utility.
At an interior saving choice, the Euler equation and
equation~\eqref{eq:owner_old_resources} imply:
\begin{equation}
 b'=\Lambda(c-\chi n)+\1\{m=O\}q\ell_{t+1}h.
 \label{eq:saving_rule}
\end{equation}
The buyer saves the ordinary old-age claim plus the discounted future tax bill
attached to the house. At $b'=0$, the corresponding complementary-slackness
condition replaces equation~\eqref{eq:saving_rule}.

Define residual consumption $x=c-\chi n$, residual adult space
$s=h-\kappa n$, and the tenure-specific effective housing price
$r_t^R=r_t$ and $r_t^O=r_t+q\ell_{t+1}$. Substituting the saving rule into the
young budget gives the lifetime resource identity:
\begin{equation}
 y_i+b_i=(1+\Lambda)x+r_t^m s+
 \bigl(\chi+\kappa r_t^m\bigr)n.
 \label{eq:lifetime_resource_identity}
\end{equation}
Thus $\chi+\kappa r_t^m$ is the unconstrained full price of a child. When the
housing and financing constraints are slack, the log problem allocates
lifetime resources according to:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 D_t&=1+\alpha+\vartheta_t+\Lambda,
 &x_t^m&=\frac{y_i+b_i}{D_t},\\
 n_t^m&=\frac{\vartheta_t(y_i+b_i)}
 {D_t(\chi+\kappa r_t^m)},
 &h_t^m&=\frac{\alpha(y_i+b_i)}{D_t r_t^m}+\kappa n_t^m.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:young_unconstrained_policies}
\end{equation}
Higher housing costs reduce fertility because each child requires $\kappa$
units of space; they also reduce adult housing directly.

\newpage
To characterize constrained households, let $\lambda_t^m$ be the multiplier
on the young budget, $\eta_t^m$ the multiplier on the tenure-specific size
limit, and $\mu_t$ the multiplier on the owner's down-payment constraint. The
goods value of relaxing the active housing constraints is:
\begin{equation}
 \zeta_t^R=\frac{\eta_t^R}{\lambda_t^R},
 \qquad
 \zeta_t^O=
 \underbrace{\frac{\mu_t}{\lambda_t^O}(1-\phi)P_t}_{
 \text{financing gap}}
 +\underbrace{\frac{\eta_t^O}{\lambda_t^O}}_{
 \text{owner-size gap}}.
 \label{eq:access_gap_definition}
\end{equation}
The housing first-order condition then identifies the total access gap from
the household's bundle:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\alpha(c_t^m-\chi n_t^m)}{h_t^m-\kappa n_t^m}
 =r_t^m+\zeta_t^m,
 \qquad \zeta_t^m\geq0.
 \label{eq:access_gap}
\end{equation}
An unconstrained household has $\zeta_t^m=0$; a constrained household values
one more unit of space above its effective price by exactly $\zeta_t^m$.

Finally, the fertility first-order condition prices the marginal child in
units of current goods:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\vartheta_t(c_t^m-\chi n_t^m)}{n_t^m}
 =\chi+\kappa\bigl(r_t^m+\zeta_t^m\bigr).
 \label{eq:fertility_foc}
\end{equation}
A binding housing constraint therefore acts as a household-specific tax of
$\kappa\zeta_t^m$ on fertility.

\section{Demography, markets, and transition equilibrium}

Let $\bar n_t$ and $\bar h_t^Y$ denote average fertility and housing among the
young, integrating the policies in equation~\eqref{eq:young_problem} over
$F$. Let $\bar h_t^O$ denote average housing among the old distribution
generated by the preceding young cohort. Births are therefore:
\begin{equation}
 B_t=Y_t\bar n_t.
 \label{eq:births}
\end{equation}
The baseline closed economy and its open-market extension are nested in the
cohort law:
\begin{equation}
 Y_{t+1}=M_{t+1}+\rho\nu B_t,
 \qquad
 O_{t+1}=Y_t.
 \label{eq:cohort_law}
\end{equation}
The closed national economy sets $M_t=0$ and $\rho=1$. In the open extension,
$M_t$ is an outside inflow and $\rho\in[0,1]$ is the retention rate of locally
born future households.

Housing clears at every date rather than only at an endpoint:
\begin{equation}
 Y_t\bar h_t^Y+O_t\bar h_t^O=\bar H.
 \label{eq:housing_clearing}
\end{equation}
The clearing condition determines the price path jointly with household
expectations and the inherited cohort distribution.

\begin{definition}[Transition equilibrium]
Given the initial cohort masses, old-household distribution and tax bases, a
path of primitives, and a terminal condition when one is available, a
transition equilibrium is a sequence of prices, household policies,
distributions, births, and cohort masses such that: (i) young and old choices
solve their problems given the anticipated price path; (ii) the old
distribution is the push-forward of the preceding young cohort's tenure,
housing, and saving choices; (iii) equation~\eqref{eq:cohort_law} generates the
next young cohort; (iv) housing clears according to
equation~\eqref{eq:housing_clearing} at every date; and (v) asset pricing and
government accounting hold at every date.
\end{definition}

A transition equilibrium is a dated sequence, not by definition movement
between two steady states. Convergence to a new steady state is an additional
boundary condition that can be imposed only when that steady state exists.

The two-cohort law also makes demographic momentum explicit. In the closed
economy, define the effective reproduction rate
$g_t=\nu\bar n_t$ and the inherited old-young ratio $\mu_t=O_t/Y_t$. Total
adult-household population $S_t=Y_t+O_t$ then obeys:
\begin{equation}
 S_{t+1}=Y_t(1+g_t),
 \qquad
 S_t=Y_t(1+\mu_t),
 \qquad
 S_{t+1}>S_t\ \Longleftrightarrow\ g_t>\mu_t.
 \label{eq:demographic_momentum}
\end{equation}
Hence below-replacement fertility can coexist with population growth when
$\mu_t<g_t<1$. Starting from an exact steady state, however, $\mu_t=1$, so an
immediate fall below replacement reduces total population in the next period.

\section{Steady states}

Under constant primitives, let $\mathcal N(P)$ denote average fertility per
young household in the stationary household problem, and let
$\mathcal H^Y(P)$ and $\mathcal H^O(P)$ denote stationary average housing by
the two cohorts.

\begin{proposition}[Closed steady state]
Suppose the stationary household problem is well defined. A positive closed
steady state exists only at a price $P^*>0$ satisfying:
\begin{equation}
 \nu\mathcal N(P^*)=1.
 \label{eq:replacement_condition}
\end{equation}
For every such price, the associated cohort masses are:
\begin{equation}
 Y^*=O^*=\frac{\bar H}
 {\mathcal H^Y(P^*)+\mathcal H^O(P^*)}.
 \label{eq:closed_scale}
\end{equation}
If $\mathcal N(P)$ is continuous and crosses $1/\nu$ exactly once, the positive
closed steady state is unique.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Stationarity of the aging law gives $O^*=Y^*$. With $M=0$ and $\rho=1$, the
young-cohort law becomes
$Y^*=\nu Y^*\mathcal N(P^*)$. Dividing by the positive mass $Y^*$ gives
equation~\eqref{eq:replacement_condition}. Substituting $O^*=Y^*$ into
housing clearing gives equation~\eqref{eq:closed_scale}. A unique crossing of
the replacement schedule gives a unique price, after which housing clearing
gives a unique positive scale.
\end{proof}

Demographic replacement selects the stationary price; housing clearing then
selects how many households can live at that price. If
$\nu\mathcal N(P)<1$ at every positive price, the model has no positive closed
steady state. A finite transition can still be computed, but it should not be
described as movement to a second positive steady state.

With constant outside inflow $M>0$, the open steady-state cohort mass instead
satisfies:
\begin{equation}
 Y^*=O^*=\frac{M}{1-\rho\nu\mathcal N(P^*)},
 \qquad \rho\nu\mathcal N(P^*)<1.
 \label{eq:open_scale}
\end{equation}
Together, equations~\eqref{eq:housing_clearing} and
\eqref{eq:open_scale} determine the open-market price and scale. Thus an open
local economy can remain stationary with below-replacement fertility because
outside inflows fill the replacement gap.

Suppose the economy begins at an old steady state and $\vartheta_t$ then falls
permanently. On impact, $Y_t$ and $O_t$ are inherited, while prices, tenure,
housing, and fertility respond. Fertility changes $Y_{t+1}$ through
equation~\eqref{eq:cohort_law}; the new cohort changes subsequent housing
demand and prices. If a new root of equation~\eqref{eq:replacement_condition}
exists, convergence to it defines a transition between steady states. If it
does not exist, the equilibrium remains a nonstationary demographic path.

One implication is immediate. Average fertility in every positive closed
steady state equals $1/\nu$. Housing policy can change fertility during the
transition, the distribution of fertility across households, and the
stationary population scale, but it cannot permanently change average
fertility across closed steady states without changing survival or household
formation summarized by $\nu$.

\section{Intergenerational housing misallocation}

The July reallocation result can be stated at any date of the transition. Hold
the price path, cohort masses, tenure assignments, and all other household
allocations fixed. Consider a young owner whose financing constraint binds and
whose owner-size cap is slack, and an old incumbent at an interior retention
choice. Moving one unit of housing from the incumbent to the young buyer
generates goods-equivalent surplus:
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\left(r_t+q\ell_{t+1}+\zeta_t^{O,F}\right)}_{
 \text{young buyer's marginal value}}
 -\underbrace{\left(r_t-\ell_t\right)}_{
 \text{old incumbent's marginal value}}
 =\zeta_t^{O,F}+q\ell_{t+1}+\ell_t>0.
 \label{eq:dynamic_misallocation}
\end{equation}
In a stationary equilibrium, $\ell_t=\ell_{t+1}=\bar\ell$, so
equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic_misallocation} becomes the July surplus formula
$\zeta^{O,F}+q\bar\ell+\bar\ell$.

This is a conditional date-$t$ allocation result, not a dynamic welfare
theorem. A policy that reallocates housing also changes fertility, future
cohort sizes, and equilibrium prices. Evaluating that policy requires welfare
weights over present and future cohorts and an explicit decision about whether
the utility of unborn children is counted separately from parental utility.
