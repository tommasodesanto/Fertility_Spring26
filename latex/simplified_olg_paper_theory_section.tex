\section{Analytical Model}
\label{sec:analytical_model}

This section isolates two implications of housing access in a two-period
overlapping-generations economy.  First, a financing-constrained young buyer
can value family-sized space more than an old incumbent who would realize a
taxable gain by selling it.  Second, a permanent change in desired fertility
can move the economy between positive steady states, so house-price
appreciation and taxable gains can arise along the transition even though they
vanish at both endpoints.  Appendix~\ref{app:olg_proofs} provides the household
solutions, conditional transition results, and a high-accuracy mixed-tenure
transition approximation.

\subsection{Environment and household choices}

At date $t$, the economy contains $Y_t$ young households and $O_t$ old
households.  A young household has income $y_i$, liquid wealth $b_i$, and
chooses consumption $c$, housing $h$, completed fertility $n$, saving $a'$, and
tenure $m\in\{R,O\}$.  The pair $(y_i,b_i)$ is drawn from a fixed distribution
$F$ in each cohort; estates are warm-glow expenditures rather than the source
of the next cohort's $b_i$.  A child requires $\chi>0$ units of goods and
$\kappa>0$ units of space.  Young and old flow utilities are
\begin{align}
 u_t^Y(c,h,n)&=\log c+\alpha\log(h-\kappa n)+\vartheta_t\log n,
 \label{eq:olg_young_utility}\\
 u^O(c^O,h^O,e)&=\log c^O+\gamma\log h^O+\omega_B\log e,
 \label{eq:olg_old_utility}
\end{align}
where $e$ is a warm-glow estate.  A child born at $t$ produces $\nu>0$ young
households at $t+1$, after survival and household formation.  Thus
$Y_{t+1}=\nu\bar n_tY_t$ and $O_{t+1}=Y_t$, where $\bar n_t$ is average
fertility among the young.

The fixed housing stock is $\bar H$.  Rental units satisfy
$h\leq\bar h^R$, owner units satisfy $h\leq\bar h^O$, and
$\bar h^R<\bar h^O$.  A one-period bond has price $q\in(0,1)$ and gross return
$R=q^{-1}$.  Competitive housing intermediation implies the rental user cost
\begin{equation}
 r_t=(1+\tau^p)P_t-qP_{t+1},
 \qquad
 \ell_t=\tau^g[P_t-P_{t-1}]_+,
 \label{eq:olg_prices}
\end{equation}
where $P_t$ is the house price, $\tau^p$ the property-tax rate, and $\ell_t$
the capital-gains tax per unit sold by an old owner.  Housing retained until
death receives a stepped-up basis; intermediaries mark their basis to market.
A young buyer finances the share
$\phi\in(0,1)$ and therefore posts $(1-\phi)P_th$ at purchase.

Conditional on renting, the young household pays $r_th$ and enters old age
with financial wealth $Ra'$.  Conditional on owning, it solves
\begin{align}
 W_t^O(i)=\max_{c,h,n,a'}\;&u_t^Y(c,h,n)+\beta V_{t+1}^O(a_{t+1},h) \\
 \text{s.t.}\quad&
 c+\chi n+a'+[(1-\phi)+\tau^p]P_th=y_i+b_i+T_t,\nonumber\\[-2pt]
 &a_{t+1}=Ra'-R\phi P_th,\qquad
 (1-\phi)P_th\leq b_i,\qquad h\leq\bar h^O,\qquad a'\geq0,\nonumber
 \label{eq:olg_young_owner}
\end{align}
and the renter problem $W_t^R(i)$ replaces the two owner cash-flow equations
with $c+\chi n+a'+r_th=y_i+b_i+T_t$, $h\leq\bar h^R$, and $a'\geq0$.
Only $b_i$ is pledgeable at closing.  The transfer $T_t$ is the equal
per-household rebate of housing-tax revenue.

An old renter spends $a+T_t$ on $c^O$, rental housing, and the estate.  An old
incumbent enters with financial wealth $a$ and inherited housing $H$ and has
the flow-equivalent budget
\begin{equation}
 c^O+qe+(r_t-\ell_t)h^O
 =a+(P_t-\ell_t)H+T_t,\qquad
 0<h^O\leq H,\quad e\geq P_{t+1}h^O.
 \label{eq:olg_old_owner}
\end{equation}
The marginal cost of retaining a unit is therefore $r_t-\ell_t$: selling
realizes the current tax, while retaining the unit avoids it.

To keep both tenures active without altering conditional policies, households
receive iid type-I extreme-value tenure tastes with scale $\kappa_T>0$ and an
ownership intercept $\bar\xi$.  The owner share for type $i$ is
\begin{equation}
 \pi_t^O(i)=
 \frac{\exp\{[W_t^O(i)+\bar\xi]/\kappa_T\}}
 {\exp\{W_t^R(i)/\kappa_T\}+\exp\{[W_t^O(i)+\bar\xi]/\kappa_T\}}.
 \label{eq:olg_owner_share}
\end{equation}
The taste shocks smooth aggregate tenure choice; they do not enter the
conditional renter or owner first-order conditions.

\subsection{Housing access and conditional policies}

Let $A=1+\gamma+\omega_B$, $k=\beta A$, and define lifetime resources and
tenure-specific user costs by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_{it}=y_i+b_i+T_t+qT_{t+1},\qquad
 r_t^R=r_t,\qquad r_t^O=r_t+q\ell_{t+1}.
 \label{eq:olg_lifetime_objects}
\end{equation}
When young saving and next-period old choices are interior, the Euler equation
reduces each conditional problem to
\begin{equation}
 (1+k)c+\chi n+r_t^mh=\mathcal W_{it}.
 \label{eq:olg_lifetime_budget}
\end{equation}
Writing $B_t=1+k+\alpha+\vartheta_t$, unconstrained policies are
\begin{equation}
 c_t^m=\frac{\mathcal W_{it}}{B_t},\qquad
 n_t^m=\frac{\vartheta_t\mathcal W_{it}}
 {B_t(\chi+\kappa r_t^m)},\qquad
 h_t^m=\frac{\alpha\mathcal W_{it}}{B_tr_t^m}+\kappa n_t^m.
 \label{eq:olg_unconstrained_policies}
\end{equation}
The effective housing limits are
\begin{equation}
 \widehat h_{it}^R=\bar h^R,\qquad
 \widehat h_{it}^O=\min\left\{\bar h^O,
 \frac{b_i}{(1-\phi)P_t}\right\}.
 \label{eq:olg_effective_caps}
\end{equation}
If the unconstrained policy exceeds $\widehat h_{it}^m$, housing equals the
limit and fertility is the unique interior root
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\vartheta_t}{n}
 -\frac{\alpha\kappa}{\widehat h_{it}^m-\kappa n}
 -\frac{(1+k)\chi}
 {\mathcal W_{it}-r_t^m\widehat h_{it}^m-\chi n}=0.
 \label{eq:olg_binding_fertility}
\end{equation}

The housing first-order condition prices the access constraint in goods:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\alpha c_t^m}{h_t^m-\kappa n_t^m}=r_t^m+\zeta_{it}^m,
 \qquad
 \zeta_{it}^{O,F}=\frac{\mu_{it}}{\lambda_{it}}(1-\phi)P_t,
 \label{eq:olg_access_wedge}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{it}$ is the marginal value of current resources and $\mu_{it}$
is the multiplier on the down-payment constraint.  When the owner-size limit
is slack, $\zeta_{it}^{O,F}$ is the entire owner access wedge.

\subsection{Positive steady states and transitions}

Let $\mathcal N(P,T;\vartheta)$ be average fertility and let
$\mathcal H^Y(P,T;\vartheta)$ and $\mathcal H^O(P,T;\vartheta)$ be housing per
young and old household after tenure choice and the induced old-state
distribution.  Write $\mathcal H=\mathcal H^Y+\mathcal H^O$.  Market clearing
and the government budget are
\begin{align}
 Y_t\bar h_t^Y+O_t\bar h_t^O&=\bar H,
 \label{eq:olg_housing_clearing}\\
 (Y_t+O_t)T_t&=\tau^pP_t\bar H+\ell_tO_t\bar s_t^O,
 \label{eq:olg_government}
\end{align}
where $\bar s_t^O$ is housing sold by old owners per old household, assigning
zero sales to renters.

\begin{proposition}[Positive steady state]
\label{prop:olg_steady_state}
Every positive steady state is a root $(P^*,T^*)\in\mathbb R_{++}^2$ of
\begin{equation}
 \nu\mathcal N(P,T;\vartheta)-1=0,
 \qquad
 T-\frac{\tau^pP}{2}\mathcal H(P,T;\vartheta)=0.
 \label{eq:olg_steady_state_system}
\end{equation}
Given a root, $Y^*=O^*=\bar H/\mathcal H(P^*,T^*;\vartheta)$ and
$\ell^*=0$.  Hence every positive steady state has average fertility
$1/\nu$, but prices, transfers, tenure, individual fertility, housing, and
population scale can differ across steady states.
\end{proposition}

Existence follows under the boundary and continuity conditions in
Appendix~\ref{app:olg_steady_states}; local uniqueness requires the two-by-two
Jacobian of \eqref{eq:olg_steady_state_system} to be nonsingular.  The steady
state is thus an equilibrium implication, not a normalization.  Replacement
fixes average fertility only after imposing stationarity.

Suppose $\vartheta_t$ rises permanently at date zero and the economy starts at
the old positive steady state.  The predetermined state is
$S_t=(Y_t,O_t,G_t,P_{t-1})$, where $G_t$ is the distribution of old financial
wealth, inherited housing, and tenure.  Given a candidate perfect-foresight
sequence, household policies generate the two date-$t$ residuals
\begin{align}
 \mathcal R_t^H&=Y_t\bar h_t^Y+O_t\bar h_t^O-\bar H,\\
 \mathcal R_t^G&=(Y_t+O_t)T_t-\tau^pP_t\bar H-\ell_tO_t\bar s_t^O.
 \label{eq:olg_transition_residuals}
\end{align}
A transition equilibrium is a root of this infinite sequence together with the
cohort and distribution laws, converging to the new steady state.  Computation
uses terminally closed finite-horizon truncations: prices, transfers, and value
functions are fixed at the new steady state beyond the terminal date, while the
terminal endogenous state is allowed to approach it.  A regime-consistent
price branch and a nonsingular stacked Jacobian give a locally unique
truncation for sufficiently small permanent shocks; explicit boundary signs
give global existence of a truncation.  A convergent sequence of such roots is
an exact infinite-horizon transition under the uniform-tail condition in
Appendix~\ref{app:olg_transitions}.

The capital-gains basis needs no second shock.  If equilibrium prices satisfy
$P_s>P_{s-1}$ on several successive dates, then
$\ell_s=\tau^g(P_s-P_{s-1})>0$ for each corresponding old cohort.  A two-period
life makes the preceding date's price the basis; it does not restrict the
number of dates over which the equilibrium price adjusts.

\subsection{Intergenerational reallocation and fertility}

Consider an old incumbent at an interior retention choice and a young owner
whose down-payment constraint binds while the owner-size limit is slack.  Their
marginal values of one housing unit, in current goods, are
\begin{equation}
 MV_{it}^Y=r_t+q\ell_{t+1}+\zeta_{it}^{O,F},
 \qquad
 MV_{jt}^O=r_t-\ell_t.
 \label{eq:olg_marginal_values}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Conditional intergenerational reallocation]
\label{prop:olg_reallocation}
Fix fertility, cohort masses, tenure, and all unaffected household allocations.
Suppose a planner can transfer the marginal unit's title and occupancy, provide
pledgeable bridge finance, compensate the incumbent with lump-sum transfers,
and reclaim the incremental gains-tax rebates at dates $t$ and $t+1$.  If the
real transfer cost is $K_{ijt}$, moving a sufficiently small unit from old
incumbent $j$ to constrained young buyer $i$ is a compensated improvement when
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\zeta_{it}^{O,F}+\ell_t+q\ell_{t+1}}_{MV_{it}^Y-MV_{jt}^O}
 >K_{ijt}.
 \label{eq:olg_reallocation_surplus}
\end{equation}
At a steady state the condition reduces to $\zeta_{i}^{O,F}>K_{ij}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{figure}[!htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/simplified_olg_reallocation_wedge.png}
\caption{Marginal values on the computed path.  The financing wedge raises
the young buyer's value; realization taxes lower the old incumbent's value and
raise the buyer's anticipated private cost.  The displayed object is the gross
marginal-value gap.  Under the ledger in
Proposition~\ref{prop:olg_reallocation}, a compensated improvement obtains when
this gap exceeds the real transfer cost.}
\label{fig:olg_reallocation_wedge}
\end{figure}

This is deliberately a local allocation result.  A reform that changes
fertility changes the number of future households, so a global ranking also
requires an explicit social criterion for comparing populations.  The
reallocation proposition instead fixes population and identifies the exact
intergenerational wedge before choosing such a criterion.

Housing access nevertheless has a precise fertility implication.  Holding
tenure, prices, lifetime wealth, and the active set fixed, let
$D=\mathcal W_{it}-r_t^m\widehat h-\chi n$ and
$S=\widehat h-\kappa n$.  Differentiating
\eqref{eq:olg_binding_fertility} gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd n}{\dd\widehat h}
 =\frac{\alpha\kappa/S^2-(1+k)\chi r_t^m/D^2}
 {\vartheta_t/n^2+(1+k)\chi^2/D^2+\alpha\kappa^2/S^2}.
 \label{eq:olg_fertility_derivative}
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive.  Using the housing first-order condition, the
exact sign restriction is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd n}{\dd\widehat h}\geq0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \chi\leq
 \frac{(1+k)\kappa(r_t^m+\zeta_{it}^m)^2}{\alpha r_t^m}.
 \label{eq:olg_fertility_sign}
\end{equation}
More accessible space raises fertility when children are sufficiently
space-intensive relative to their goods cost.  This partial-equilibrium sign
does not by itself sign a funded policy: prices, rebates, tenure, and lifetime
resources also respond in general equilibrium.
