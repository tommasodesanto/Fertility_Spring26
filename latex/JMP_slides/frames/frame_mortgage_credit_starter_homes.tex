\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item Easier credit does not raise completed fertility. Some couples have their first child earlier, so births rise by about half a percent in the first four years, then fall back.
\item In the model, credit lowers completed fertility by 1.4\% for one reason: young childless couples buy two-room homes and then find them too small for a child. In the model 30\% of young childless owners live in two rooms; in the PSID, 3\%.
\item Without two- and four-room homes on the market, the effect disappears. With the actual US distribution of home sizes, credit leaves completed fertility unchanged.
\item Credit offered only to parents raises completed fertility by 1.2\%. The gain is small because parents can already rent a family-sized home.
\item {\color{draftred}The PSID tells a different story about why credit does not raise births. Boom buyers in 2003--07 bought the same size as earlier buyers but paid 2.24 times their income, not 1.77. Credit went into higher prices, not smaller homes; the model's starter-home mechanism is not in the data.}
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0pt}
Credit changes when households buy and when they have children, not how many.
