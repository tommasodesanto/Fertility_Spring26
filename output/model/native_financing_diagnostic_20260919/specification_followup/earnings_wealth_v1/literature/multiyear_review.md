# Multi-year earnings implementations: primary-source review

**Date:** 2026-09-21
**Scope:** read-only source review before an earnings-specification choice. This memo records what the papers actually implement; it does not adopt parameters or authorize model changes.

## Executive finding

The strongest directly documented convention is De Nardi (2004): construct complete five-year PSID income cells and estimate the log-income process at the same frequency. Bick (2016) provides the closest family-model analogue: three-year model periods, gross annual income converted into monthly averages and summed over the model period, direct estimation of a single AR(1) shock, stationary initial shocks, and Tauchen discretization. De Nardi and Yang (2016) is a useful five-year OLG extension, but it imports the five-year estimates from De Nardi (2004), then replaces the upper tail with a hand-built four-state transition matrix. Kolasa (2024) is the closest four-year fertility OLG reference. Its Warsaw working-paper version explicitly labels the reported earnings parameters annual, while the published indexed table is reformatted and does not expose the same frequency heading; the published-version provenance therefore remains unresolved. Doepke and Kindermann (2019) uses three-year periods but fixes female wages over the lifecycle, so it is a timing precedent only.

### Version and estimator addendum

The Warsaw working-paper version of Kolasa (Working Papers 4/2024 (440), Table 1, printed p. 22) explicitly says **“Externally set parameters, annual values”** above the rows containing the persistent AR parameter .9, persistent-shock SD \(\sqrt{.03}\), and transitory-shock SD .25. The published JEDC indexed table is reformatted: it exposes the same shock values but marks only the beta and interest-rate entries as annual. I therefore preserve the working-paper annual evidence and label the published-version frequency heading unresolved; this is a version difference, not evidence that the values are four-year estimates.

For Bick, the published Wiley page confirms that Online Appendix A/B and a replication package exist, while direct supplementary retrieval was inaccessible in this review. The accessible final author version reproduces Table C.1 (printed p. 46) with \(\rho=.882\) and innovation SD \(\sigma_u=.272\), matching the working paper. Appendix C.1 describes direct estimation of the AR(1) process after constructing three-year income from monthly averages of annual income. It reports no separate permanent type plus iid/transitory residual and does not state an estimator more precisely than “estimated directly.” Thus .272 is the AR innovation SD; calling it a separately estimated transitory component would overstate the source.

## Parameter and unit cautions

1. A persistence parameter and a variance are not interchangeable with an innovation standard deviation. De Nardi’s Table A.1 labels “variance” for the residual prediction error in the five-year regression; De Nardi–Yang imports persistence .92 and variance .38 from that table. Bick reports AR innovation **standard deviation** .272, not a separately estimated transitory component. Kolasa’s indexed table reports shock **standard deviations** (\sqrt{.03}) and .25; the Warsaw working paper explicitly labels those entries annual.
2. De Nardi (2004) directly estimates five-year cells. De Nardi–Yang’s five-year Markov process is partly imported and partly calibrated to the upper tail. Bick directly constructs three-year gross-income observations, then estimates the process. None of these papers establishes a generic annual-to-four-year shortcut.
3. “Income” is not the same object across sources: De Nardi and De Nardi–Yang use individual labor earnings/productivity with different transfer treatment; Bick uses spousal gross income and then taxes; Kolasa’s table combines an age-productivity profile with persistent and transitory household earnings shocks. A parameter import must therefore align the income concept, household aggregation, tax treatment, and observation schedule before comparing moments.
4. A four-year endpoint state transition and a four-year average/sum of annual income are different objects. The former can be mapped algebraically from an annual AR(1); the latter requires integrating the annual path and its within-period covariance structure. Direct multi-year cell estimation is the clearest precedent when complete cells are available.

## Mechanical routes supported by the evidence

**Direct period estimation.** Construct complete four-year income cells using the same income concept intended for the model, estimate the age-profile and shock process on those cells, and discretize the resulting process. De Nardi (2004) is the direct precedent. The PSID’s later biennial observation schedule means four consecutive annual values cannot be silently fabricated; the sample must either use genuinely complete annual cells or an explicitly documented observed-income construction.

**Annual process plus simulated aggregation.** Estimate an annual process, simulate annual paths, aggregate each path to four-year sums/means, and fit a parsimonious finite process to the resulting four-year object. Bick supports the principle that period income should be constructed first and that gross income should precede taxes, but does not validate a four-year AR(1) closed form. The resulting approximation needs checks on dispersion, autocovariances, lower-tail mass, entrant distribution and household choices.

**What the literature does not support.** No reviewed primary source supports silently taking an annual AR(1), raising persistence to the fourth power, separately averaging an iid shock, and treating the result as the exact four-year income process. That endpoint mapping is an algebraic latent-state identity; it is not a citation for four-year period income.

## Recommendation status

These sources support the author’s requested literature-first sequence: close the income concept and observation schedule, then choose direct four-year estimation versus a simulated annual-to-four-year approximation, and only then fit the finite state process. Kolasa is the best four-year fertility reference; its working paper establishes annual labels, while the published-version heading remains unresolved. De Nardi (2004) is the strongest primary measurement precedent; Bick is the strongest family-model implementation precedent for gross-income aggregation, stationary entry shocks and discretization, but its process has no separately estimated iid/transitory component. No source reviewed here supplies an adopted project specification.
