# PAYGO pension calibration in U.S. quantitative OLG work

Purpose: compare primary-source U.S. OLG precedents on who pays, what is taxed, and whether the pension tax or benefit is calibrated. Page numbers below are the printed journal or report pages; PDF page numbers may differ.

| Paper and primary source | Published tax value and base | Pension calibration / closure | What it supports here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conesa and Krueger (1999), *Social Security Reform with Heterogeneous Agents*, Review of Economic Dynamics 2, 757–795. [Published paper PDF](https://editorialexpress.com/jrust/econ698s/conesa.pdf), pp. 765–766. | 10.7% OASI payroll-tax benchmark (their data discussion says total Social Security taxes are 15.3%, then removes Medicare and DI); proportional tax on modeled labor earnings. The simplified model does not apply an individual statutory cap or separate employer wedge. | They set the common replacement rate to match the observed 10.7% tax. The retiree/active-population ratio is 21.6%, matched to the 1990 Census age-65+ / age-20–65 ratio; under their model’s common benefit, \(\tau=b\times (R/W)\), giving 50% replacement. Their reform then changes both benefit and tax to zero. OASI includes survivors coverage, which the authors treat as transfer from working to retired households. | Closest precedent for an externally benchmarked proportional payroll rate plus an endogenous common pension. Crucially, retirees enter the dependency ratio that maps contributions to benefit spending, not the labor-earnings tax base. |

Separately, the general labor-income tax is 17%. | PAYGO tax is set to balance the pension budget each period; benefits use a U.S.-style concave lifetime-earnings formula, implying a 44% average-earner replacement rate. Table 2 reports pensions/output 7.11%, \(\theta=.103\), and general labor tax \(\tau=.17\). Thus 10.3% is the baseline equilibrium result under their benefit and demographic structure, not an exogenous tax target. | Shows that a 10.3% rate can emerge endogenously from a specific progressive benefit schedule and retiree/worker composition. It is not a rate to transplant while changing the pension rule or demographic masses. |
| Huggett and Ventura (1999), *On the Distributional Effects of Social Security Reform*, Review of Economic Dynamics 2, 498–531. The model’s reform comparisons hold the U.S.-system tax and retirement age fixed; the relevant passage does not report a standalone numerical \(\theta\). | Explicit period PAYGO balance: aggregate payroll receipts equal benefits paid to retirees. The U.S. benefits are earnings-linked and concave; the alternative proposal’s floor is paid from general revenues. | Strong equation-level precedent for the accounting distinction: workers’ earnings generate receipts, retirees’ benefit payments use them. Levy applies to nonretired workers’ pre-tax labor earnings, capped at the Social Security maximum; the consumer pays half of combined contributions and the other half is employer-paid. Retirees pay no payroll contribution in this model. | The tax is set so Social Security is self-financing: aggregate capped contributions by workers equal benefits to retired agents. Benefit parameters use U.S. marginal replacement rates 90/32/15%, bend points and cap at 0.21/1.29/2.42 times average earnings, and retirement ages 62–70 (normal age 66). It directly excludes retirees from the payroll-tax contributor set and shows how a capped earnings base, employer incidence, benefit formula, and retiree mass jointly determine the balanced PAYGO rate. |

## Read-through for the maintained model

The proposed 8.751% is our aggregate-base calculation, not a tax estimate reported by any paper. It applies the 2007 combined OASI rate to the ratio of SSA taxable covered earnings to uncapped covered earnings, then maps that effective rate to the model’s uncapped working-earnings base. Social Security benefits are not covered earnings; the retired population belongs on the pension-payment side of PAYGO balance, not in the payroll-tax denominator. People who work after claiming/retirement can still have covered earnings under the actual program, but the model’s maintained base is its working-earnings measure.

The current model proposal is a chosen rate on uncapped modeled working earnings, while the pension is calculated residually from receipts and survival-weighted retiree exposure. For the baseline, that follows the first calibration route above: choose a payroll-tax benchmark and let the common pension/replacement outcome follow from demographics and earnings. Using the current masses supplied for the model (0.740801 workers, 0.259199 retirees), the retiree/worker ratio is about 35.0%; with the proposed rate, the implied common pension is about 25.0% of the model’s relevant mean annual worker earnings, subject to the exact earnings weighting in the implemented budget equation.

The proposed 8.751% is not directly comparable to every paper rate: Conesa–Krueger’s 10.7% is a historical OASI benchmark; Fuster et al.’s and Peterman–Sager’s 10.3% are budget-balanced model outcomes under different demographic and benefit schedules; and Peterman–Sager cap earnings and split employer/employee contributions while this model taxes an uncapped household earning endowment. Keep the 8.751% described as the proposed aggregate OASI effective-base proxy already documented in `paygo_rate_review_20260924.md`, not as a universal statutory rate.
