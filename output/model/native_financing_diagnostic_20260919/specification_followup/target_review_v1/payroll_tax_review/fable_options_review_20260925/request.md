The author wants an overview that permits a decision, after an unhelpfully circular discussion. Please give an independent judgment rather than endorsing either of the lead's successive recommendations. Spend up to one hour on a focused literature and specification review. Do not run the model, change targets or parameters, edit manuscript files, or launch empirical work.

## Question

What simple, defensible pension-financing specification should this paper use in its reference steady state and in transitions with changing cohort sizes and endogenous fertility? Which scalar should be calibrated externally, to what number and definition, and which fiscal object should adjust? The author is open to a transparent literature value and accepts that model cohort sizes will not match U.S. demographics exactly. He is concerned about both squeezing retirees through benefit cuts and squeezing working families through tax increases.

## Model and current evidence

- This is a household OLG housing/fertility model with saving, tenure and sequential fertility choices; working earnings are exogenous endowments with an age profile and persistent risk. Do not attribute endogenous labor-supply responses to this implementation.
- Retired households receive a common pension. The current payroll levy applies to gross working earnings only, excludes pension and asset income, and has no individual earnings cap or separate employer payment. Pension outlays exhaust contributions. General government spending is not the residual recipient of this levy.
- The old rate was 17.9%. Our recent proposed 8.751% is 2007 combined OASI 10.6% times taxable covered earnings / uncapped covered earnings: 5,268,200 / 6,381,306 (both totals include wages and self-employment, SSA 2025 vintage). It is a statutory-rate-times-base calculation, not directly estimated cash contributions. Covered earnings are not automatically identical to the model's household gross earnings. Employer incidence and coverage are maintained approximations.
- The saved economy has worker mass 0.740801474 and retiree exposure 0.259198526. With a uniform pension, fixed 17.9% gives pension/mean annual working gross earnings 51.159%; fixed 8.751% gives 25.011%. These are not replacements relative to each person's own career earnings.
- Two controlled tax evaluations kept structural parameters and fertility utility fixed. Lower tax raised wealth/earnings 5.241 to 7.263, ownership 39.493% to 41.881%, lowered childlessness 16.909% to 12.538%, and advanced first birth 26.658 to 25.942. These are conditional on old demographic accounting and do not determine the right pension institution.
- A separate short lower-tax recalibration bundled new empirical targets, external bequest shift and housing-cost inputs; it cannot isolate taxation. Do not pick a pension specification by weighted loss or by the ownership miss.
- Adopted but unimplemented demography: retain independent dependency departure probability 2/9 per four-year period, separately schedule half of each birth cohort into adulthood after 16 years and half after 20, applying birth-to-household conversion 1/2.1 once. The frozen stationary solve instead uses departure-based entry and stores conversion 0.5. Equivalence of those laws is not established. Separate imperfect levels from inconsistent accounting; a different queue delay alone leaves constant-cohort flows unchanged under otherwise identical conversion and attrition.
- Nothing in this request adopts a tax or pension replacement rate. The lead recommended fixed 8.751%, then suggested a fixed 50% common pension with endogenous tax when the author raised retirement-income concerns. That change of advice is precisely why an independent overview is needed.

## Existing source checks to verify and extend selectively

1. Conesa–Krueger (1999), RED pp. 765–766: OLG, constant population growth 1.1%; retiree/active ratio 21.6% matched to 1990 Census; choose benefit replacement to reproduce 10.7% OASI tax, implying approximately 50%. Their transition prescribes benefit changes and allows financing taxes to change. This is not evidence that a 50% promise is uniquely appropriate here.
2. Fuster–Imrohoroglu–Imrohoroglu (2007), ReStud Table 2 p. 125: 10.3% is the endogenous baseline pension payroll rate for an earnings-linked benefit formula, with 44% replacement for an average earner. General labor-income tax is separately 17%.
3. Peterman–Sager (2018), FEDS 2018-028 pp. 12,15,20–21: capped worker earnings, employee/employer split, benefit formula calibrated to U.S. rules; 10.3% balances the pension budget. It is an equilibrium outcome, not an external empirical rate.
4. Kitao (2014), RED 17,756–779, published author PDF: entrant growth 1.2% to 0.5% plus increasing longevity, exogenous demographic transition. Tax-increase option approximately 10.6% to 16.4%; fixed-tax option cuts the benefit formula 32.6%. Verify transition financing: policy schedules span 50 years while demographic changes span 90. Do not assume this equals our exact period-by-period residual-benefit rule. Published table 3 prints 16.3 where table 2/text use 16.4; do not mix 2011 working-paper numbers with the published version.
5. Dynamic Urban Economics (Greaney, Parkhomenko, Van Nieuwerburgh), public Dec. 5,2025 version: table 1 p.27 lists 17.9%, text p.31 says 15.6% over 2005–09; fn.33 identifies OECD income tax for single childless average-wage earner. Revenue supports pensions and other government spending. No numerical pension calibration or complete allocation rule was established in our bounded check. Do not assume their tax is an OASI contribution or that all revenue funds pensions.
