- The direct-allocation result is valid and modest. Let $u=qr$ be the present value of one unit's rent, the user cost. A constrained young buyer values a unit at $MV^Y=\alpha x/s$ in goods, an interior old owner at $MV^O=u$. A compensated transfer with marginal real cost $K$ improves welfare when $MV^Y>u+K$. This is the ordinary "unequal marginal rates of substitution" inefficiency of any binding borrowing limit. It needs no welfare weights. It earns its place only as the definition of the misallocation and as the source of the first figure. It is not a first best and should not be described as one.
- The constrained construction is mathematically correct. I re-derived the compensation of the initial old, the recursion and its contraction factor $\lambda=\alpha h^O/(\rho s)$, the gain derivative $\Delta W'(0)=m h^O\eta,(MV^Y-u)/(1-q\lambda)$, and the one-time-gift recurrence with its negative stable root. All hold. So this is a valid result, not an invalid proof. The question is whether its assumptions are the right ones, and whether it is described correctly.
- It is misdescribed, and the description is what matters for your question. Strip the notation and the reform is a public down-payment loan. For every cohort after the first, the state advances $(1-\phi)P$ per extra unit before closing. When that household is old it collects $-q,\mathrm d\ell^{O}=\bigl[(1-\phi)P+MV^Y-u\bigr]\mathrm dh$. That is repayment at the world rate plus a fee equal to the buyer's surplus. The fee can be waived. I checked what happens then. Let $\rho=1+\beta A$ be the lifetime weight on adult consumption and $B=1+\beta(1+\omega_B)$ the present value of goods a cohort absorbs per unit of adult consumption. With a pure world-rate loan the goods needed to compensate the initial old, $u$ per unit they release, equal exactly the present value of goods later cohorts give up by paying user cost on their extra space, because $\rho-\beta\gamma=B$ identically. Nothing is created. The fixed stock is reassigned from households who value a unit at $u$ to households who value it at $MV^Y$. Every young owner strictly gains and everyone else is indifferent to first order. A small fee closes the finite case. That is the honest economics, and it is far easier to state than "committed household-specific transfers plus a passive claimant."
- Three elements of the current proof are artifices, not powers. The passive outside owner is used as financier only because the note imposed period-by-period budget balance. Auerbach and Kotlikoff's authority balances in present value, so let the government hold world bonds and tax the windfall on titles held by the intermediary and dead estates. Identity-based targeting can be replaced by conditioning repayment on taking the loan. The surplus should go to the young, not to an outsider.
- The label "constrained inefficiency" should be dropped. In the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis or Bisin sense, the planner faces the same frictions and only redistributes. Here the planner has a power private lenders lack: it collects from future income through taxation. Your own no-go result says that with one-shot cash and no such power there is locally no Pareto improvement. Read together, the two results say the distortion is a missing credit market and only an instrument that substitutes for that market repairs it. That is Boldrin and Montes's education argument and Schoonbroodt and Tertilt's parent-child argument transplanted to housing. It is a better claim than the one the note currently makes.
- Fixed fertility restricts the comparison set, not behavior. Under free fertility each household's gain from reoptimizing is second order, its fertility change is first order, so the population path moves. An anonymous program that makes owners strictly better off shifts the tenure cutoff at first order; an identity-targeted one does not. The proposition must say "holding each household's fertility at its equilibrium level, including in tenure comparisons." The strict-margin device in the note is exactly that restriction.
- Literature departures are small. From Auerbach and Kotlikoff: splitting a cohort's transfer into a pre-closing grant and an old-age tax. With a binding liquidity constraint that timing is the whole point and must be stated. From Boldrin and Montes and Schoonbroodt and Tertilt: nothing structural. I did not re-verify the Auerbach and Kotlikoff pages myself; I rely on the previous team's reading and my own recollection.
Do you accept, as the planner's defining power, that the state can lend to a young buyer and be repaid through a lump-sum tax when that household is old, with a present-value budget at the world bond price? This is an economic choice. It means writing the maintained friction as an enforcement asymmetry: private lenders can only collateralize the house, the state can collect from future income. Boldrin and Montes make that assumption explicit.
My recommendation is yes. It is not an invented planner; it is the standard intergenerational state. With it, the existing proof becomes a short proposition: a small public down-payment loan program is a Pareto improvement at fixed fertility, and one-time grants are not, locally. Without it, the honest theorem is the no-go. The paper would then claim local constrained efficiency with respect to one-shot cash, which is respectable but a different result.
