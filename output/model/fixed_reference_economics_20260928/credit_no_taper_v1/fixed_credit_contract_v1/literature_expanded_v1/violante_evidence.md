# Violante borrowing-limit evidence — September 30, 2026

Bounded primary-source review; no model runs, code execution, or calibration adoption. Published versions were inspected rather than relying on working-paper numbers. Page numbers below are printed journal/appendix pages.

**Kaplan–Violante (2014), Econometrica.** [Published PDF](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/voices.uchicago.edu/dist/2/981/files/2018/07/kaplan_violante_ecmtra_2014-1sxn3k8.pdf), §3 pp.1209–1212, §5 pp.1220–1221: working-age liquid saving obeys \(m_{j+1}\ge-0.74y_j\); retirees have zero borrowing allowance. The coefficient comes from the 2001 SCF median ratio of reported total credit limit to quarterly household labor income, ages 22–59. It measures available credit, not realized borrowing. Short excerpt: “The median ratio of credit limit to quarterly labor income … is 74%.”

Income is current realized earnings: \(\log y_{ij}=\chi_j+\alpha_i+z_{ij}\), including the current persistent shock, not permanent income or only the deterministic age profile. It is before the tax liability separately subtracted in the budget. Empirical household labor income includes unemployment/disability insurance, TANF and child benefits (p.1216 fn.22). Thus the allowance is 18.5% of the household’s own annualized current income; it is not 18.5% of mean income for every household. Real borrowing costs 6% annually (10% nominal), versus −1.48% real after-tax risk-adjusted liquid saving return. Borrowing cost targets an intermediate borrower share: SCF 17% net-liquid-negative versus 37% revolving debt; model 26%. There is no default choice in the stated household problems.

[Published supplement](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d69437d65a29d0001ae6520/t/5daa1ac39dc16566bcdcde1d/1571429059695/kaplan_violante_ecmtra_2014_appendix.pdf), Appendix D Table D.I p.13 and discussion p.14: limits 0, 0.74 and 1.48 times quarterly own income are sensitivity cases, with the discount factor recalibrated. Doubling credit has little rebate-response effect; zero credit raises it. Appendix E.1.7 p.20 solves backward from the last age. No separate terminal debt-forgiveness rule was established; retirement already prohibits new negative liquid positions.

**Kaplan–Moll–Violante (2018), AER.** [Published author PDF](https://benjaminmoll.com/HANK/), §IIIA pp.708–709 eq.(13), §IIIB p.721 and Table 6 p.724: \(b_t\ge-\underline b\), with a constant exogenous unsecured allowance. Short excerpt: “exogenously at 1 times quarterly average labor income.” Table 6 gives $16,500, equivalent to 25% of $66,000 annual average labor income. This is a representative scalar, not an individual income-dependent constraint. No reported credit-limit moment estimates it; five other parameters target wealth moments. The annual saving return is 2%, borrowing 8%, wedge 6 percentage points. The wedge targets 15% of households with net liquid wealth below −$1,000 (p.721 fn.37); only 0.02% reach the limit (fn.36). Stochastic death and perfect annuity markets redistribute estates (p.708), unlike deterministic terminal repayment.

[HANK appendix](https://benjaminmoll.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/HANK_appendix.pdf), Appendix E Table E.1 p.29: loosening the bound accompanies discount-rate recalibration. Its note says 1→4 times quarterly **GDP**, conflicting with the main text/Table 6 labor-income denominator. This review leaves that conflict unresolved and uses the explicitly stated published main-text benchmark, not a code-derived resolution.

**Mapping inference.** A four-year decision period does not multiply an asset stock limit by four. If one model asset unit equals mean annual gross income, HANK’s stated rule suggests \(D=0.25\), or 6.25% of four-year income. KV suggests \(D_i=0.185Y_i^{annual}\), a different contract; substituting mean income creates an additional approximation. Neither supports calling the current \(D=0.14\) empirically estimated. Both use sizable borrowing spreads, so importing their limits at the fertility model’s symmetric 2% rate changes borrowing incentives.
