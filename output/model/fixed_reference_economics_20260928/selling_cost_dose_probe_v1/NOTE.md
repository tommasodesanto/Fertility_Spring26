# Selling-cost dose response and lock-in check — lead readout (2026-10-02)

Experimental mechanism test. Not a calibration and not adopted. Fixed price and H0, with the
per-child need. The psi = 0.06 and psi = 0 arms reproduce B1 and B2 of the tenure-barrier probe
bit for bit (RECEIPT.md).

## Dose response: financing effect (100% minus 80%)
| Selling cost | 6% | 4% | 2% | 0% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Completed fertility | -0.010 | -0.009 | -0.005 | +0.012 |
| Entrant 18-21 first-birth probability | +0.28 pp | +0.34 pp | +0.54 pp | +1.22 pp |
| Entrant ownership | +21 pp | +29 pp | +39 pp | +49 pp |

The response is monotone but strongly nonlinear: most of the sign change happens between a 2% and
a 0% cost. Even a small transaction cost removes most of the positive effect.

## Lock-in evidence (childless households aged 22-25, selling cost 6%)
- At 100% financing, the mass of childless households owning a 2-room home rises 7.5-fold
  (0.00065 -> 0.0049).
- Their first-birth probability is 0.14, against 0.21 for renters of the same age.
- A 2-room home is below the parent floor (2.49 rooms) and is infeasible for parents. These owners
  must sell, paying the selling cost, before they can have a child.
- Caveat: these are comparisons across different households, so selection matters. Owners of
  2-room homes still have a low first-birth probability (0.13) when the selling cost is 0.
  The lock-in story is supported but not cleanly isolated. Post-birth tenure moves are not saved.

## Implication
Credit pulls marginal young buyers into homes too small for a family. Any cost of trading up then
deters births. The positive credit channel exists only when trading up is nearly free. One option
for the author: the smallest owner size (2 rooms) sits below the parent floor, so it is a starter
home that is structurally incompatible with children.
