# Independent housing, ownership, and fertility diagnosis

The selected quarter-fit [rental-cap and purchase-access overlap diagnostic](quarter_overlap_v1/README.md) compares the one-period child-specific rental-cap value with newly feasible ownership and birth-choice responsiveness on matched renter states.

The subsequent [quarter fixed-price and crossed-price Bellman diagnostic](quarter_fixedprice_v1/README.md) changes only today's financed share at the selected 80% fit while fixing inherited households, next-age values, and prices. First-birth flow rises 0.1841% directly, whereas the accepted temporary equilibrium flow falls 0.4306%. Applying the accepted date-zero price and rent to the same household problem almost exactly reproduces the fall. In a two-order crossed-price accounting, the renter unit-price change contributes 95.2% of the joint current-price offset. This identifies the proximate household-level price channel, not a separate causal equilibrium rent effect. The [rental-cap overlap result](quarter_overlap_v1/README.md) explains why the direct financing response is weak: newly owner-feasible renters rarely combine a binding parent-specific rental cap with a responsive birth margin.

**Crosswalk with the separate selling-cost experiment.** The [tenure-barrier probe](../../tenure_barrier_probe_v1/NOTE.md) instead solves stationary household distributions at a fixed house price and with an *experimental per-child housing-need rule*. Removing the 6% selling cost there changes the effect of 80%-to-100% financing on completed fertility from about -0.010 to +0.012 children. This establishes sensitivity to selling costs in that altered environment; it does not identify the cause of the accepted selected-quarter date-zero equilibrium decline. Its capped renter **parents** are a different population from the childless first-birth risk set in the overlap diagnostic. Their ability to buy even at 80% financing is consistent with the credit change reaching relatively few renters for whom the parent-specific rental cap binds. The proposed small-home trade-up mechanism remains an inference until matched tenure and birth-timing choices are measured.

The follow-up [selling-cost dose probe](../../selling_cost_dose_probe_v1/NOTE.md) holds that same per-child-need and fixed-price contract: financing lowers completed fertility at selling costs of 6%, 4%, and 2%, and raises it only at 0%. Credit increases ownership of two-room homes among young childless households, but their low first-birth rate also persists when selling is free; the saved arrays lack post-birth tenure moves. Thus the dose pattern confirms parameter sensitivity while leaving the specific trade-up path unverified. Neither stationary fixed-price probe can be directly compared with the accepted dated equilibrium effect.

## Measurement limits that matter for this diagnosis

- The empirical 1.465-room first-birth target is the PSID calendar-year $+3/+4$ versus $-3/-2$ event-study contrast for baseline household heads/spouses. The model uses a matched birth/no-birth branch after one four-year period; it does not reconstruct the event prepath, event weights, or the baseline household-head restriction. This is an acknowledged proxy, not proof of a wrong housing preference. See [`prepare_e5f_evening_contract.py`](../../../../../code/model/tools/prepare_e5f_evening_contract.py) and [`CALIBRATION_STATUS.md`](../../../../../CALIBRATION_STATUS.md).
- The recent-parent ownership target is an ACS contrast using the age of the oldest resident child. The model compares actual births into previously empty dependent homes with all currently empty dependent homes, including former parents, after the post-fertility tenure choice. The model's later-birth share of this selected group is about 57%; whether the corresponding empirical share is similar is **unknown**. The statistic is not a forced-birth treatment effect. Source: [`e5f_recent_parent_flow_observer.py`](../../../../../code/model/tools/e5f_recent_parent_flow_observer.py) and the [ACS builder](../../../../../code/empirical/housing/build_initial_housing_profile_diagnostic.py).
- The adopted child-at-home approximation gives each resident child an independent $2/9$ chance of departure each four-year period, for an 18-year expected stay, with no newborn exemption. It therefore permits a child to leave in the first four years. This can create empty-home later births at young parent ages and is a concrete reason to audit the 57% model group share, though its quantitative contribution has not been isolated. Sources: [`CALIBRATION_STATUS.md`](../../../../../CALIBRATION_STATUS.md) (September 24 maturation decision) and [`parameters.py`](../../../../../code/model/intergen_eqscale_seq_optimized/parameters.py) (`make_independent_child_count_transition_matrix`).
  In the saved hard80 observer, later births into empty homes are already 9.2% of all selected empty-home births at model age 22–25, 21.9% at 26–29, and 38.7% at 30–33 (`recent_parent.groups.{first_birth,continuation_birth}.mass_by_age`). The observed ages do not by themselves prove the departure law caused the eventual 57% aggregate share; fertility timing and earlier child counts also matter.
- The active 14-row calibration does not score childless-renter room levels. The model's early-fertility analogue also projects an exact-age-25 CPS outcome from a four-year model age cell; its survey exposure and uncertainty are not fully matched. These limits argue for a measurement audit before attributing a miss to the housing service function. Source: [`e5f_calibration_runtime.py`](../../../../../code/model/tools/e5f_calibration_runtime.py), [`e5f_initial_fertility_observer.py`](../../../../../code/model/tools/e5f_initial_fertility_observer.py).

The first-birth contribution **improves** with finance, while the later-birth contribution falls more. The scored recent-parent row accounts for roughly 59–86% of each fixed-coordinate loss (see the full [`target-fit table`](../../purchase_rule_comparison_v1/readout/target_fit.csv)). Its group composition needs an empirical check before interpreting the miss as a preference parameter failure or changing the target.

## Provisional diagnosis

1. **Ownership gains reach first-birth households, yet births barely move in the solved cohorts.** First-birth households show a large rise in ownership conditional on being observed giving birth. The scored recent-parent difference weakens because it heavily weights later births after earlier children have left, and that subgroup's ownership advantage over the control narrows. This rejects the simple interpretation that new parents fail to gain ownership, but it does not show an exogenous ownership effect on conception. The next question is how purchase access and birth hazards change for the *same inherited childless households*, including those who do not give birth.
2. **The measured attempt-versus-wait value gap narrows; the quarter sign reversal is now localized.** The saved date-zero logit and inclusive values recover both branches at the same inherited states, as shown above. Under the hard rule, the attempt branch loses value at the choice margin and waiting gains relative value; under quarter saving, the attempt branch gains value but waiting gains more. The quarter native one-date calculation finds a small *positive* direct financing effect at fixed prices. The accepted current-price pair reverses it almost entirely, with the renter unit-price change accounting for 95.2% of the joint price effect in the crossed household calculation. The hard-rule direct/price split has not been run. Aggregate wealth/earnings includes housing and does not measure liquid saving.
3. **The housing-service contribution to the measured value gap remains unmeasured.** Conditional first-birth ownership and the first-birth room response both increase with financing, while births move little. The owner service premium $\chi$ multiplies residual room services for both childless households and parents; the physical parent room floor reduces services in both tenures. At identical consumption and residual room services, $\chi=1.09343$ and the consumption share $\alpha=0.733$ raise the Cobb–Douglas composite by only $\chi^{1-\alpha}-1=2.41\%$, regardless of family state. Actual owner and renter budgets and room choices differ, so this is a service-preference comparison, not a full ownership value estimate. Owner access need not deliver a uniquely parental value gain. The difference can arise at the rental cap, discrete owner rungs, financing and transaction costs, or from their dynamic interaction. Matched menu values are needed to determine which matters. The observed first-birth group is selected under each rule, so its ownership change is not a causal response to easier finance among a fixed risk set. Sources: [`shared.py`](../../../../../code/model/experiments/strict_purchase_solvency/source/small_credit_lab/engine/shared.py), [`kernels.py`](../../../../../code/model/experiments/strict_purchase_solvency/source/small_credit_lab/engine/kernels.py), and [`household.py`](../../../../../code/model/experiments/strict_purchase_solvency/source/small_credit_lab/engine/household.py).
   The saved [`hard80`](../../strict_purchase_sandbox_v1/collected/run/strict_purchase/phase_b_ge/selected_root/standard_diagnostics/fertility_policy_by_age_income_state.png) and [`hard100`](../../hard_zero_down_solvency_v2/collected/run/hard100/phase_b_ge/selected_root/standard_diagnostics/fertility_policy_by_age_income_state.png) policy plots show a steep income gradient in childless fertility choices and little visual change when financing increases. The plot is weighted by each solution's risk distribution and is labelled expected children; it does not isolate a matched-state birth response or establish that income, rather than housing, is the binding margin. Source for the plotted calculation: [`diagnostics.py`](../../../../../code/model/experiments/strict_purchase_solvency/source/small_credit_lab/engine/diagnostics.py).
4. **The accepted temporary equilibrium response is small and negative at impact.** The accepted 48- and 64-date temporary paths start from the same inherited households and find date-0 first-birth flows falling about 0.54% (hard) and 0.43% (quarter) as financing rises. For quarter saving, the one-date household calculations above nearly reconstruct the impact response with the accepted current asset price and rent; they do not allocate the equilibrium price movement causally. The no-arbitrage rule is $r_t=u p_t+(p_t-p_{t+1})$. The accepted date-zero price and rent imply that roughly 85.6% of the rent increase comes from the current-minus-next asset-price term, with the rest from $u p_t$; this does not establish a rise in rental demand. One combined market clears renter and owner housing services. Permanent-policy and completed-fertility effects remain unverified. Fertility normalization in a stationary reference is not a policy impact constraint on dated birth flows.

Label the exact economic differences between the two fitted baseline rules before cross-rule comparisons.
2. For the matched initial risk set, decompose first-birth response by beginning tenure, age, wealth relative to each owner rung's down payment, earnings, child state, and location. Report direct changes in purchase feasibility separately from changed tenure preference conditional on feasibility. For existing owners, report the mass choosing $b'<-0.8Q$ under $\phi=1$ to identify use of extra stayer credit. A raw blocked-versus-unblocked birth comparison is selected and is not a causal credit effect.
3. Trace owner purchases and first births jointly: renter-to-owner versus owner-switcher flow, children ever born and currently at home, room rung, and timing around birth. Retain the separate first-birth, later-birth, never-parent empty-home and former-parent empty-home ownership rates. Measure the comparable later-birth share in CPS or PSID histories if feasible; the ACS resident-child field alone cannot reveal older nonresident children.
