**“Structurally absent” fails your own T1 prediction.** You predict nonpositive credit responses in every state. The [verified decomposition](/Users/tommasodesanto/Desktop/Projects/Fertility/Fertility_Spring26/output/model/credit_mechanism_20261004/diagnostics/README.md) instead gives a **+0.165939%** policy contribution at baseline exposure and **−1.040389%** composition contribution, totaling **−0.874450%** explicit births. At fixed states, the exact birth index depends on \(\pi(V_1-V_0-f)/\kappa\). Equal borrowing/saving rates and equal user costs do not force \(\partial_\phi V_1=\partial_\phi V_0\): the branches choose different housing, debt and future allocations and can have different constraint shadow values. The lowest-income, nonpositive-wealth group does receive *less* credit value after success than while waiting: 0.026 versus 0.066 utility units on 92.5% supported exposure. This supports weak or adverse **child-relative** gains there, not an everywhere-absent channel. The \(\phi=1\) reporter failure still limits consumption/saving claims.

**Two-room purchases are a plausible adjustment-cost mechanism, not an established causal chain.** A two-room owner cannot *remain* in that product after childbirth, but the birth branch reoptimizes tenure and can sell, rent, or buy larger housing. “Credit induces a costly move later” is a testable candidate; “credit locks households out of birth” overstates it. The observed negative composition term combines wealth, tenure and fertility histories. New debt exposure does not isolate debt crowd-out; the decomposition even assigns positive composition contributions to debt cells and negative ones to nonnegative-asset cells. Likewise, a common parenthood floor does not mean later children have zero housing-demand effects: equivalence scales and optimal intertemporal allocation remain active. Total rooms are chosen, so calling the observed first-birth room response simply mechanical is inaccurate.
