## Diagnosis: the credit→fertility channel is structurally absent in this model, and the saved evidence already shows it

**Verified facts (read-only, chain 13 reference).**

- Saving and borrowing earn one rate. Resources are `R·b + y` for positive and negative `b` alike (`code/model/production/engine/household.py:298`).
- The only credit objects are an owner collateral floor `b' ≥ −φ·p·h` and a cash test `R·b + y ≥ (1−φ)·p·h` (`household.py:189-190`, `:423-426`, `:896-901`). Renters have zero credit (`household.py:39-48`, `best_params.py:39`).
- Rent equals the owner user cost times price (`engine/equilibrium.py:641`). A child's space costs the same per period whether rented or owned, apart from the 5% owner premium `chi` and the 6% selling cost.
- The parental floor of 2.59 rooms applies at the first child only and never rises for later children (packet README `fable_analysis/credit_mechanism/README.md:20-22`). Renters may hold up to 6 rooms. Parents owning 2 rooms are infeasible by construction (`kernels.py:854-860`).
- Birth choice is a logit over wait versus try, evaluated after optimal tenure and housing, so the floor and down payment are fully internalized at the moment of birth (`household.py:970-994`).
- The stationary root imposes adjusted births equal to 2.1 times entry (`production/equilibrium.py:76`). Every stationary policy endpoint therefore has identical replacement fertility by construction. Only price and population scale can differ. The claimed "population −1.5%" is an endpoint scale, not a fertility effect.

**Saved fixed-price evidence, φ=0.8 versus 1.0 at the common price** (`credit_relaxation/README.md`, `credit_distribution_report_v1`):

| Object (age 22–25 renters at equal `b`, baseline weights) | φ=0.8 | φ=1.0 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Income state 4: childless ownership probability | 0.038 | 0.295 |
| Income state 4: first-birth attempt probability | 0.00562 | 0.00544 |
| Income state 6: childless ownership probability | 0.437 | 0.439 |
| Income state 6: first-birth attempt probability | 0.7379 | 0.7382 |
| All ages: ownership rate | 0.679 | 0.774 |
| Age 42–45: childless share | 18.89% | 19.61% |

Two-room units account for 99.3% of the state-4 ownership gain. Removing the down payment entirely raises young small-unit ownership by a quarter at the constrained state and lowers that state's birth attempt. The unconstrained state, where almost all first births occur, does not move. Aggregate childlessness rises. The historical temporary 100%-financing paths at the hard and quarter calibrations also cut first-period births by 0.43 to 0.54% (daily note 2026-10-02:78-85). The permanently-lowest-productivity population has zero births under both φ values.

**Reading.** With `βR ≈ 0.94`, households are impatient and homeownership is the only borrowing device. Credit relaxation is therefore a consumption-timing instrument: it pulls poor young households into 2-room units, which lock them out of a first birth without a costly move. Nothing in the budget makes a child cheaper to finance, because the child's space is a flow cost at identical rent and user cost and the rental cap of 6 rooms leaves 3.4 service rooms for any family size.

**Answer to question 1.** Income sorting is real but neither necessary nor sufficient. The model does concentrate births at the top (state 4 attempts 0.006 versus state 6 at 0.74 at the same age and wealth), so credit does not bind for marginal parents. But at the state where credit binds hardest, the birth response is slightly negative. Correcting sorting by itself would move births toward households whose saved credit response is non-positive. The weak effect is structural first, compositional second. Timing and weights check out: the overlay uses pre-fertility childless weights at common states, and the φ=1 reporter failure concerns cells with mass near 1e−38 (manifest).

**Answer to question 3.** The model's income state is a single persistent shock with four-year autocorrelation 0.73 and no permanent type (CALIBRATION_STATUS, Earnings). Its gradient by current state at 42–45 is not comparable to CPS children by current household income, which embeds mothers' labor-supply responses and permanent-type sorting. The relevant target is completed fertility by permanent household income or education, which is roughly flat in the 2000s US. The decisive failure is the age-26 reversal (model 0.08/0.53/1.10 versus data 0.64/0.45/0.23, user claim 6, unverified data). My derivation, not a verified fact: with flow utility `−e(m)/x + ψ m^{1−γ}` at σ=2, the first child's net flow value at unchanged spending is `ψ − (e(1)−1)/x` with `e(1)−1 = 0.234` and `ψ = 0.179`. It is negative below a composite of about 1.3 and positive above. A time cost proportional to earnings scales spending by `(1−τ)` and leaves the cost term `[e/(1−τ) − 1]/x`, still falling in `x`. So within this utility, time costs cannot reverse the gradient unless spending is strongly concave in income. The additive utility prize plus equivalence-scale cost is the Jones–Schoonbroodt–Tertilt structure, and the means-tested benefit the author tried is a direct patch on exactly that term, which is why it "nearly fixes" the gradient. It is a preference change, not a causal credit experiment.

## Three decisive tests, ranked

**T1. Common-state decomposition from the two saved φ arrays, zero solves.** Compute the first-birth attempt difference at every (age, income state, wealth, inherited tenure) cell, weighted by the φ=0.8 pre-fertility childless distribution, and split the aggregate into within-cell policy change at constrained cells versus composition. Structural hypothesis predicts non-positive differences in all cells, including constrained ones. Sorting hypothesis predicts clearly positive differences in constrained cells with small mass. The two published cells already point to structural. Decision: if structural, stop treating mortgage credit as the paper's fertility lever and do not add a means-tested benefit to revive it. If sorting, the credit story survives conditional on fixing heterogeneity.

**T2. One fixed-price solve at a 10% higher price, chain-13 parameters.** Rent follows automatically. Measure first-birth attempts by state for childless renters and completed children at 42–45. This tests the paper's actual narrative, "children need space," independently of credit. The child floor costs about 0.36 per four-year period, roughly 9% of mean annual earnings per year, so a 10% price rise adds about 1% of period income. If births fall by less than about 0.3%, the space channel is quantitatively dead at this calibration and the slides' rebated-tax conclusion has no household mechanism behind it. If births fall materially, the narrative stands with credit demoted to an ownership and timing margin.

**T3. Two fixed-price solves with the rental cap lowered to 4 rooms, φ=0.8 versus 1.0.** `hR_max` is an ordinary native field. This checks the author's unverified claim that a tight cap does not create a credit channel. If constrained-cell attempts now rise with φ, tenure segmentation is the minimal structural ingredient, and the external restriction is the AHS distribution of rental units by room count, which the slides already cite. If attempts still do not rise, segmentation cannot rescue credit either. The rental-wedge switch is a cleaner alternative, but the native purchase-income mode rejects it (`household.py:718`), so it needs code.

## Contribution of the unchanged model, limits, and the conditional decision

**What the unchanged model can claim.** Fertility responds to the cost of family space, not to mortgage credit. Credit under a single rate and tenure-neutral space costs raises young small-unit ownership and slightly lowers first births. That is a sharp, citable negative result against extrapolating Dettling–Kearney-style credit evidence, and it is consistent with the theory ledger, which already records that a 90% reform initially lowers fertility as tenure shifts (claim ledger row 32). The normative result is separate: the title-transfer Pareto improvement requires a binding young down-payment constraint (ledger row 65). The saved evidence says that constraint does not bind for the households that have children. The theory's premise is not met by the quantitative model's marginal parents, so the welfare result should not be presented as a fertility result. Property-tax gross-versus-net supplier accounting changes the supply response in policy runs, not the household credit mechanism, and remains a separate decision.

**Limits that an adviser will see.** The parental floor sits at its bound (2.594 of 2.6) and still misses the first-birth room response (1.38 versus 1.465); the response is mechanical rather than chosen. Later children need no space, and the 3-plus-versus-1–2 room gap is missed (0.30 versus 0.385, unscored). Early fertility is 0.53 against 0.81. No permanent transition has passed gates.

**Decision, conditional on evidence.** If T1 confirms structural and T2 shows a live space channel: keep the model, reframe credit as timing, and make one minimal change grounded in an existing moment. Free the per-child floor (`hbar_child_rooms`, currently zero) and promote the 3-plus-versus-1–2 room gap from validation to scored. That gives the second and third child a space need, which is what the title claims, without new preference objects. If T2 shows a dead space channel, the contribution is at risk regardless of credit, and the next step is identification, not retuning. Replace the age-25 stock target with completed fertility by permanent income or education and reinstate permanent income types, which the September architecture had. Adopt no means-tested child utility term. Adopt no rate spread for this purpose: a spread makes borrowing costlier and cannot create a child-specific financing benefit.

**Unknown or unverified.** All scratch magnitudes in the user claims (ownership +11–15 points, births −0.25% to +1%, renter credit −5%, benefit lifting the credit response 1–3%, CPS terciles) await Sol recovery. Identification rank of the ten-parameter fit and grid adequacy are uncertified. I inspected no transition code and endorse no run beyond the pinned φ pair.
