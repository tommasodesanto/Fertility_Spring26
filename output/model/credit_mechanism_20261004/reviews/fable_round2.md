**Revised verdict.** I accept Astra's central correction. The verified decomposition rejects my "structurally absent, nonpositive everywhere" prediction. What survives is narrower: there is no tenure-cost asymmetry for a child's space, and the measured credit response is small, heterogeneous and dominated by an adverse exposure shift. I also withdraw the age-25 target substitution, the permanent-type proposal, the T2 cutoff, the Dettling–Kearney attribution, and "mechanical" for the room response.

## What changed, with evidence

**Structural absence versus small heterogeneous effects.** Measured accounting from the common-state packet (`diagnostics/flow_decomposition.csv`):

| First births, φ=0.8→1.0 at fixed price | Policy at baseline exposure | Composition |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ages 18–21 | −0.34% of that age flow | 0 |
| Ages 22–25 | −0.30% | negative |
| Ages 26–29 | +0.16% | negative |
| Ages 30–45 | +0.65% to +2.6% | small |
| All explicit births | +0.166% | −1.040% |

The positive responses sit among older childless renters with nonnegative assets, and in the compact income-by-wealth table only the high-income groups and the middle-income high-wealth group respond positively. Low-income groups respond negatively at every wealth level. So the channel is not everywhere nonpositive. Proof-level content that remains: rent equals the owner user cost (`engine/equilibrium.py:641`) and one rate applies to both signs of wealth (`household.py:298`), so the renter path supplies a child's space at the same flow cost without any credit. Astra is right that the two branches still have different constraint shadow values; the parent's larger down payment on four or more rooms is a child-specific credit term, and it is what older owner-track households respond to. Its measured size is small.

**Negative exposure versus causal lock-in.** I downgrade lock-in to a hypothesis. The composition term is negative because exposure moves from renter cells with high birth rates into two-room debt cells with low ones, but the decomposition cannot separate selection into small-unit ownership from state dependence. The clean zero-solve test is a same-arm comparison of the first-birth attempt probability for a childless two-room debtor and a childless renter at equal net worth, income and age, which the saved φ=0.8 arrays support. I am not proposing it as the priority item.

**Whether the gradient explains the credit response.** It does not, and here Astra and I agree. Policy responses are positive exactly where the model already places births and non-positive in the low-income cells. Re-sorting births toward low-income households would move them to cells whose common-state response is zero or negative. The recovered larger response in the "benefit world" comes from an unfinanced cash floor of 0.75 plus 0.6 per child with ψ lowered to 0.0618, so it is a resource effect from a bundled change, not evidence about sorting (`evidence/RECOVERY.md`, `CLAIMS.csv` row 8). The gradient itself remains a validation failure: the audit shows the model's positive sorting survives grouping, age and family-unit checks, while the baseline-vintage income gradient is unavailable rather than flat.

**Whether the theory requires a positive quantitative fertility effect.** It does not. The title-transfer proposition holds fertility fixed (ledger rows 25 and 66). The fertility propositions are conditional on a binding down payment written as `b/[(1−φ)P]` before income (`simplified_olg_amendment_proposal.tex:536`, `:586-599`), while the quantitative screen adds four-year income. The ledger already records that a 90% reform lowers fertility initially (row 32). The theory's constrained young owner is a different household from the quantitative model's marginal parent, who is high-income and unconstrained. The positive policy contribution therefore stands as a welfare statement about constrained young owners. It rejects an everywhere-nonpositive channel but does not need, and does not have, a quantitatively important fertility effect.

**Corrections accepted.** Hacamo and van Doornik et al. are the direct credit-access precedents; Dettling–Kearney is a price-by-ownership design. The frozen-productivity label applies only to that separate experiment. The first-birth room response is floor-driven, with the floor at its bound, not purely mechanical. No rejection threshold attaches to any price test.

## What I maintain

Credit relief at fixed prices does not raise aggregate births at this point: −0.87% explicit births for φ=1.0, and −0.25% in the recovered 0.95 scratch pair, which lacks runtime certification. Stationary endpoints impose replacement fertility, so the "population −1.5%" is a scale statement and no fertility claim can come from endpoints. The recovered tax pair, also uncertified, shows fixed-price births falling about 15% without a rebate and about 0.4% with one (`preserved/property_tax_ss.json:36-38`). As a hypothesis, this says the model's births are highly elastic to household resources and weakly responsive to compensated carrying costs and to credit terms. That ordering matters more for the paper than the credit sign.

**Strongest defensible unchanged-model claim.** At the working calibration and fixed prices, removing the down payment raises ownership by about ten points and shifts childless households into small indebted units, while changing births by under one percent in either direction. Small positive responses arise among older, higher-income childless renters; the young respond negatively; the exposure shift dominates. Adequate rentable family space at tenure-neutral cost is the substitute margin. The model's fertility moves with resources, not with mortgage access. This is measured for φ=1.0 and awaits the authenticated φ=0.95 pair for the policy-relevant size.

**Single most useful remaining item.** An empirical restriction, not a solve: first-birth hazards by predetermined pre-birth household earnings tercile at ages 22–33 in the PSID panel the project already uses for the first-birth room contrast. The model predicts attempt probabilities near zero in the bottom tercile and near 0.7 in the top at the same age. If the data gradient is flat or negative, the model misplaces the birth margin relative to every constraint, credit included, and the tax-rebate redistribution results inherit the error. This moment adds to, not replaces, the age-25 stock. The identifying variation is cross-sectional dispersion in birth timing by predetermined resources at fixed age, which the pooled stock cannot supply. The affected parameters are the child-value block (`psi_child`, `child_benefit_curvature`, `first_birth_fixed_cost`) against the externally imposed equivalence-scale exponent of 0.7. If that gradient rejects the model, the first economic change is to that child-cost block, identified by that moment with all current targets retained. Not a transfer floor, not a credit parameter, and not a change chosen for its credit response. Sol's φ=0.95 pair and financing census remain the owners of the remaining model-side numbers.
