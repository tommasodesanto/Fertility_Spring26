## 1. Executive assessment

**Established.**

1. **[V] Where the fit stands.** Primary loss 19.58 at block0506. Early fertility (children ever born at age 25) contributes 7.51, 38 percent of the total. The other nine scored rows sum to 12.07, and every one of them sits within about two working-scale units of its target. The largest of those nine misses are the first-birth rooms response (1.62 vs 1.47, contribution 3.40), aggregate wealth to earnings (6.33 vs 6.93, 2.74), mean rooms (5.85 vs 5.73, 1.80), the recent-parent ownership gap (0.120 vs 0.128, 1.76) and ownership 30–55 (0.655 vs 0.676, 1.08). Full table in the appendix.

2. **[V] The early-fertility target is not a model-consistent object, and most of the gap is time aggregation.** The model has four-year periods from age 18, at most one birth per period, and measures children ever born at exact age 25 as `P(birth in cell 18–21) + 0.875 × P(birth in cell 22–25)`. The mean first-birth age target is built on the same cells, so matching it pins the model's first-birth timing to the data's cell shares (34.2 percent of first births in the first cell, 21.6 percent in the second). Under those shares and the targeted childlessness rate, the model's early fertility is bounded by `0.425 + 0.240 p2`, where `p2` is the share of first-cell mothers with a second birth in the next cell. The ceiling at `p2 = 1` is 0.665. A data-plausible `p2` of 0.5–0.7 gives 0.55–0.59. The model is at 0.535. The 0.274 gap to 0.810 is therefore mostly the model's inability to count closely spaced second and third births and pre-18 births as separate children, not a preference or search failure.

3. **[V] Locally, early fertility cannot be moved independently of the other fertility moments.** From the current Jacobian, the early-fertility gain per unit log-parameter step is 0.87 unconstrained, 0.08 with mean first-birth age held fixed, 0.06 with childlessness also held, and 0.012 with the one-child share also held. Every early-fertility "improvement" produced so far sits on the same locally predicted trade-off line: the overnight relative-weight lane (early 0.657, mean age 24.05, other-moment loss 550), the half and quarter first-birth-scale probes (0.602 and 0.667, other-moment loss 492 and 1398), and the doubled-continuation profile (0.688, mean age 23.69, other-moment loss 806). The linearized frontier prices each 0.10 of early fertility at about 1.4 years of mean first-birth age and hundreds of loss points elsewhere (366 for the first 0.10, about 700 for the next).

4. **[V] The searches have established very little beyond the Jacobian.** The six DE lanes have had roughly ten successful evaluations each in ten dimensions (59 search successes across six lanes at 15:46), 49 percent of search proposals time out, and the quadruple-continuation lane is inadmissible because the normalized child benefit turns negative. The 40-probe Jacobian is the informative product of the run; the DE stage cannot support reachability, optimality, or global-identification statements and the contract correctly says so.

5. **[P] The primary lane is not at a local optimum for the nine other rows.** A linearized Gauss–Newton step from block0506 (largest log step 0.30, all parameters inside bounds) predicts primary loss 9.5 with early fertility unchanged at 0.53: first-birth fixed cost 0.62 to 0.46, first-birth scale 0.176 to 0.136, continuation scale 0.33 to 0.26, annual discount factor 0.9635 to 0.969, housing loading 0.135 to 0.120. This is a linear prediction; the quarter-scale probe reproduced the Jacobian's first-order prediction closely, so it is worth one objective evaluation to test. If it holds, the local random-proposal design, not the model, is what limits the fit of the other nine rows.

6. **[V] Numerical failures do not overturn the conclusions but censor the direction the early-fertility lanes need.** Timeouts occur after 4–8 completed equilibrium solves, never in the first, and 54 of 61 evening timeouts died inside the child-benefit normalization loop. Proposals that move the normalized benefit far from the warm start (continuation scale, curvature, first-birth cost) are the ones censored; all five frontier timeouts involved continuation-scale moves.

**Unresolved.**

- **[H] Sign of the young-age fertility–income gradient.** The standard fertility-policy plot shows expected births rising monotonically in the income state at every age, with the lowest states near zero; in the data, births before 25 are concentrated among low-income and low-education women. If the model's gradient is wrong-signed, no reweighting closes the early-fertility gap; only a mechanism (opportunity cost of time, permanent taste or type heterogeneity) can.
- **[V] Period–cohort mixing in the fertility block.** Timing rows are 2003–2006 period objects; childlessness and the one-child share are the 1960–66 cohorts (CPS women 40–44 in 2004/2006, completed fertility about 1.89 uncapped); early fertility is the 1979–81 cohorts; the normalization is replacement 2.1. With the two cohort moments matched and 2.1 imposed with the 3.602 top-bin weight, the model must put about 0.43 of women at three or more children against about 0.29 in the CPS cohort, and about 0.19 at exactly two against about 0.34.
- **[V] Wealth and ownership age profiles.** The aggregate wealth ratio miss (−9 percent) hides a profile error: model wealth at 22–33 is 1.2–1.8 times the PSID, and at 78–85 it is about 0.4 of the PSID. Old-age ownership is 0.95 against ACS 0.75. Whether the young are credit constrained enough for the paper's mechanism depends on this.
- **[P] Income-chain constructor.** The generic transition constructor in `parameters.py:1281–1286` is a reset-to-stationary chain, while the status notes describe the B15 process as Rouwenhorst. Which one the checkpoint carries needs a zero-solve print of the transition matrix.

**Recommended direction.** Stop treating 0.810 as a number the model should hit. Re-express the early-fertility information as the object the model can represent (share of women with any birth by [25,26), already computed by the CPS builder, plus a cell-collapsed count if desired), put the fertility block on one time basis, verify the Gauss–Newton prediction with one solve and switch the local search to damped Gauss–Newton, and test the young-age fertility–income gradient before any model change.

## 2. Technical analysis

### Q1. What model is being calibrated

The pinned runtime is `code/model/tools/e5f_evening_calibration_runtime.py` (contract `files.runtime`, SHA verified against the working file), which imports `e5f_calibration_runtime.py` for the normalization loop and loads the household solver `code/model/intergen_eqscale_seq_optimized/solver.py` with all parameters from the checkpoint `…/night_launch_v4/primary_continuation/search/de_0093/case`, not from constructor defaults. **[V]**

**Household and timing.** A household enters at 18, lives in four-year cells (`period_years = 4`), works to about 66 and can act to about 82 (`J = 17`, `J_R = 12`; defaults, not runtime-asserted). Fertility choices are offered in cells 1–7, ages 18–45 (`A_f_start = 1`, `A_f_end = 7`, asserted by the observer at `e5f_initial_fertility_observer.py:83–84`). Two child states are distinct: children ever born $n \in \{0,1,2,3^+\}$ and children at home $m \le \min(n,3)$ (`parameters.py:1059–1060`). Children leave home with a per-child hazard that depends on the parent's age only: 0.05 per cell before parent age 34, rising linearly to 1 at 62 (`parameters.py:1102–1121`, `use_stochastic_aging` required). A child born at parent age 18 therefore stays home for roughly five cells (about twenty years) in expectation; a child born at 38 for about two cells. **[V]**

**Utility.** With $x = c^{\alpha(m)} h^{1-\alpha(m)}$ and $\sigma = 2$ fixed,
$$u = e(m)\,\frac{x^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma} + \psi\, m^{1-\gamma},\qquad e(m)=\Big(\tfrac{2+0.7m}{2}\Big)^{0.7},\qquad \alpha(m)=\alpha_0-\delta\,\mathbf 1\{m\ge 1\},$$
with $\alpha_0 = 0.733$ fixed, $\delta$ = `delta_alpha_jump` searched, later-child loading `delta_alpha` fixed at zero, and **no minimum housing requirement** (`h_bar = 0`, `child_room_floor = False`; `solver.py:2578–2592`). The child benefit is $\psi m^{1-\gamma}$ with $\gamma$ = `child_benefit_curvature` searched in $[0,0.8]$ and $\psi$ = `psi_child` normalized, applied to children **at home** (`child_preferences.py:27–30`). So the "housing requirement" in this specification is a budget-share shift at the first child, and the equivalence scale $e(m)$ is a separate multiplicative effect on flow utility; neither is a floor. Owners receive housing services scaled by $\chi$ = `chi` (owner premium, `solver.py:2681–2682`). **[V]**

**Fertility choice.** Sequential binary logits, one birth at most per cell. The first birth (`solver.py:3498–3506`):
$$V^{\text{try}} = \pi_j\,(V^{n=1}_{\text{dest}} - \kappa_{\text{fbc}}) + (1-\pi_j) V^{n=0},\qquad V = \kappa_1 \log\!\big(e^{V^{\text{wait}}/\kappa_1}+e^{V^{\text{try}}/\kappa_1}\big),$$
with $\kappa_1$ = `kappa_fert`, the first-birth fixed cost $\kappa_{\text{fbc}}$ = `first_birth_fixed_cost` charged only on success, and fecundity $\pi_j = 1$ at every age unless the checkpoint sets `fecundity_omega1` (default off). Second and third births (`solver.py:3525–3551`) use the same structure with scale $\kappa_2$ = `kappa_fert_continuation` and no fixed cost; no choice exists at $n = 3^+$. **[V]** Two arithmetic implications worth stating: (i) at most one birth per four-year cell, which drives Q2; (ii) each logit node adds option value $\kappa \ln 2$ when the two branches are close, that is 0.12 per cell at the first-birth node and 0.23 per cell at continuation nodes at the anchor, against a per-child benefit $\psi \approx 0.136$ per cell. Fertility in this model is substantially shock-driven, and the continuation scale is itself a fertility lever: quadrupling it makes replacement fertility attainable with a negative $\psi$, which is exactly the inadmissibility observed in the quadruple lane. **[V]**

**Tenure.** Smoothed logit over tenure with scale `tenure_choice_kappa` (`solver.py:3039–3040, 3104–3108`), searched in $[0.001, 0.1]$ (log). Renter cap 6 rooms, owner rungs up to 10, selling cost 0.06, all inherited. **[V]**

**Credit.** Purchase: down payment $(1-\phi)\,p h$ with $\phi = 0.8$ asserted (`solver.py:2711–2713`; `e5f_evening_calibration_runtime.py:157`). Existing owners (the September 27 adopted DUE stayer rule, commit a81f0593): debt may not grow above the inherited balance, inherited above-limit debt is grandfathered after a price fall, and a death-solvency floor applies (`solver.py:143–156`). Payment-to-income is deferred; `native_solvency_credit` is asserted off. Purchase restrictions are unchanged; only the stayer rule changed. **[V]**

**Income, rate, entry, bequests, closure.** One persistent Markov state on 15 grid points ("B15"), deterministic age profile, flat pension in retirement; a single gross return $R = 1 + q$ on saving and debt with $q$ = 2 percent annual (checkpoint value; the constructor default is 4 percent). Entrant wealth and income come from the checkpoint's conditional arrays. Warm-glow bequests $\theta_0(1+\theta_n m)\,(\theta_1 + b)^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)$ with $\theta_0$ searched, $\theta_1 = 0.0082$ fixed by declaration only (not runtime-asserted), $\theta_n$ inherited and absent from the 31-row parameter table. Positive net estates fund entrant assets, the residual goes to a non-utility sink (`e5f_overnight_estate_audit.py:159–172`). Single housing market with static elastic supply, elasticity 0.63 around the initial price and supply scale $H_0$ searched; balanced PAYGO with pension ratio 0.229; closed population requiring births per entrant household equal to 2.1 within $5\times10^{-4}$ (`adult_entry.py:88–98`). The child-benefit level $\psi$ is solved to hit that 2.1 with at most 23 stationary solves (`e5f_calibration_runtime.py:216–219`), and $\psi \le 0$ is rejected (`e5f_calibration_runtime.py:37–43`). **[V]**

**Approved versus numerical versus experimental** (from `CALIBRATION_STATUS.md` and the September 27 daily note; line references in the appendix ledger): author-adopted are the DUE stayer credit rule, the retained 2 percent rate, the B15 income process (inherited, not newly changed), the searched tenure scale, estate-funded entry with a residual sink, and the share-shift utility with no floor. Numerical repairs only: the warm-start $\psi$ guess, the classifier fix, the import-path fix, the price-start bracketing sensitivity. Experimental and not adopted: the 10× and 100× weights, the identity/relative-error lane, the fixed continuation-scale profiles. **[V]**

**Discrepancies found.** (a) `primary_objective.json` still says "13 scored moments for 9 free parameters"; the live contract is 10 scored, 3 validation, 1 normalization, 10 searched. (b) `e5f_parenthood_utility.py` metadata advertises an active housing floor that no bind path applies. (c) The near-bound flags on both fertility scales are artifacts of a raw-range rule (`panel_validator.py:59`): with bounds $[0.02, 50]$ the threshold is 0.50, so any scale below 0.52 is "near" the lower bound; in log units the scales sit 28 and 36 percent of the way across the interval. (d) $\theta_1$ and $\theta_n$ are not asserted at runtime. (e) The income-chain constructor question above. None of these changes the economics; (a) and (c) generate misleading monitoring language. **[V]**

**Is the early-fertility versus mean-age tension real?** Yes, but its source is measurement, not preferences. Three verified facts: (i) the NCHS counts file gives first-cell share 0.342 (including 7.7 percent of first births before age 18), second-cell share 0.216; (ii) the model observer counts at most one birth per cell and weights second-cell births by 0.875; (iii) the mean-age target is built on the same cells, so a model that matches it has the data's cell shares. Then `early ≤ 0.802 × [0.342 × (1 + 0.875 p2) + 0.216 × 0.875] = 0.425 + 0.240 p2 ≤ 0.665`. In the data, a woman with births at 17, 20 and 24 has three children at 25; the model can record at most two, with the second weighted 0.875. Raising the model's number above 0.665 requires moving first births into cell 1 beyond the data's share, which is what lowers mean first-birth age. This is what the Jacobian, the frontier probes and the search lanes all show. **[V]** The cohort–period wedge is secondary: my recollection of 2004–06 age-specific birth rates puts the period synthetic-cohort value at roughly 0.77 rather than 0.81, which should be checked on the natality files rather than trusted. **[H]**

**The 2.1 normalization and the cohort moments.** The CPS 2004 extraction (`fertility_availability.json`) shows unweighted 40–44 counts of 1060, 950, 1936 and 1654 women at 0, 1, 2 and 3+ children (shares 0.189, 0.170, 0.346, 0.295; mean 1.895). With childlessness and the one-child share matched and completed fertility fixed at 2.1 using the 3.602 top-bin weight, the model's implied shares are about 0.20, 0.17, 0.19, 0.43. The normalization is a closure requirement (stationary closed population), so this is not a target to drop; it is a reason to state the fertility block on one time basis and to read the family-rooms validation row (3+ versus 1–2 rooms gap, 0.353 vs 0.385) with that composition in mind. **[V]**

**Measurement caveats carried by the contract** that remain open: household "reproductive member" is not certified female exposure; the CPS bootstrap is not design-consistent; the recent-parent and first-birth-rooms observers are matched-state proxies, not the panel estimators. These are disclosed and unchanged. **[V]**

Timeouts are not economic nonexistence: two evening timeout points replayed successfully under the longer cap. **[V]** But the censoring is not neutral. The normalization warm start is the anchor's $\psi = 0.1428$ with step 0.005; the profile lanes' normalized $\psi$ values are 0.170 (half) and 0.065 (double), so those lanes spend their solve budget bracketing. The five frontier timeouts were exactly the continuation-scale and paired cases; the evening designs with the most timeouts were the joint-local proposals (34 of 84) and the first-birth-cost coordinate moves (6 of 18). So the region that early-fertility lanes must explore is the region most likely to be censored. **[V]**

**Numerical improvement that preserves the economics and gates.** Use the Jacobian's $\partial\psi/\partial\theta$ row (already measured) to set the warm-start guess $\psi_0 = \psi_{\text{anchor}} + \sum_i (\partial\psi/\partial\theta_i)\Delta\theta_i$ and a bracket step proportional to the predicted move, per lane. Only the root-finder's initial guess changes; tolerance, cap and gates are untouched. Expected effect: two to four fewer solves per objective on far proposals, which is the difference between 1100 s and a timeout. The price-bracketing sensitivity (original start fails, half start passes) deserves the same treatment: a bracket expansion strategy rather than a fixed start. **[P]**

### Q6. Do the weights make sense

The loss is a diagonal minimum-distance criterion on raw gaps with fixed working scales; it is not efficient GMM, there is no covariance, no standard errors, no overidentification test, and the contract says so. Most scales are near survey standard errors (CPS generalized-variance SEs for the 40–44 rows, bootstrap SEs for wealth and ownership rows), two are time-series dispersions (the NCHS annual SD, 0.085 years), one is synthetic (bequests, 0.00044), and two are judgments: early fertility at 0.10 against a bootstrap SE of 0.028 (weight 100 versus 1272 by inverse variance) and first-birth rooms at 0.085 against a clustered SE of 0.050 (138 versus 396). **[V]**

Consequences. Under the primary weights, early fertility is already the dominant contribution (38 percent) although it is down-weighted 12.7-fold relative to its own SE; under inverse-variance weights it would contribute about 96 and swamp the objective, and the 10× lane is the one closest to statistical weighting. The relative-error "identity" lane is not neutral either: it prices a full year of mean first-birth age at $(1/26)^2$, which is why that lane bought early fertility with two years of timing. **[V]**

Judgment. Raising the early-fertility weight is not the right response to a target the model cannot represent; it only moves the model along the trade-off line. Nor should the row be dropped: it carries the only information about young-age timing, which the mean-age row, being a full-window average, does not pin down. The defensible move is to replace the object (Q8, experiment 1) and then weight it at its own SE, which preserves the identification of the first-birth scale and fixed cost from young-age timing and leaves the other blocks untouched. Second, the first-birth rooms row is the largest non-fertility contribution and is weighted below its SE while its model observer is a matched-state proxy; that row's weight is a judgment on proxy error, and should be recorded as such rather than as a statistical scale. **[P]**

### Q7. Are the policies and distributions plausible

From the 17-plot packet for block0506 (`saved_review_v2/primary_page1–3.png`) and the morning lifecycle table:

- **Market clearing.** Relative residual $1.8\times10^{-9}$; one market; supply and demand 5.85 rooms per household. **[V]**
- **Fertility gradient.** Expected births rise monotonically in the income state at every age; the four lowest of fifteen states have essentially zero births at all ages; completed children rise from 1.65 to 2.05 across states while ownership rises from 0.24 to 0.90. Children are a strongly normal good in this model. The empirical early-fertility gradient by income and education is negative, so the model produces its (too few) early births from the wrong part of the distribution. This is the main economic hypothesis of this review (Q8, experiment 3). **[H]**
- **Age profile of children.** Model capped children ever born against CPS by cell: 0.13 vs 0.24 at 18–21, 0.42 vs 0.64 at 22–25, 0.74 vs 1.04 at 26–29, 1.06 vs 1.45 at 30–33, 1.61 vs 1.71 at 38–41. The shortfall starts in the first cell and is proportionally largest there; it closes by 40 because the normalization forces catch-up through third births. **[V]**
- **Housing near the grid maximum.** Mean housing for the top three income states is 8.5–9.3 rooms against an owner maximum of 10; the owner-rung plot implies roughly a fifth of households at the top rung. The dip at age 30 for the highest state is visible. Rooms by age exceed the ACS by 0.55–0.62 rooms at 26–45, with the excess shrinking to 0.15 by 54–57, and fall short after 66. This matters for mean rooms, the family-rooms validation row and any price elasticity. **[V]**
- **Ownership.** Rises monotonically with age to 0.95 at 82–85 against ACS 0.75; high-income ownership peaks at 0.95 around 22–30 and declines to 0.85 by 60. The owner-entry probability of the childless renter at 30 is flat at 0.83 across the whole wealth grid and slightly declining at the top. With additive Gumbel tenure shocks and concave utility, the owning-versus-renting utility gap shrinks relative to the shock scale as consumption rises, so choice probabilities flatten toward one half at high wealth; this is the likely mechanism behind the high-wealth ownership decline. **[H]** The occupied wealth region at 30 and 42 is 0–30 in earnings units, while the policy plots run to 3000, so the standard packet does not resolve the occupied region; that is a packet limitation, not a policy finding. **[V]**
- **Wealth profile.** Liquid wealth peaks at 62–66 and is decumulated to about −1 by 82 in every income state. Against the PSID: 0.19 vs 0.19 at 18–21 (entry is right), 0.80 vs 0.44 at 22–25, 1.99 vs 1.32 at 30–33, 7.86 vs 11.70 at 58–61, 2.34 vs 6.21 at 82–85. Young households save more than in the data and the old hold far less. **[V]** Given the B15 innovation SD of 0.48 per cell, strong precautionary saving by the young is the obvious candidate; the old-age decumulation is the standard consequence of no medical or longevity risk with a small warm glow ($\theta_0 = 0.12$). **[H]** For the paper's mechanism this is not a side issue: if the young are richer than in the data, the down-payment constraint binds less in the model than in reality.
- **Bequest flow.** Matched (0.00706 vs 0.00729) while old-age wealth is a third of the data's, so the flow is generated by a different age composition of deaths than in the SCF construction. A zero-solve decomposition of model estates by age at death would show whether the bequest row disciplines old-age saving at all. **[H]**

**The poor PSID net-worth comparison at old ages.** The PSID audit found no arithmetic or unit error, the aggregate reproduces to $5\times10^{-14}$, and the oldest cell has 245 family-years; topcodes, outliers and weights are unverified. Ranking the explanations: (1) population mismatch with a known sign, since PSID survivors at 82–85 are wealth-selected and the model's survival does not depend on wealth; (2) missing mechanisms, medical expense risk and a bequest motive that binds at old age; (3) a calibration effect through $\beta$ and $\theta_0$ that is bounded by the bequest and aggregate-wealth rows; (4) measurement, plausible for the level but not for a factor of 2.7 with 245 family-years. Bequests are not established as the explanation, and the matched bequest flow argues against a simple "stronger warm glow" story. **[P]**

### Q8. What to do next: three discriminating checks

**1. Measurement: replace the early-fertility object with what the model can represent.** Hypothesis: the early-fertility gap is mostly time aggregation. Intervention: from the same CPS builder, take the share of women with any birth by [25,26) (`build_early_fertility_target.py:34`, `share_with_any_birth`, already computed) and, if birth histories are available (NSFG 2002 or 2006–10, or NCHS order-and-interval data), the cell-collapsed count that records at most one birth per four-year cell with pre-18 births folded into the first cell; produce the model counterparts from the block0506 checkpoint with zero solves (the observer already has the [25,26) machinery). Held fixed: everything. Outputs: data and model values with SEs, the decomposition of the 0.274 gap into first-birth timing, collapsed higher-order births, and cohort–period wedge. Support: the ever-mother gap is below 0.05 and the collapsed-count gap is small. Reject: the ever-mother gap is 0.15 or more, which would mean the model's first-birth timing itself is too late and the mean-age row is hiding it. Scope: data work and one zero-solve Torch job; CPS microdata lives on Torch or the Datasets folder. Author decision: needed to adopt any new target row; not needed to run the check.

**2. Search and numerics: test the Gauss–Newton prediction and switch to a directed local method.** Hypothesis: the primary lane is far from a local optimum in the non-early rows because the random-proposal design is misaligned with the loss valley. Intervention: one objective evaluation at the predicted point (fixed cost 0.458, first scale 0.136, continuation scale 0.257, $\beta$ 0.969, loading 0.120, $H_0$ 6.15, $\chi$ 1.095, $\theta_0$ 0.130, curvature 0.062, tenure scale 0.0114; exact values in `linear_analysis_outputs.md`), then, if realized loss is within about 30 percent of the predicted 9.5, a damped Gauss–Newton loop of three iterations (ten forward differences plus one step each, 33 objectives) with the Jacobian-based $\psi$ warm start from Q5. Held fixed: targets, weights, bounds, gates, normalization. Outputs: realized versus predicted loss and moments, full 14-row and 31-row tables, 17 plots, per-objective solve counts. Support: loss near 10 with early fertility flat, confirming that the search design is the constraint for the nine rows. Reject: realized loss above 16, indicating strong nonlinearity within a 0.3 log step, in which case the Jacobian's guidance should be restricted to smaller steps. Scope: about one to two Torch hours on 24 workers. Author decision: a new bounded contract; no economic decision.

**3. Economics: sign the young-age fertility–income gradient before any model change.** Hypothesis: the model's early births come from high-income households while the data's come from low-income households, so the residual early-fertility gap is compositional and no reweighting fixes it. Intervention: zero-solve tabulation from the block0506 checkpoint of the share with any birth by 25 and children ever born at 25 by income-state tercile, plus the systematic net birth value relative to the scale, $\Delta v/\kappa_1$, by age and state; compare with CPS 2004/2006 women at 25 by family income or education. Held fixed: everything. Outputs: two gradient tables and the value-by-state plot. Support: model gradient positive, data gradient negative. Reject: both flat or both negative. Scope: one zero-solve job and one CPS tabulation. Author decision: only if a mechanism change follows, for example a wage-proportional time cost of children (Becker; Jones, Schoonbroodt and Tertilt 2010) or permanent taste types; that is a structural change and needs explicit authorization.

**Not yet worth time:** more DE or random-proposal lanes; 100× weights or the relative-error lane as anything but diagnostics; dropping or demoting early fertility; global reachability or optimality claims; changing $\phi$; reverting the credit rule; reconstructing the PSID wealth targets; a floor specification the author has already declined; transitions or policy runs on an uncertified point.

## 3. Missing evidence and limitations

- Torch-only files were not inspected: `run_v1/complete.json` (per-case wall time and failure reasons), the `gated_v1` case folders, the CPS early-fertility receipt, the frontier driver, and the CPS and PSID microdata. Per-lane timeout counts for the identification run are therefore inferred from status totals and the frontier pattern, not tabulated.
- The lane snapshot is from about 13:25 EDT; results after that, including the half-scale profile case 0133, are not reflected.
- Linear predictions use the full-step Jacobian; the half-step matrix gives the same conclusions, but nonlinearity beyond 0.3 log steps is untested except along the first-birth scale.
- The 2004–2006 period synthetic-cohort early fertility (about 0.77) and the CPS ever-mother share at 25 (about 0.47) are recollections, marked as hypotheses.
- Checkpoint values of $\theta_n$, fecundity, survival, the income transition matrix and the retirement income scale were not read; only the 31-row parameter table and the code paths were.
- I did not read the plot-generation code, so the units of the "expected births" curve in `fertility_by_age.png` are unverified; its sum over ages (about 1.1) does not reconcile with the 2.1 normalization without a units note, which the packet should carry.

## 4. Direct answer

**Given the evidence, where is this calibration going, and what would you do next if this were your paper?**

It is going in circles on the wrong number. The housing, wealth and later-fertility rows are close and, on the linear evidence, could be tightened substantially with a directed local method; the early-fertility row is being chased with weights and profiles along a trade-off line that the model's own time aggregation creates. The model cannot record two children born three years apart to a 19-year-old, and the target counts them. If this were my paper I would, in this order: (1) recompute the early-fertility information as the share of women with a birth by 25 from the same CPS builder, put the fertility block on one time basis, and report the time-aggregation bound so the reader knows why children ever born at 25 is not a moment for a four-year model; (2) run the single Gauss–Newton verification solve and, if it lands near 10, replace the DE lanes with three damped Gauss–Newton iterations under the primary weights; (3) tabulate the fertility–income gradient at 25 in model and data, because if the model's early births come from the rich, the paper's housing-constraint mechanism is being asked to explain births it does not produce, and that is a mechanism question to raise with the author before any further calibration. I would not adopt a heavier early-fertility weight, a profile, or a floor. I would also stop describing the fertility scales as near a bound, and I would add the wealth-profile comparison to the standard readout, because a model whose 22–33-year-olds are about 50 percent richer than in the PSID understates the constraint the paper is about.
