## Version A

Fertility is falling almost everywhere. In the United States, the period fertility rate fell from 2.12 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in 2023. The world as a whole is likely below replacement for the first time. [fn: World Bank World Development Indicators; Fernández-Villaverde and Norrick (2026).] The decline has become a major concern in the public debate. While its causes remain debated, housing affordability will shape the conditions facing future parents. Children need space. Three to four years after a first birth, parents live in 1.44 more rooms and are 27 percentage points more likely to own their home. [fn: Author’s calculations from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics.]

Family space is expensive in two distinct ways. Young households have little savings, while larger homes are concentrated in the owner-occupied market: 43 percent of owned homes have seven or more rooms, compared with 7.5 percent of rented homes. [fn: 2023 American Housing Survey.] Buying such a home requires a down payment, so credit determines whether a family can purchase it. But families must also pay for their space year after year, whether they rent or own, including when earnings fall. Easier credit reduces the first burden but not necessarily the second. Housing supply can reduce the cost of space itself. These differences also unfold over time, because policies that change births today alter the number of families demanding homes a generation later.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over what horizon.

- I moved directly from the fertility facts to the two points on the slide: children need space, and young households have little savings.
- I organized the mechanism around the distinction between financing family space and paying its recurring cost.
- I kept the equilibrium feedback to one sentence so that it motivates the horizon question without overtaking the opening.

## Version B

The US period fertility rate fell from 2.12 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in 2023. [fn: World Bank World Development Indicators.] Its causes remain debated, but housing and affordability have a role to play in the future of family formation. Children need space, and young households must find that space when their savings are limited. Housing is already their largest expense: it accounts for 33.4 percent of average household spending. [fn: Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Expenditure Surveys, 2024.] It is therefore natural that housing sits at the center of the public conversation about fertility. In 2024, JD Vance argued that young people were delaying families because they “can’t afford a place to raise a family.” [fn: Remarks by JD Vance, October 2024. A Harris campaign proposal that year similarly linked down-payment assistance to renters’ difficulty saving after paying rent and other bills.]

The cost of family space is not just the cost of buying a home. Buying requires savings and credit, but living in a larger home requires payments for as long as the family occupies it. The distinction matters because larger homes are far more common among owners than renters: 43 percent of owned homes have seven or more rooms, against 7.5 percent of rented homes. [fn: 2023 American Housing Survey.] Broad credit assistance may help young households buy sooner without making space less expensive. More housing supply lowers the cost of space more directly. Credit tied to children targets the housing cost when the need for space arises. Each policy can therefore have a different effect on the timing and number of births, and those effects can build as one generation replaces another in the housing market.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over what horizon.

- I made this version more closely follow the public-debate framing, using one short quotation and placing the second political example in a footnote.
- I replaced the down-payment-versus-recurring-cost sequence with a sharper contrast between the cost of buying a home and the cost of occupying family space.
- I introduced the three policy margins briefly so the research question arrives with concrete alternatives already in view.
