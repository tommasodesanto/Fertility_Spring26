Fertility is falling almost everywhere. In the United States, the period fertility rate fell from about 2.1 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in 2023, and the world as a whole is likely below replacement for the first time. The decline has become a central concern for policymakers. Pinpointing its causes is hard, but housing and its affordability have a role to play in what comes next, and housing is often at the center of the conversation. The logic is intuitive. Children need space, and young households, who have little savings, find that space costly to pay for.

Many of the housing policies proposed or enacted for families work through credit. The reason is that family space usually has to be bought. In the United States, 43% of owned homes have seven or more rooms, against 7.5% of rented homes. A couple that wants a large home must usually buy one, and buying requires a down payment. But a down payment is only part of what a child costs in housing. A family also pays for its extra space every year its children live at home, whether it rents or owns, and it keeps paying in years when earnings fall. If the down payment is what holds young couples back, easier credit could raise births. If the recurring cost of space is what holds them back, credit may mainly change who owns a home and when. The answer also changes over time, because births today become households a generation later: as births decline, fewer families compete for homes, and a policy that raises fertility raises the price of space for the next generation of parents.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over what horizon.
