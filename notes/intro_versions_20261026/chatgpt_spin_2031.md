### Version A

Fertility is falling almost everywhere. In the United States, the period fertility rate fell from 2.12 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in 2023, and the world as a whole is likely below replacement fertility for the first time.[fn: World Bank WDI; Fernandez-Villaverde and Norrick (2026).] This decline has become a policy concern. Its causes are debated, but housing affordability will help shape the future of family formation. Children need space, and young households have little savings with which to pay for it. Housing is the largest category of average household spending in the United States.[fn: Housing accounts for 33.4 percent of average US household spending in 2024; BLS.]

Family space is costly both to obtain and to keep. Large homes are much more common among owners: 43 percent of owned homes have seven or more rooms, compared with 7.5 percent of rented homes.[fn: 2023 American Housing Survey.] Parents expand their housing after having children. Three to four years after a first birth, they live in 1.44 more rooms and are 27 percentage points more likely to own. Yet the mean age at first birth is 27.5, while the median first-time home buyer is 40.[fn: NCHS, 2023; NAR, 2025.] A down payment may therefore constrain a couple before its first child. But the cost of an additional room also recurs throughout childhood, including when earnings fall.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over what horizon. I develop a quantitative life-cycle model with an equilibrium housing market in which households choose children, tenure, housing space, and saving. Easier credit for all buyers mostly shifts births earlier. More housing supply and credit directed to parents raise fertility, with effects that build over a generation. Policy raises births when it lowers the cost of a child rather than the cost of a home.

- Starts from the decline, then moves directly to the two elements of the slide’s logic: space and low savings.
- Separates the upfront down-payment channel from the recurring cost of family space.
- Keeps the paper’s causal boundary clear: housing shapes future family formation, not the source of the decline.

### Version B

Fertility has become a central issue in public debate. In the United States, the period fertility rate fell from 2.12 children per woman in 2007 to 1.62 in 2023.[fn: World Bank WDI.] Housing costs are often part of that debate. Young people may delay family formation because they cannot afford “a place to raise a family.”[fn: JD Vance, October 2024.] The causes of declining fertility remain debated, but the affordability of family space will matter for the families formed in the future.

The connection begins with two simple facts. Children require space, and housing is expensive. Housing accounts for one-third of average household spending in the United States.[fn: Housing accounts for 33.4 percent of average US household spending in 2024; BLS.] Parents move into larger homes after a first birth, gaining 1.44 rooms on average within three to four years. They also become much more likely to own. But young households often lack the savings needed for a down payment. The relevant policy question is therefore not only whether credit makes homeownership earlier. It is whether a policy reduces the total cost of making room for a child, including the cost paid year after year.

This paper asks which housing policies raise births, by how much, and over what horizon. I study this question in a quantitative life-cycle model with an equilibrium housing market. Broad credit expansion does little for completed fertility, while housing supply and credit for parents raise fertility over time. The difference is simple: policy raises births when it lowers the cost of a child rather than the cost of a home.

- Leads more directly with the public conversation, while keeping the claim modest and evidence-based.
- Uses the first-birth housing transition to make “children need space” concrete.
- Gives the policy distinction in plain language before introducing the model.
